Chapter 6.1 — Asset Classes

🧩 Owner or lender?

An asset class is a group of investments that behave similarly. Classes matter because they respond differently to the same conditions — and combining imperfectly correlated classes is what makes diversification work. Two roles anchor everything: owning and lending.

📈 Stock = you're an OWNER

A residual claim — paid after creditors and bondholders (which is why it's riskier and pays more). You get dividends + capital appreciation, voting rights, and limited liability.

🧾 Bond = you're a LENDER

A loan to an issuer — you're a creditor, paid before shareholders. You get coupon interest + par back at maturity. Safer than the same company's stock.

🧮 Taxable-equivalent yield

TEY = tax-free yield ÷ (1 − marginal rate). A 3.5% muni in the 32% bracket = 3.5 ÷ 0.68 = 5.15%. In the 12% bracket it's worth only ~3.98% — so munis suit high brackets and never a tax-deferred account.

🏅 The investment-grade line

Investment grade = AAA to BBB− (S&P/Fitch) / Aaa to Baa3 (Moody's). Below that = speculative / high-yield ("junk"). The BBB−/Baa3 boundary is directly testable.

⚠️
A call provision lets the issuer redeem early — they do it when rates fall, so you lose an above-market coupon and must reinvest at lower rates (reinvestment risk). Callable bonds pay more to compensate; use yield to call when a call is likely. Don't measure stock return by price change alone — total return = (end − begin + income) ÷ begin.
Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?
The coupon is fixed. When prevailing rates rise, an existing bond paying below the new market rate is less attractive, so its price must fall until its yield to maturity matches new issues.
A stock bought at $80 pays $3 in dividends and ends the year at $85. Total return?
($85 − $80 + $3) ÷ $80 = $8 ÷ $80 = 10.0%. Price appreciation alone would show only 6.25% — dividends matter.
Which rating boundary separates investment grade from speculative?
BBB− (S&P/Fitch) or Baa3 (Moody's). At or above is investment grade; below is speculative or high-yield.

Chapter 6.2 — Types & Measurements of Risk

🎭 The two families of risk

In investing, "risk" means variability of returns. The organizing split is between risk diversification can remove and risk it cannot.

🌎 Systematic — market-wide

Hits EVERYTHING: recessions, rates, inflation, currency. Diversification cannot remove it — so it's the risk the market compensates. Measured by beta.

🏢 Unsystematic — company-specific

A CEO scandal, a factory fire, one bad product. Diversification CAN nearly eliminate it — so it's not compensated. The core argument against concentrated positions (including employer stock).

Sort each risk into its family:

🧠
Systematic mnemonic — PRIME: Purchasing power (inflation), Reinvestment, Interest rate, Market, Exchange rate. These five can't be diversified away. Correlation runs +1 (move together) to −1 (move opposite); the lower the correlation, the greater the diversification benefit — the closest thing to a free lunch in finance.
⚠️
Exam trap: "Which risk can diversification eliminate?" → unsystematic. Beware apparent diversification — twenty holdings all in one sector are still concentrated. Diversification needs low correlation, not merely many positions.

📏 Quick measures to recognize

Match each measure to what it captures — the beta-vs-standard-deviation distinction is directly testable:

📐 Standard deviation (total risk)

A fund averaging 9% with a 15% SD: about 68% of years fall in −6% to +24% (±1 SD), and 95% in −21% to +39% (±2 SD). Right measure for a whole portfolio.

📊 Beta (systematic risk)

1.0 = moves with the market; 1.3 ≈ 13% move per 10% market move; 0.7 ≈ 7%; negative moves opposite. Right measure for a component inside a diversified portfolio.

Risk-adjusted return puts investments on equal footing. Here's the book's Sharpe example (risk-free 3%): the higher raw return doesn't win.

🧠
Sharpe = (return − risk-free) ÷ SD (per unit of total risk). Treynor = same, ÷ beta (per unit of systematic risk). Alpha = return above what the risk level predicts. And plan to the lower of risk tolerance (emotional) and risk capacity (financial) — the sustainable portfolio beats the theoretically optimal one the client won't hold.
Why is unsystematic risk not compensated by the market?
It can be eliminated through diversification at essentially no cost. Investors aren't rewarded for bearing a risk they could have removed for free, so expected return compensates only systematic risk.
When is standard deviation the right measure, and when is beta?
Standard deviation measures total risk — right for a whole portfolio. Beta measures systematic risk only — right for a security held within an already diversified portfolio, where unsystematic risk is already gone.
Fund X: 14% return, 18% SD. Fund Y: 10% return, 9% SD. Risk-free 3%. Which is better risk-adjusted?
X Sharpe = (14−3)/18 = 0.61; Y Sharpe = (10−3)/9 = 0.78. Fund Y delivers more excess return per unit of risk despite the lower raw return.
Why can't diversification protect against inflation risk?
Inflation (purchasing power) risk is systematic — it hits the whole market, not individual securities. Adding holdings doesn't help; only asset classes with different inflation sensitivity (real assets, TIPS) address it.

Chapter 6.3 — Mutual Funds & ETFs

🧺 Funds: baskets of investments

A fund pools money and buys a portfolio — a client with $5,000 gets diversification across hundreds of securities. The counterweight is cost: expenses are certain, outperformance isn't. Sort each trait into open-end, closed-end, or ETF:

NAV = (total assets − total liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding, computed once daily after close. Mutual fund orders execute at the next computed NAV — forward pricing — not a price you know when ordering. ETFs and stocks price continuously instead.

Class A costs more up front (front-end load) and less annually; Class C costs less up front and more forever — so over a long horizon Class C's drag beats a front load. Claim available breakpoints on Class A. ETFs/index funds are more tax-efficient — prefer them in taxable accounts (an asset-location decision). And reinvested distributions are taxable in the year distributed and raise basis — track them or you'll be taxed twice.
Why are ETFs generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds?
Mutual funds may sell securities to meet redemptions, realizing gains distributed to all remaining shareholders. ETFs use in-kind creation and redemption, transferring securities rather than selling them, so few gains are realized.
What is NAV and when does a mutual fund order execute?
NAV = (total assets − total liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding, computed once daily after close. Orders execute at the next computed NAV — forward pricing — so the exact price isn't known when the order is placed.
When does Class A generally beat Class C?
Over long holding periods. Class A charges a front-end load once plus lower ongoing fees; Class C charges little up front but higher 12b-1 fees every year, so its recurring drag eventually exceeds the load.
Why is buying a fund just before a distribution unwise in a taxable account?
The investor receives a distribution that's economically a return of part of the purchase price — the share price drops by that amount — yet the distribution is taxable. They pay tax with no economic gain.

Chapter 6.4 — Investment Decisions

🧭 Asset allocation & the horizon rule

Asset allocation — how a portfolio is divided among classes — explains the large majority of return variability over time. Security selection and market timing account for far less, so allocation deserves the most care. And the single biggest input is time horizon: a longer horizon allows recovery, so it supports more volatility. Match each horizon to its emphasis:

🧠
Two symmetric, costly errors: a near-term goal held in equities (a drawdown arrives with no time to recover) and a long-term goal held in cash (inflation erodes it while the return differential compounds against the client). Strategic allocation sets long-term targets and holds them; tactical makes short-term bets and is where costs and taxes pile up.

Over time, appreciation drifts a 60/40 portfolio to something like 72/28 — riskier than intended, with no decision made. Rebalancing restores targets (calendar or ±5-point threshold triggers) — but it's behaviorally hard because it means selling winners. In taxable accounts, rebalance with new contributions first to avoid realizing gains.

🥄 Strategies: value, growth & taming timing

📆 Dollar-cost averaging

Fixed amount, fixed schedule → automatically more shares when cheap. Its value is behavioral; a lump sum has better expected outcomes, but DCA is the plan clients actually follow.

🪨 Buy-and-hold

Hold through cycles: low costs, deferred gains, immune to the biggest behavioral errors. Missing a few of the best market days badly hurts returns. Not the same as neglect — still rebalance.

⚖️ Rebalancing

Periodically sell winners / buy laggards back to target. Forces "buy low, sell high" and controls risk drift. A written rule is what makes it actually happen.

📋 The IPS & putting it together

Vehicle selection comes near the end, not the start — clients and salespeople both tend to start with products; the process starts with goals. Put the build sequence in order:

📄 The Investment Policy Statement

A written document covering objectives & required return, risk parameters, target allocation & ranges, constraints, selection criteria, rebalancing policy, monitoring, and responsibilities. Written while calm, it's the reference point when markets fall: "this decline is within the range we planned — rebalance, don't liquidate."

🧭 SWOT for a client's position

Strengths (high savings rate, long horizon, match) · Weaknesses (concentrated employer stock, high-cost funds, thin reserve) · Opportunities (unused Roth/HSA, tax-loss harvesting) · Threats (volatility, inflation, job loss, tax changes). Its value is forcing attention on the external half.

⚠️
Required-return check: if a client's target allocation implies 9% but the plan only needs 5%, question the extra risk — a client who has already won doesn't need to keep playing. Pitfalls: selecting investments before the allocation · letting drift silently raise risk · rebalancing in taxable accounts without regard to gains · ignoring existing concentrations (employer stock).
Why does asset allocation receive more attention than security selection?
Allocation across asset classes explains the large majority of variability in portfolio returns over time, while security selection and market timing explain much less. Effort is best spent where it has the greatest effect.
A 60/40 portfolio drifts to 72/28 after a strong equity run. What's the problem?
It's now meaningfully riskier than intended, and the client got there without deciding to. Rebalancing back to 60/40 restores the planned risk level — which requires selling recent winners, the behaviorally hard part.
What is the purpose of an investment policy statement?
To document objectives, risk parameters, target allocation, constraints, and rebalancing policy in advance — providing an agreed reference during volatility, so decisions follow the policy set while calm rather than impulse under stress.
A client's required return is 5% and their allocation targets 9%. What should the planner raise?
Whether the additional risk is necessary. If a more conservative allocation funds the goals reliably, extra volatility adds risk of failure without added benefit. The client may still choose growth, but as a deliberate choice.
