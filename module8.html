Chapter 8.1

📊 Tax structures: progressive, flat, regressive

Three ways a tax can relate to income. Sort each example into its structure:

🧠
The standard example of regressivity: the Social Security payroll tax applies only up to an annually indexed wage base. A high earner stops paying partway through the year; a lower earner pays on every dollar. The stated rate is flat — the effective burden is regressive. (The Medicare portion has no cap.)
Why is the Social Security payroll tax described as regressive?
It applies only up to an annually indexed wage base, so a high earner pays on a smaller fraction of total income than a lower earner who pays on every dollar. The effective burden falls more heavily on lower incomes despite a flat stated rate.

🪜 Marginal vs. effective: the staircase

🧮 Worked example — $60,000 taxable income

10% × $12,000          = $1,200.00
12% × ($47,000−$12,000) = $4,200.00
22% × ($60,000−$47,000) = $2,860.00
Total tax = $8,260.00

Marginal rate: 22% (the next dollar). Effective rate: $8,260 ÷ $60,000 = 13.77%. Use marginal for decisions about additional income or deductions; use effective to describe the overall burden.

Slide an income and watch each bracket fill — the effective rate always trails the marginal rate:

⚠️
Exam trap: "A raise pushed me into the 24% bracket, so ALL my income is taxed at 24%" — FALSE. Only the dollars inside the 24% band are. This misconception makes people refuse raises and extra work.
Your income fills buckets from the bottom up. Only the money that reaches a higher bucket pays that higher rate — a raise never re-taxes the money below it.

🗂️ The three categories of income

The code sorts income into three buckets, and it matters because losses generally stay in their lane. Sort each item:

⚠️
The passive-loss trap: a client with a $40,000 passive loss from a rental and $150,000 of wages generally cannot use that loss against the wages. Passive losses only offset passive income; the excess is suspended and carried forward. Material participation is the pivot separating active from passive.
🧠
Portfolio nuance: not all dividends are equal — qualified dividends and long-term gains get preferential rates; ordinary (nonqualified) dividends and short-term gains are taxed at ordinary rates. The short-vs-long line is simply more than one year.
Why can't a passive loss offset wage income?
The passive activity loss rules confine passive losses to offsetting passive income. Excess losses are suspended and carried forward, becoming usable against future passive income or generally on a fully taxable disposition of the activity.

🎚️ Character, timing & location — the three levers

Essentially all tax planning is one of three levers. Recognizing which lever a strategy pulls beats memorizing strategies one by one — tap each:

🧠
Filing basics: individuals use the calendar year and the cash method (income when received, expenses when paid). Tax is pay-as-you-go via withholding or quarterly estimates. The assessment statute is generally 3 years, 6 for a substantial omission of income, and unlimited for fraud or failure to file.

👥 Filing status & dependents

Status is set by your situation on the last day of the tax year and governs bracket widths, the standard deduction, and many phaseouts. Match each status to its requirement:

🏆 MFJ & HOH win

MFJ is generally most favorable. HOH is materially better than single — wider brackets, bigger standard deduction — but the "qualifying person" test is specific and misapplying it is a frequent error.

⚠️ MFS usually loses

It restricts or kills several credits and deductions. Justified only occasionally — e.g. one spouse with large medical expenses against a smaller AGI, or to avoid joint-and-several liability.

🧠
Dependents come in two flavors — qualifying child (relationship, age, residency, support tests) and qualifying relative (gross-income and support tests). Dependency drives the child tax credit, education credits, the dependent-care credit, and HOH status.

🧮 AGI, adjustments & deductions

AGI cascades through the whole return — it gatekeeps IRA deductibility, Roth eligibility, education and dependent-care credits, the medical floor, NIIT, Social Security taxation, and IRMAA. An above-the-line adjustment can be worth more than face value by restoring a phased-out benefit. First, sort what's in gross income vs. excluded:

⚠️
The standard-deduction reality: since it was raised, most taxpayers no longer itemize — which means mortgage interest and charitable gifts produce no tax benefit for them. Advice premised on their deductibility is simply wrong for most households. Schedule A still matters for medical (above an AGI floor), SALT (capped), mortgage interest, investment interest, and charitable gifts.
What's the advantage of an above-the-line adjustment over an itemized deduction?
It's available whether or not the taxpayer itemizes, and it reduces AGI — which cascades into eligibility for other deductions and credits keyed to AGI. An itemized deduction only helps if the taxpayer itemizes and doesn't reduce AGI.

💎 Credits vs. deductions — which is worth more?

A deduction reduces taxable income (worth deduction × your rate). A credit reduces the tax itself, dollar for dollar. Sort each item into what it actually is:

✂️ $1,000 deduction (22% bracket)

Saves $1,000 × 22% = $220. Value depends on your bracket.

💎 $1,000 credit

Saves the full $1,000 — regardless of bracket. Worth ~4.5× the deduction here.

🧠
Refundable vs nonrefundable: a nonrefundable credit can cut tax to zero but no further; a refundable one (EITC, part of the child tax credit) can pay you beyond your liability — which is why filing can be worthwhile even when not required.
Why is a $1,000 credit worth more than a $1,000 deduction?
A credit reduces tax dollar-for-dollar, so it's worth $1,000. A deduction reduces taxable income, so it's worth the deduction times the marginal rate — $220 at 22%. The credit's value is also independent of bracket, making it proportionally more valuable to lower-bracket taxpayers.

🧾 Payments, withholding & safe harbor

The last stretch of the return — additional taxes (self-employment, NIIT, additional Medicare, the 10% early penalty), then payments. Tap each fact that trips people up:

🧠
Forms feed the flow: W-2 (wages), the 1099 series (interest, dividends, proceeds, nonemployee pay), K-1 (pass-throughs), 1098 (mortgage/tuition). Schedules: A itemized · B interest/dividends · C sole prop · D capital gains · E rental/pass-through · SE self-employment.
Why can increasing year-end withholding cure an underpayment penalty?
Withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year regardless of when it occurred, so late-year withholding is credited to earlier quarters. A Q4 estimated payment gets no such treatment and won't cure earlier-quarter shortfalls.

Chapter 8.3

🧱 Basis: what you recover tax-free

Basis is your investment in the property for tax purposes — the amount you get back untaxed on sale. It starts at cost and changes over the holding period. Sort each event by its effect:

⚠️
Two persistent errors: (1) Reinvested dividends in a taxable fund were already taxed, so they increase basis — miss them and you pay tax twice. (2) Basis is reduced by depreciation allowable whether or not you actually claimed it — neglecting to depreciate loses the deduction and reduces basis anyway.

🎁 Basis by how you acquired it — step-up & gifts

How property came to you sets its basis. Match each route to its rule:

⚠️
The step-up is one of the most consequential rules in the code. An asset bought for $50,000 and worth $400,000 at death passes to heirs with a $400,000 basis — the lifetime appreciation escapes income tax entirely. Planning rule of thumb: gift cash or high-basis assets; bequeath low-basis appreciated ones. The dual basis rule for gifts (carryover for gain, lesser-of for loss) is why a donor should sell a depreciated asset and gift the proceeds.
A client gifts stock with a $20,000 basis now worth $12,000. The recipient sells for $10,000. What's the loss?
For loss purposes, basis is the lesser of the donor's basis ($20,000) or FMV at the gift date ($12,000) — so $12,000. The loss is $2,000. The $8,000 of decline during the donor's ownership is lost to both parties, which is why the donor should have sold and gifted the proceeds.

⏳ Holding period, netting & wash sales

🏃 Short-term (held ≤ 1 year)

Taxed at your ordinary income rates — up to 37%. The period starts the day AFTER acquisition and includes the sale date.

🧘 Long-term (held > 1 year)

Preferential 0/15/20% rates. Inherited property is always long-term; gifted property tacks the donor's holding period.

When you have gains and losses, netting follows a defined sequence — put it in order:

🧮 Worked example — stock held 3 years

Basis $28,150 ($28,000 + $150 commission). Amount realized $46,800 ($47,000 − $200). Long-term gain = $18,650. Same year, a $6,000 short-term loss → net long-term gain of $12,650 at preferential rates. Lesson: given a choice, offset short-term gains (ordinary rates) with losses, not long-term.

⚠️
Wash-sale rule: a loss is disallowed if you buy substantially identical securities within 30 days before or after the sale (a 61-day window). Normally the loss is added to the replacement's basis — but a repurchase inside your IRA makes the loss permanently lost. Personal-use losses (a home, a car sold at a loss) aren't deductible at all — though gains are taxable.
What is the wash sale rule and why does an IRA repurchase make it worse?
A loss is disallowed if substantially identical securities are bought within 30 days before or after the sale. Normally the disallowed loss is added to the replacement shares' basis, deferring it. If the repurchase occurs in an IRA, there's no basis benefit to preserve, so the loss is permanently lost.

💰 Annuities & collectibles

Two asset types with their own rules — and both surprise investors. Tap each:

A client invested $60,000 in an annuity now paying $800/month for a 25-year life expectancy. Compute the exclusion ratio and the taxable portion.
Expected return = $800 × 12 × 25 = $240,000. Exclusion ratio = $60,000 ÷ $240,000 = 25%. Tax-free per payment = $200; taxable = $600. Once the full $60,000 basis is recovered, payments become fully taxable.

Chapter 8.4

🎁 Fringe benefits: excluded, taxable, or deferred

Every benefit is compensation — the only question is whether it's currently taxable, excluded, or deferred. Many are excluded, which makes them worth more than equivalent salary. Sort each:

⚠️
The two employers get wrong: personal use of a company car and cash / gift cards are taxable compensation — the de minimis exclusion never covers cash equivalents, no matter how small. And group term life above $50,000 creates imputed income via the IRS uniform premium table (Table I) by age.
Why is employer-provided health coverage worth more than equivalent salary?
It's excluded from income entirely with no dollar cap, so the full value is received tax-free. Equivalent salary would be reduced by both income tax and payroll tax before the employee could purchase the same coverage.

🍔 Cafeteria plans & FSAs

A §125 cafeteria plan lets you elect qualified benefits with pre-tax salary reduction — cutting income tax and FICA. Tap each rule:

🧠
Health FSA vs dependent-care FSA: the health FSA makes your full annual election available on day one; the dependent-care FSA only reimburses what you've contributed so far. And you can't use a dependent-care FSA and the dependent-care credit for the same expenses — the FSA usually wins at higher incomes, the credit at lower ones.

🌊 Social Security taxation: provisional income

Up to 85% of benefits can be taxed, based on provisional income = AGI + tax-exempt interest + 50% of benefits against thresholds that aren't inflation-indexed. Sort what counts toward it:

Why does municipal bond interest fail to protect Social Security benefits from taxation?
Tax-exempt interest is explicitly added back in computing provisional income. So while it escapes federal income tax directly, it still increases provisional income and can push more of the Social Security benefit into taxation.

Chapter 8.5

🗄️ Records & retention periods

The dullest topic that most reliably costs money: in a tax dispute the burden of proof is on the taxpayer. A legitimate deduction without substantiation is, practically, not a deduction. Match each record to how long to keep it:

⚠️
The statute of limitations, and why some records are forever: generally 3 years from filing to assess, 6 for a substantial omission of income, and unlimited for fraud or a failure to file. Without basis records, the IRS may treat basis as zero and tax the entire proceeds. Rule of thumb: returns forever, supporting records until the statute expires, basis records until the asset is gone.
Why should tax returns be kept permanently?
They document carryforward items — capital loss carryovers, suspended passive losses, charitable carryovers, nondeductible IRA basis — that may not be used for years. They also establish filing history, which matters because the limitations period never begins on an unfiled return.

🧾 Substantiation & basis records

The requirements that quietly disqualify real deductions — tap each:

🧠
Basis records are the highest-value records a client keeps — and the most often lost. Brokers report basis for covered securities (acquired after certain dates); for older non-covered holdings, the taxpayer is on the hook. Download brokerage history before closing an account — access usually ends when the relationship does.
What does "contemporaneous" mean for charitable acknowledgments?
Obtained by the earlier of the return's filing date or its due date. An acknowledgment secured later — including after an audit begins — doesn't satisfy the requirement, and the deduction is lost even for a genuine gift.

Chapter 8.6

⚖️ Avoidance vs. evasion & after-tax return

Tax avoidance — minimizing tax within the law — is legitimate and is what planning is. Tax evasion — concealing income or fabricating deductions — is criminal. The line is substantive. Sort each action:

🧮 After-tax return decides it — 32% bracket

Corporate 6% × (1 − 0.32) = 4.08%
Municipal (federally exempt) = 4.30% ✅
TEY = 4.3% ÷ (1 − 0.32) = 6.32%

The muni wins despite a lower headline yield. But for a 12%-bracket investor, TEY = 4.3% ÷ 0.88 = 4.89% — now the 6% corporate wins. The right answer depends entirely on the bracket, so compute it.

⚠️
Second framing point, often violated: taxes are a consideration, not the objective. The classic mistake is refusing to sell a dangerously concentrated position solely to avoid the gain. A move that cuts tax but worsens the overall outcome is a bad recommendation.
A 5% corporate bond vs a 3.6% municipal, investor in the 24% bracket. Which wins?
Corporate after tax = 5% × 0.76 = 3.80%. The municipal yields 3.60% tax-free. The corporate wins by 0.20 points. Equivalently, the muni's TEY is 3.6 ÷ 0.76 = 4.74%, below the corporate's 5%.

🔧 Harvesting & the three levers in action

Every strategy below is character, timing, or location at work. Tap each:

🧠
Tax-gain harvesting is the less-familiar mirror of loss harvesting: in a year when long-term gains fall in the 0% bracket, deliberately realize them at no tax cost and reset basis higher. The wash-sale rule doesn't apply to gains, so you can repurchase immediately. Prime candidates: early retirees before Social Security & RMDs.

👨‍👩‍👧 Shifting income to lower-bracket taxpayers

Moving income to a lower bracket works — but each technique has a real limit. Match each to its catch:

⚠️
UGMA/UTMA's two big disclosures: (1) the kiddie tax taxes a child's unearned income above a modest threshold at the parents' rate (under 18, or full-time students under 24) — gutting the shift. (2) The child gains unrestricted control at the age of majority and the gift is irrevocable. Meanwhile, hiring your child for real work creates earned income that enables a Roth IRA — genuinely powerful with decades to compound.
Why does the kiddie tax undercut UGMA/UTMA income shifting?
A child's unearned income above a modest threshold is taxed at the parents' marginal rate — applying to children under 18 and full-time students under 24 who don't provide more than half their own support. Only a small amount escapes, so the shifting benefit is largely eliminated.

📍 Asset location

The location lever — placing each asset in the account type that minimizes total tax — is one of the most reliable sources of value a planner adds, because it needs no forecasting. Sort each asset to where it belongs:

⚠️
Two specifics: municipal bonds in a tax-deferred account waste their exemption entirely, and appreciated assets meant for heirs should stay in a taxable account to capture the step-up — the same assets in a traditional IRA get no step-up and emerge as ordinary income to beneficiaries. Roth accounts ideally hold the highest-growth assets, since all growth escapes tax permanently.
Why keep appreciated assets intended for heirs in a taxable account?
Taxable-account assets receive a step-up in basis at death, eliminating the built-in gain permanently. The same assets in a traditional IRA get no step-up and are distributed to beneficiaries as ordinary income.
