Chapter 10.1 — Getting to Know the Whitfields

👪 Meet the Whitfields

👨‍💻 Marcus Whitfield, 44

Senior software engineer, $128,000 salary. 401(k) with a 50% match on the first 6% of pay. Ten stable years with the firm. Risk tolerance: moderately aggressive.

👩‍⚕️ Dana Whitfield, 42

Nurse practitioner at a nonprofit hospital, $96,000 salary. 403(b) with a 3% nonelective employer contribution. Risk tolerance: moderate to conservative — moved a balance to stable value after a past downturn.

💍 Married 15 years 💰 Combined income: $224,000 👧 Nia, 12 — 6 yrs to college 👦 Theo, 9 — 9 yrs to college 🧓 Dana's mother, 71, may need support within a decade 🏠 Possible ~$240,000 inheritance — excluded from the plan on purpose

Data-gathering pitfalls: accepting stated goals without probing their order · recording only numbers and missing context like Dana's mother or her loss aversion · treating an expected inheritance as a planning asset · missing titling/beneficiary details · omitting savings from cash flow (overstates true surplus) · not noticing the standard deduction before giving deduction-dependent advice.
Why exclude Dana's expected inheritance from the plan?
Timing is unknown, the amount is uncertain, and assets may be consumed by her mother's long-term care costs. Planning around it would overstate resources; if it arrives, it's a surplus — the safer direction to be wrong.
Net worth is $869,000. Why doesn't that represent goal-funding capacity?
Use assets — residence, vehicles, personal property — total $603,000, about 49% of total assets, and won't be liquidated for goals. Invested assets of $588,000 are the relevant figure, and $41,000 of that is already earmarked for education.
What's the significance of the risk-tolerance gap between spouses?
Household capacity is high but Dana's tolerance is lower. Planning purely to capacity risks her abandoning the strategy during a decline. Separate account allocations plus a written IPS agreed while calm reconciles this.
Why does the standard deduction matter for the advice given here?
Their itemized deductions fall below the standard deduction, so mortgage interest and charitable gifts produce no current tax benefit. Bunching gifts via a donor-advised fund becomes the relevant strategy instead.
Why question the Whitfields' ranking of college above retirement?
Retirement can't be financed by borrowing; education can, through loans, scholarships, work, and lower-cost schools. Prioritizing college at retirement's expense can leave parents dependent on their own children later — the ordering deserves to be examined with them, not just accepted.

Analysis pitfalls: presenting point estimates instead of ranges · accepting the client's goal priority without testing it · analyzing only what the client asked about · assuming market value equals replacement cost for homeowners · forgetting to tax-adjust group disability benefits · mistaking healthy ratios for a complete plan.
Why does the retirement surplus change how this whole case is approached?
It removes the constraint the Whitfields assumed was binding. Retirement is over-funded by ~$843,000, so resources can shift toward education without endangering it — and the required return is below 7%, permitting a more conservative allocation for Dana.
Why is the $6,000 credit card balance a behavioral finding, not a capacity one?
With a $3,607 monthly surplus, they could retire it in under two months. Paying 19.9% while holding surplus cash isn't an affordability problem — it points to mental accounting or inattention, so the fix is a conversation plus automation, not a budget.
Why does homeowners Coverage A need verification rather than assumption?
Market value includes land, which can't be destroyed, so it may overstate or understate replacement cost. If replacement cost exceeds $600,000, the $480,000 limit breaches the 80% coinsurance requirement and partial losses get proportionally reduced.
Why is the estate finding critical despite zero estate-tax exposure?
The issue isn't tax — with no will, a court selects the guardian for two minor children, and with no powers of attorney, incapacity would require conservatorship. Among the highest-consequence, lowest-cost gaps in personal financial planning.
Why is 31% in employer stock worse than 31% in an unrelated stock?
It compounds an existing concentration — Marcus's salary, job security, and retirement plan all depend on the same company, so one adverse event threatens income and assets together. The risk is also unsystematic and therefore uncompensated.

🗣️ Presenting, implementing & monitoring

🌟 Lead with the good news

Retirement is on track with a big surplus — true, and it's what they were most anxious about. It earns credibility to raise harder items.

🔀 Explain the reordering

Protection isn't competing with education — every goal depends on both parents being alive, insured, and having named who'd raise the kids.

🤝 Make the education trade-off theirs

Present the funding levels and costs; let THEM choose. A single recommended figure here substitutes the planner's values for the client's.

💛 Address Dana directly

Her risk tolerance is a documented input, not an obstacle. Showing the plan doesn't need aggressive returns should ease her anxiety more than reassurance would.

Monitoring: annual review of net worth, cash flow, education/retirement progress, allocation drift, and insurance adequacy. Off-cycle triggers: a job change, either parent's health change, Dana's mother needing support, an inheritance arriving, a big market move, a state move, tax law changes, or Nia starting to drive. ⚠️
Recommendation & implementation pitfalls: ordering by client priority instead of consequence · presenting analysis instead of specific actions · making a values decision (like the education funding level) FOR the client · not documenting alternatives considered · leaving actions unassigned or undated · allocating every dollar of surplus (no slack) · cutting retirement contributions below the full match · selling employer stock before checking NUA · treating implementation as the finish line instead of monitoring.
Why do estate documents outrank education funding despite the Whitfields' stated priorities?
Consequence and cost-effectiveness. With no wills, a court would choose the guardian for two minor children — an outcome touching every other goal, fixable for a few thousand dollars. Education is important but is a gradual shortfall, not a catastrophic exposure.
Why is term rather than permanent insurance recommended here?
The need is temporary — it declines as the mortgage amortizes, education gets funded, and the kids become independent. Term affordably provides $850,000/$600,000; the same amounts as permanent coverage would be unaffordable and crowd out education funding.
Why pay the credit card from the reserve rather than the monthly surplus?
The reserve sits above guideline at 6.87 months, so $6,000 can come out while staying near 6.0 months. This kills the 19.9% cost immediately and preserves the monthly surplus for ongoing commitments like premiums and 529 contributions.
Why leave $1,474 of surplus unallocated?
A plan with no slack breaks on the first unplanned expense and then tends to get abandoned entirely. The remainder funds the family trip, a sinking fund, and genuine flexibility.
What must be checked before selling Marcus's employer stock?
Whether the position carries substantial net unrealized appreciation (NUA). NUA treatment can allow appreciation to receive long-term capital gain rather than ordinary income treatment at separation, and it can be forfeited by a later rollover — confirm cost basis with the plan administrator first.
Why plan now for cash flow that frees up in nine years?
When the education commitment ends, $2,300/month becomes available. Deciding in advance where it goes prevents it from being silently absorbed into lifestyle spending — the default outcome when a scheduled obligation ends without a plan for the freed cash flow.
