Chapter 5.1 — Life Insurance

⚖️ Life insurance: rent it or own it

Life insurance transfers the financial consequence of a premature death. Two decisions structure everything: how much coverage, and what type. Type comes down to how long the need lasts and whether the client has a use for the savings element permanent policies carry.

⏱️ TERM — rent protection

Pure death benefit for a set period (10/20/30 yrs). Cheapest per dollar of coverage. No cash value — outlive it, nothing back. Most policies are convertible to permanent without new evidence of insurability. Perfect for temporary needs (young family, mortgage!).

🏠 PERMANENT — own it

Whole life: fixed premium + guaranteed cash value. Universal: flexible premiums/death benefit. Variable: cash value in investment subaccounts (market risk!). VUL: both. Lasts for life — costs far more.

⚠️
Exam trap: young family + max coverage + low budget → term. "Flexible premiums" → universal. "Investment subaccounts / separate accounts" → variable (needs a securities license + prospectus). The frequent real-world error is buying permanent on a budget and ending up badly under-insured — adequate amount matters more than policy type.

📉 See the cost over time

Drag through the years: term stays cheap but stops; whole costs far more yet is permanent and builds cash value. The whole-life premium is level because early-year overpayments fund later years and build the cash value.

Term is like renting protection — cheap while you need it most, then it ends. Whole life is like buying — much pricier, but it lasts your whole life and slowly builds a savings value inside.

🧮 How much? Two roads to the number

💼 Human life value

Present value of the person's future earnings, net of taxes and self-consumption. Quick, but ignores existing resources and specific obligations — so it can over- or under-state badly.

📋 Needs analysis (preferred)

Add up actual needs — final expenses, debts, income replacement, education, emergency reserve — then subtract existing resources. Need = Total needs − Available resources.

Here's the book's worked example: a married client, 40, with two kids. Watch how resources shrink a $1.37M need down to the actual coverage gap:

Needs = final expenses $20k + mortgage $285k + other debt $32k + income replacement $759,558 (PV of $60k/yr, 18 yrs at 4%) + education $240k + reserve $30k. Resources = group life $190k + individual $250k + investments $145k. Gap ≈ $781,558, rounded up to $800,000 of level term matching the dependency period. The need is clearly temporary → term is indicated.

🧠
The analysis drives the type. A temporary need (income replacement, mortgage, kids' dependency) → term. A genuinely permanent need (special-needs dependent, estate liquidity, funded buy-sell) → permanent. Round up, since estimates usually omit inflation.

📜 Provisions, dividends & taxation

The exam loves the standard policy provisions. Tap each card to reveal what it does:

💵 Death benefit

Generally received income-tax-free by the beneficiary.

📈 Cash value

Grows tax-deferred inside the policy. Surrender is taxable to the extent proceeds exceed basis (premiums paid).

🏛️ Estate inclusion

Proceeds are in the gross estate if the insured held incidents of ownership at death. Fix: own it in an ILIT.

⚠️
Pitfalls: choosing type before amount · relying solely on group coverage (it ends at termination; employer-paid coverage over $50k creates taxable imputed income) · stale beneficiary designations (ex-spouse still named) · naming a minor directly (forces guardianship) · surrendering a policy with an outstanding loan, triggering unexpected taxable gain.
Why does the duration of the need drive the term-vs-permanent decision?
Term is cheapest per dollar but expires; permanent costs more but lasts for life. A temporary need is efficiently covered by term; a genuinely permanent need (special-needs dependent, estate liquidity) requires coverage that won't expire.
What's the main risk of universal life's premium flexibility?
Underfunding. If cash value can't cover the ongoing cost of insurance and expenses, the policy lapses — often decades later when the insured is old and replacing coverage is costly or impossible. Periodic in-force illustration reviews catch it.
When are death benefits included in the gross estate, and what's the standard fix?
When the insured possessed incidents of ownership at death (right to change beneficiary, borrow, or surrender). The standard technique is ownership by an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT), so the insured holds no incidents of ownership.
Why is convertibility on a term policy valuable?
It allows conversion to permanent coverage without new evidence of insurability — so if the insured's health deteriorates during the term, they keep access to permanent coverage they could no longer qualify for on the open market.

🐷 The HSA: the triple-tax champion

The HSA is the most tax-favored account in the entire code — the only one with a triple tax advantage:

The HSA and the FSA look similar and are constantly confused. Sort each trait into the right account:

WithdrawalTax treatment
Qualified medical, any ageTax-free ✅
Non-qualified, before 65Ordinary income + 20% penalty

🧠
Memory hook: after 65 the HSA behaves like a traditional IRA for non-medical withdrawals and stays fully tax-free for medical ones — so it doubles as a stealth retirement account. Contribution limits are set by statute and indexed annually (year-sensitive — always confirm the current figure), with catch-up contributions starting at age 55. Enrolling in Medicare ends HSA contribution eligibility.
What is the HSA's triple tax advantage?
Contributions are deductible or pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. No other account offers all three.
A plan has a $2,500 deductible, 20% coinsurance, and a $6,000 OOP max. The insured incurs $80,000 in covered charges. What do they pay?
$6,000. Pay the $2,500 deductible; 20% of the remaining $77,500 would be $15,500, but the OOP max caps total cost-sharing at $6,000, so coinsurance stops at $3,500. Premiums are additional and don't count toward the max.
Key differences between an FSA and an HSA?
The HSA requires HDHP enrollment, is individually owned and portable, and rolls over without limit. The FSA needs no HDHP but is employer-tied, generally forfeited at termination, and subject to use-it-or-lose-it (softened only by a limited grace period or small carryover).
Why might COBRA not be the best option after job loss?
The individual pays the full premium plus an administrative charge with no employer subsidy — often far more than the prior payroll deduction. Marketplace (ACA) coverage with premium tax credits may cost substantially less, though COBRA preserves network and deductible progress.

Chapter 5.3 — Disability Income Insurance

🤕 Disability insurance: protect the paycheck

For most working clients the largest asset isn't the house or the portfolio — it's the ability to earn income, and it's usually uninsured. Disability is more probable than death during working years. The single most important provision is the definition of disability. Tap each card:

⏳ Elimination, benefit period & the tax twist

⏳ Elimination period

Waiting period before benefits begin (30/60/90/180/365 days) — a deductible in time. Size it to the client's emergency reserve: more reserve → longer wait → cheaper policy.

📅 Benefit period

How long benefits last (2 yrs, 5 yrs, to 65/67). Since the point is protecting retirement funding, to retirement age is generally recommended.

💲 Benefit amount

Capped at ~60–70% of gross on purpose — full replacement would remove the incentive to return to work (moral hazard).

🧾 Worked example — the tax adjustment reveals the gap

Client earns $8,000/mo gross ($5,900 net); non-discretionary outflows $4,600/mo. Group LTD pays 60% of base, employer-paid, 90-day elimination, to 65. Four-month reserve.

Group benefit: 60% × $8,000 = $4,800 gross Employer-paid → taxable. At 22%: × 0.78 = $3,744 net Required: $4,600 Gap: $856/mo

The 60% benefit looked adequate and wasn't — the tax adjustment is what exposed the gap. Fix: an individual policy ~$900–$1,000/mo paid with after-tax dollars (so benefits are tax-free), 90-day elimination to match the group coverage and the reserve, with a residual benefit and a future-increase option.

⚠️
Pitfalls & distinctions: assuming group LTD is enough without the tax adjustment · treating SSDI as real coverage (its strict any-occupation standard and 5-month wait make it a backstop, not a plan) · omitting residual coverage when most disabilities are partial. Non-cancelable locks the premium AND coverage; guaranteed renewable locks only coverage (premiums can rise by class).
Why is own occupation more favorable than any occupation?
Own-occ pays when the insured can't perform their own occupation's material duties, even if capable of other work. Any-occ pays only if they can't perform any suited job. A surgeon who can't operate but could teach collects under own-occ and likely not under any-occ.
How should the elimination period be chosen?
By matching it to the emergency reserve. Six months of expenses saved → a 180-day elimination period and the premium savings. No reserve → a shorter one, since the client can't self-fund the gap.
What does a residual disability benefit do, and why does it matter?
It pays a proportional benefit when the insured can work but at reduced capacity or income. It matters because most disabilities are partial, not total — without it, a client working at reduced capacity may collect nothing.

Chapter 5.4 — Long-Term Care Insurance

👵 Long-term care & Medicare

Long-term care is extended help with everyday living — bathing, dressing, eating, moving — rather than medical treatment. That distinction is the whole subject, because it's exactly the line health insurance and Medicare draw. They cover skilled medical care; long-term care is largely custodial, and custodial care is where the gap sits.

🏛️ Medicare — very limited

Skilled nursing only after a qualifying hospital stay, only while skilled care is needed, for a limited number of days with heavy coinsurance later. Does NOT cover custodial care — the majority of the need.

🩹 Medicaid — payer of last resort

Does cover custodial care, but it's a means-tested welfare program — you spend down first. 5-year look-back on transfers, spousal-impoverishment rules, and estate recovery after death.

Timing sweet spot: mid-50s to early 60s. Tax-qualified premiums are deductible medical expenses (age-based limits); benefits are generally tax-free.
Why doesn't Medicare solve the long-term care problem?
Medicare covers skilled nursing only briefly and only after a qualifying hospital stay, with heavy coinsurance after an initial period and coverage ending well before a long stay concludes. It doesn't cover custodial care, which is most long-term care need.
State the two standard benefit triggers.
Inability to perform at least 2 of the 6 ADLs (bathing, dressing, eating, transferring, toileting, continence) for an expected 90+ days; or severe cognitive impairment requiring substantial supervision.
Which asset group most needs LTC insurance, and why?
Middle-asset clients. Low-asset clients can't afford premiums and rely on Medicaid; high-asset clients can self-fund. Middle-asset clients have enough to lose entirely but not enough to absorb several years of care — the greatest risk of ruin.

Chapter 5.5 — Annuities

🔄 The mirror image of life insurance

An annuity is a contract with an insurer: pay a sum, receive income for life. It's the mirror image of life insurance and transfers longevity risk — the one retirement risk that can't be diversified away. Only pooling mortality across many people solves it, and that's what an insurer provides.

❤️ Life insurance

Protects against dying too soon. Pays at death, income-tax-free to the beneficiary.

🔄 Annuity

Protects against living too long. Pays until death. Gain is taxable as ordinary income; no step-up in basis.

💸 Payouts, taxation & when annuities fit

Every guarantee added to a payout reduces the payment, because the insurer's obligation lasts longer. Put the payout options in order from the highest payment to the lowest:

🧾 Taxation of a non-qualified deferred annuity

Funded with after-tax dollars → growth is tax-deferred. Withdrawals before annuitization are LIFO — earnings out first, taxed as ordinary income (not capital gains), plus a 10% penalty before 59½. On annuitization each payment splits via the exclusion ratio.

➗ Exclusion ratio

Investment in the contract ÷ Expected total return. Sets the tax-free return-of-basis portion of each payment. Once basis is recovered, payments are fully taxable.

🧠
When annuities fit: a retiree wanting guaranteed income for essentials · someone specifically worried about outliving assets · a client who has already maxed 401(k)/IRA capacity and wants more deferral. When they don't: inside an IRA without a specific reason (redundant deferral, added cost) · when 401(k)/IRA space is unused · when liquidity may be needed within the surrender period.
What risk does an annuity transfer, and why can't investing solve it?
Longevity risk — outliving your assets. No investment strategy eliminates the possibility of a very long life; only pooling mortality across many people does, which is what an insurer provides.
How are withdrawals from a non-qualified deferred annuity taxed before annuitization?
Last-in, first-out — earnings come out first and are taxed as ordinary income, not capital gains. A 10% penalty applies to the taxable portion before age 59½, subject to exceptions.
Why is an annuity inside an IRA usually redundant?
The IRA already provides tax deferral, so the annuity's primary tax advantage adds nothing while its costs — M&E charges, rider fees, surrender schedules — still apply. Justifiable only if the client specifically wants the income guarantees for their own sake.
Why can an indexed annuity underperform the index it tracks?
Crediting is limited by participation rates, caps, or spreads, and typically excludes dividends — a substantial part of long-run equity return. The floor protecting against loss is paid for by giving up part of the upside.
