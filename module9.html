Chapter 9.1 — Estate Planning Terminology

🗂️ The estate planning toolbox

How property is classified decides how it transfers — and this single distinction drives the whole module.

⚖️ Probate property

Solely owned, with no beneficiary designation or survivorship feature. Transfers by the will — or by intestacy law if there's no will.

🚀 Non-probate property

Has a survivorship feature, a beneficiary designation, or is held in trust. Transfers by operation of law or contract — the will never touches it.

Real property is governed by the law of the state where it sits (which is why out-of-state property can trigger ancillary probate). Personal property follows the law of the decedent's home state.

Every estate plan involves a cast of characters. Match each role to its job:

The executor runs the show after you die; the trustee runs a trust while it exists; a guardian raises your kids if you can't. Different jobs, often different people.

⚖️ Probate: what it does, and its drawbacks

Probate proves the will's validity, appoints the executor, gives creditors and will-contesters a forum, and produces clear title for distributed assets. Those are genuine benefits — but they come at a price. Tap each drawback:

Many states offer simplified or small-estate procedures for modest estates that cut the cost and delay substantially. And a will that omits a residuary clause (a catch-all for anything not otherwise given) risks partial intestacy — one of the most common drafting failures.

Why can a carefully drafted will still fail to control an estate?
Will substitutes — beneficiary designations, joint tenancy with survivorship, POD/TOD registrations, and trusts — pass outside the will and override it. Much of a typical estate transfers this way, so a stale designation defeats even perfect drafting.
State the four main drawbacks of probate.
Cost (court fees, executor commissions, attorney fees), delay (months to years), publicity (records are public), and ancillary probate in every state where real property sits. Court supervision also reduces flexibility.
Explain the step-up and its gifting implication.
Property from a decedent takes a basis equal to date-of-death fair market value, so lifetime appreciation escapes income tax. That's why you bequeath low-basis appreciated assets and gift cash or high-basis assets instead — gifting appreciated property wastes the step-up.
What is portability, and how is it lost?
It lets a surviving spouse use the deceased spouse's unused federal exclusion. It requires a timely-filed estate tax return to elect — commonly skipped in estates owing no tax, which can prove very costly at the survivor's death.
Why is divorce a particularly dangerous point for beneficiary designations?
Many state statutes revoke will provisions for a former spouse, creating a false sense that everything updated automatically. Those statutes generally don't reach retirement-account beneficiary forms, governed by federal law — so an ex can remain entitled to the account.
What's distinctive about community property at the first spouse's death?
The ENTIRE community interest — both spouses' halves — generally gets a step-up in basis, not just the decedent's share. In common law states, only the decedent's half steps up.

🎭 Trusts: revocable vs. irrevocable

🔄 Revocable (living) trust

You keep control, can change or cancel it. Avoids probate — but assets still count in your taxable estate and get no creditor shield.

🔒 Irrevocable trust

You give up control — permanently. In exchange: assets leave your taxable estate and gain creditor protection. Classic use: irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT).

🎁 Gifts: exclusions, UGMA/UTMA & gift vs. bequest

Lifetime gifts remove assets AND their future appreciation from the estate. Sort each item into the smarter move:

🎓 Tuition & medical, paid DIRECTLY = unlimited, no forms 👨‍👩‍👧 UGMA/UTMA = irrevocable gift; property is the minor's 🔓 Custodial control passes at majority 👶 Kiddie tax limits income shifting ⚠️ Donor-as-custodian who dies while serving → included in donor's estate
🧠
The rule of thumb: gift cash and high-basis assets; bequeath low-basis appreciated assets. Gifting carries over the donor's basis and wastes a step-up that would have erased the gain.
⚠️
Chapter 9.2 pitfalls: relying on a will while titling/designations actually control · adding a child as joint owner "for convenience" · creating a revocable trust and never funding it · assuming a pour-over will substitutes for funding · transferring an existing policy to an ILIT and dying within 3 years · leaving assets outright to a disabled beneficiary · naming a minor or the estate as beneficiary · omitting contingent beneficiaries · serving as custodian of a UTMA you funded · reimbursing tuition instead of paying the school directly · gifting appreciated assets that would have stepped up.
Why is an unfunded revocable living trust worthless?
A trust governs only assets actually titled in its name. Without retitling, assets stay in the grantor's name and go through probate anyway — exactly what the trust was meant to avoid. A pour-over will catches them, but they still go through probate first.
What's wrong with adding an adult child as joint owner "for convenience"?
It's a completed gift with survivorship — the account passes to that child alone, disinheriting siblings as to that asset. It also exposes the funds to the child's creditors and divorce, may trigger gift tax, and forfeits part of the step-up. A durable POA achieves the same convenience without these effects.
How does an ILIT keep insurance out of the gross estate, and what's the 3-year rule?
Proceeds are included only when the insured holds incidents of ownership. If the ILIT owns the policy from inception, the insured holds none. The 3-year rule pulls proceeds back into the estate if an EXISTING policy is transferred to the trust and the insured dies within 3 years — so the trust should buy the policy originally.
Why is leaving assets outright to a disabled beneficiary a problem?
It can disqualify them from means-tested benefits like Medicaid and SSI. A properly drafted special needs trust supplements those benefits without replacing them — an informal sibling promise offers no legal protection.
What's distinctive about the tuition and medical gift exclusions?
They're unlimited in amount and don't use the annual exclusion or lifetime exemption — but payment must go DIRECTLY to the school or medical provider. Reimbursing the individual instead makes it an ordinary gift.
State the general rule for gifting vs. bequeathing an asset.
Gift cash and high-basis assets; bequeath low-basis appreciated assets. Gifting carries over the donor's basis, so the recipient inherits the built-in gain, while bequeathing delivers a step-up that erases it.

♾️ Two spouse superpowers

💍 Unlimited marital deduction

Leave ANY amount to a U.S.-citizen spouse — zero estate tax at the first death. (It defers tax; the assets land in the survivor's estate later.)

📨 Portability

A surviving spouse can claim the deceased spouse's unused exemption (file the election!) — doubling the shield.

Credit shelter (bypass) trusts are the traditional alternative to portability — funding a trust with the first spouse's exclusion so assets AND their future growth bypass the survivor's estate. Trade-off: bypass-trust assets don't get a second step-up at the survivor's death. Put the taxable-estate formula in order:

🏛️ The gross estate & valuation

The gross estate is broader than probate property — the unifying rule is: retained control means inclusion. Sort each item:

Property is valued at fair market value on the date of death, or the executor may elect the alternate valuation date (6 months later) — but only if it reduces BOTH the gross estate and the tax, and only for the entire estate at once. Valuation discounts for lack of marketability/control can apply to closely held business interests, but they're an audit magnet.

💧 Administrative costs, IRD & the liquidity problem

For most families, administrative costs and liquidity matter far more than estate tax — current exclusions put most estates beyond its reach entirely. Income in respect of a decedent (IRD) — like a traditional IRA balance — gets NO step-up, and the recipient owes income tax on it, which is why an IRA should go to charity (which owes no income tax) and appreciated stock to family (which gets the step-up). Chapter 9.3 pitfalls: focusing on estate tax when admin costs/liquidity are the real issue · missing the portability election because no tax was owed · assuming a revocable trust cuts estate tax · life insurance IS included when the insured holds incidents of ownership · reimbursing tuition/medical instead of paying directly · skipping Form 709 when required · leaving IRD to individuals and appreciated assets to charity (backwards!) · ignoring low state exclusions · no liquidity plan for an illiquid estate · electing alternate valuation without confirming it helps both figures.
What does it mean that gift and estate taxes are "unified"?
A single lifetime exemption covers both, so lifetime gifts and death transfers draw on the same pool — preventing avoidance by simply giving everything away before death.
Why should an IRA go to charity and appreciated stock to family?
The IRA is income in respect of a decedent — no step-up, and heirs owe ordinary income tax. A charity pays no income tax, so that liability disappears. Appreciated securities get a step-up at death, so family receives them free of the built-in gain. Reversing the two wastes both advantages.
What is portability, and why is it commonly lost?
It lets a surviving spouse use the deceased spouse's unused exclusion. It requires a timely-filed federal estate tax return to elect — often skipped when the first estate owes no tax, forfeiting exclusion that could matter greatly later.
Why does a revocable trust provide no estate tax benefit?
The grantor retains the power to amend or revoke, so the assets remain under their control and stay in the gross estate. Retained control means inclusion — the general rule governing the gross estate.
What is the liquidity problem, and how is it usually solved?
Estate debts, expenses, and taxes come due within months while assets may be illiquid — a business, farm, or real estate — forcing a distress sale. Life insurance owned by an ILIT is the standard fix: immediate, income-tax-free liquidity that doesn't enlarge the taxable estate.
When may the alternate valuation date be elected?
Six months after death, and only if it reduces BOTH the gross estate and the estate tax. It applies to the entire estate, not selected assets.

Chapter 9.4 — Incapacity Planning

🤝 Durable power of attorney & avoiding conservatorship

Every tool in this chapter needs legal capacity to execute — once capacity is genuinely in question, it's too late, and a court-appointed conservator (costly, public, slow, chosen by the court, not the family) is the only option left.

⚡ Immediate (standing) durable POA

Effective on signing and survives incapacity. Many practitioners favor this — with a highly trusted agent — to avoid trigger friction.

🌱 Springing durable POA

Effective only when a trigger (usually certified incapacity) occurs. Feels safer, but physician certification takes time — and institutions may dispute it.

⚠️
Word matters: "durable" means the power SURVIVES incapacity. An ordinary (non-durable) POA terminates at incapacity — precisely when it's needed. Always name successor agents, and remember a POA ends at death (the executor takes over) — agents sometimes wrongly keep acting.

Chapter 9.4 pitfalls: waiting until capacity is already questionable · a non-durable POA (terminates exactly when needed) · a springing POA without appreciating the certification friction · missing specific powers (gifting, beneficiary changes) that later prove necessary · no successor agents named · an unfunded "incapacity" trust · no HIPAA authorization · never discussing wishes with the health care agent · documents nobody can find · uncompensated transfers inside the Medicaid look-back · attempting Medicaid planning without elder law counsel · overlooking ABLE eligibility · assuming an agent's authority survives the principal's death.
Why must incapacity documents be executed well before they're needed?
Executing them requires legal capacity. Once capacity is genuinely in question, the client can no longer sign — leaving court-appointed conservatorship as the only option: costly, public, delayed, and court-chosen rather than family-chosen.
What does "durable" mean, and why is it essential?
A durable power of attorney survives the principal's incapacity. An ordinary power terminates upon incapacity — exactly the moment it's needed — so without durability the document fails at its whole purpose.
How do a living will and a health care proxy differ?
A living will states wishes about life-sustaining treatment; a health care proxy appoints an agent to make medical decisions. The living will guides, the proxy decides — applying values to situations no document anticipated. Both are needed.
Why are a funded revocable trust and a durable POA complements, not alternatives?
The successor trustee manages only assets titled in the trust. The durable POA covers everything outside it — including the act of transferring assets into the trust in the first place. Neither alone covers the full picture.
What makes an ABLE account valuable for a disabled beneficiary?
Balances up to a threshold are disregarded for SSI, and Medicaid eligibility is generally unaffected — so the beneficiary can hold meaningful savings in their own name without losing means-tested benefits. Growth and qualified withdrawals are also tax-free.
Why is gifting shortly before a Medicaid application worse than not gifting?
Uncompensated transfers inside the ~5-year look-back create a penalty period that begins when the applicant would otherwise have qualified — leaving them without the transferred assets AND without coverage, right when care is needed.
