Chapter 7.1

🌅 What are we actually saving for?

Most clients can't answer this concretely — and that's the first planning problem. "Retirement" covers arrangements that differ enormously in cost: a full stop at 62, phased part-time work into the seventies, a second career, relocation abroad. Force specificity before you touch a calculator.

🎯 The replacement ratio

A rough starting heuristic: retirees need about 70–85% of pre-retirement income. Payroll taxes, contributions, commuting, and often a mortgage stop — but health care and early-retirement travel rise.

⚠️ Its limitation

It's derived from income, not spending. A client with big travel plans, a remaining mortgage, or high health costs can need 100%+. Build from projected expenses instead.

❓ Questions that force specificity

At what age — preference or constraint? Full stop or reduced work? Live where? Do what all day? Who else depends on you?

Why do people delay saving despite knowing better? Tap each obstacle — every one is a behavioral trap, not a math failure:

Saving for retirement feels hard because the reward is far away and invisible. The fix isn't more willpower — it's starting early and automating it so you never have to decide again.
What's the limitation of the 70–85% replacement ratio?
It's a rough rule derived from income rather than actual spending. Clients with substantial travel, a remaining mortgage, or high health costs may need 100% or more — an estimate built from projected expenses is far more reliable.

📏 Plan to age 90–95+

Life expectancy is an average — half exceed it by definition. Planning to the average is a coin flip on outliving the plan. For a couple, use joint life expectancy: the plan must support the survivor.

🔗 Longevity amplifies everything

A longer retirement means more years of inflation, more market cycles, and a higher chance of long-term care. It's the one risk you can't diversify away — the argument for guaranteed lifetime income.

⚠️
Demographic shift to internalize: the move from defined benefit → defined contribution pushed investment risk, longevity risk, and the savings decision itself off the employer and onto the employee — someone with no training in managing it. That single change is why this whole module exists.
Why is planning to average life expectancy inadequate?
By definition roughly half of people exceed the average, so it amounts to a coin flip on outliving the plan. Standard practice is to plan to age 90–95 or beyond, using joint life expectancy for couples since the plan must support the surviving spouse.

🧠
Roth's bonus perks beyond the rate comparison: no lifetime RMDs, tax-free growth over long horizons, great treatment for heirs, and tax diversification that lets you manage bracket exposure in retirement. Roth contributions (not earnings) can also come out anytime tax- and penalty-free.
⚠️
Backdoor-Roth pro-rata trap: a qualified Roth distribution needs BOTH the 5-year rule AND a qualifying event (59½, death, disability, first home). And if you hold any pretax IRA balance, conversions are taxed proportionally across all traditional IRA assets — you can't convert only the after-tax basis.
Roth = pay the tax now, never again. Traditional = skip the tax now, pay it when you take the money out. Pick Roth if you think your tax rate will be higher later.

🎯 Defined BENEFIT

Promises a formula-based benefit. The employer bears investment & longevity risk; often PBGC-insured. Increasingly rare. (Cash balance plans are DB plans dressed up as an account balance.)

📦 Defined CONTRIBUTION

Specifies the contribution, not the benefit. The employee bears investment risk. 401(k) is dominant — capturing the full employer match is close to the highest-priority move in personal finance (free, guaranteed return).

🚪 The 10% early-withdrawal penalty & its exceptions

Distributions before age 59½ generally face a 10% penalty plus ordinary income tax. But the exceptions split in a testable way — some are IRA-only, some employer-plan-only. Sort each one:

⚠️
The classic trap: the age-55 separation-from-service exception is employer-plan-only. A client who rolls a 401(k) into an IRA after leaving at 56 forfeits it — now they'd owe the 10% before 59½. Meanwhile education and first-time home purchase are IRA-only. Governmental 457(b) plans skip the 10% penalty entirely on separation, regardless of age.
Which penalty exceptions are IRA-only, and which are employer-plan-only?
IRA-only: qualified higher-education expenses, first-time home purchase, and health insurance premiums while unemployed. Employer-plan-only: separation from service at 55+, and QDROs.

Miss an RMD? Penalty is now 25% of the shortfall (down from 50%) — cut to 10% if corrected quickly. Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs; since 2024, Roth 401(k)s don't either.

🪜 Vesting & choosing among accounts

Vesting decides when employer money becomes non-forfeitable. Your own deferrals are always immediately 100% vested — it's your money. Employer contributions may use a cliff (0% then 100% at once) or graded (steps over years) schedule — worth checking before a job change, since leaving just before a cliff can forfeit real money.

How should you frame the claiming decision — and why isn't it a breakeven calculation?
Delaying purchases longevity insurance: a larger inflation-adjusted benefit for life protects against outliving assets — the scenario that most threatens a retiree. The right question isn't which choice wins on average, but which leaves the client better off in the bad case.

🧮 How the benefit is built (credits, AIME, PIA)

Eligibility is earned through credits — up to 4/year, and 40 credits (~10 years) confer fully-insured status. The benefit itself is three steps: index lifetime earnings → average your highest 35 years into AIME → run a progressive bend-point formula to get your PIA (benefit at FRA). Match the alphabet soup:

🧠
Two planning consequences: the formula is progressive, so lower earners get a higher percentage replaced. And it uses 35 years — work fewer and zeros get averaged in, so extra working years replacing a zero can raise the benefit meaningfully.

💑 Spousal, survivor & the earnings test

These four rules quietly decide huge sums — and clients get every one of them wrong. Tap to reveal each:

⚠️
The two-lifetime insight: the survivor benefit is up to 100% of what the deceased was receiving — including their delayed credits. So the higher earner delaying raises their own benefit AND the survivor benefit their spouse may collect for years. Standard advice: higher earner delays; lower earner may claim earlier if income is needed.
🧠
Provisional income link: up to 85% of benefits can be taxable, based on provisional income (≈ AGI + tax-exempt interest + ½ of benefits) against thresholds that aren't inflation-indexed. IRA withdrawals push more benefit into tax; Roth distributions don't count — the strongest case for tax diversification.
What happens to benefits withheld under the earnings test?
They aren't lost. The benefit is recomputed at FRA to credit the withheld amounts, so it's a deferral, not a forfeiture. The earnings test also stops applying entirely once FRA is reached.

🏥 Medicare, part by part

Federal health insurance starting at 65. Clients dramatically overestimate what it covers — tap each part to see what it does and doesn't do:

⚠️
Enrollment is where the money is lost. The Initial Enrollment Period is 7 months (3 before your 65th-birthday month, that month, 3 after). Miss it without qualifying coverage → lifetime late-enrollment penalties on Parts B & D. This is among the most avoidable and most permanent errors in retirement planning.

⚠️
IRMAA's 2-year lookback raises Part B & D premiums using MAGI from generally two years prior — and it's a cliff. A big Roth conversion or capital gain at 63 raises your Medicare premiums at 65; one dollar over a threshold bumps you a whole tier.
🧠
HSA interaction: enrolling in Medicare (including automatic Part A triggered by claiming Social Security) ends HSA contribution eligibility. Working past 65 and want to keep funding an HSA? You must delay Medicare — only possible with active employer coverage.
Why doesn't COBRA permit penalty-free deferral of Part B?
The Special Enrollment Period requires coverage through active employment. COBRA and retiree coverage aren't active employer coverage, so a client relying on them past 65 who defers Part B incurs a lifetime late-enrollment penalty.

Chapter 7.4

📉 Sequence-of-returns risk

Accumulation and distribution are different problems. During accumulation a decline is survivable — contributions keep buying cheap shares. During distribution you're selling into declines to eat, which locks in losses and permanently shrinks the base. Same average return, very different outcome depending on the order returns arrive:

Five ways to defuse it — tap each mitigation strategy:

What is sequence-of-returns risk, and why is it specific to the distribution phase?
Poor returns early in retirement do disproportionate damage because withdrawals are taken from a declining base, permanently reducing the assets available to recover. During accumulation the same decline is survivable, since contributions continue and buy more shares at lower prices.

💸 The rollover trap, plan loans & lump-sum vs. annuity

⚠️
Never take the check. A plan distribution paid directly to the participant triggers mandatory 20% withholding, and to avoid tax you must redeposit the full amount — including the withheld 20% from other funds — into an IRA within 60 days. A direct trustee-to-trustee transfer avoids all of it. (Watch NUA on employer stock too — rolling it into an IRA can destroy long-term-gain treatment.)
🧠
401(k) loans look free, aren't. Borrowed funds are out of the market; you repay with after-tax dollars that get taxed again at withdrawal; and separation from employment accelerates repayment — unpaid by the filing deadline, it becomes a taxable distribution (+10% if under 59½). Job loss and hardship arrive together — exactly when repaying is hardest.

🚰 Withdrawal sequencing & the 4% guideline

Which accounts you draw first has real tax consequences. The common default order preserves tax-free growth for last — put the three buckets in sequence:

🧠
Better than mechanical: bracket management. In low-income years — especially the window between retirement and the start of Social Security & RMDs — deliberately realize income up to the top of a low bracket (traditional withdrawals or Roth conversions). You fill brackets that would otherwise go unused and shrink later RMDs that could push you into higher brackets and IRMAA tiers.
⚠️
The 4% "rule" is a reference, not a law. It assumes a specific allocation and horizon, uses historical U.S. returns, ignores fees and taxes, and rigidly mandates inflation-adjusted withdrawals no sensible retiree would actually follow. Longer retirements or higher fees argue lower; flexibility to cut spending in bad years supports higher.
What is the low-income window and why does it matter?
The years between retirement and the start of Social Security and RMDs, when taxable income is often unusually low. Deliberately realizing income fills low brackets that would otherwise go unused and reduces later RMDs that could push the client into higher brackets and IRMAA tiers.

🛡️ Avoiding fraud & other retiree issues

Retirees are disproportionately targeted — accumulated assets, possible isolation, and gradual cognitive decline. The near-universal marker is urgency. Sort each signal:

🛡️ Protect proactively

Verify advisers on BrokerCheck / SEC IAPD, name a trusted contact, consolidate accounts, and set up a durable power of attorney while capacity is clear.

🏥 Health care before 65

Early retirees need a bridge — COBRA, marketplace, or a spouse's plan. A frequently underestimated cost that can decide whether early retirement is even feasible.

🏠 Housing & relocation

Downsizing releases equity (§121 may shelter the gain). State income, property, sales, and estate/inheritance taxes vary a lot — relocation math depends on the client's income mix.

Why is urgency a reliable fraud marker?
Legitimate investments don't require immediate decisions; pressure exists specifically to prevent verification and consultation. Combined with a promise of high returns at low risk — a relationship that doesn't exist — it's close to definitive.
