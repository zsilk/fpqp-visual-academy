Chapter 2.1 · Financial Statements

📸 Two statements, two questions

Two statements describe a client's condition, and they answer different questions. A client can look wealthy on one and be in trouble on the other — so you need both.

📷 Statement of financial position

A snapshot — what the client owns and owes at a single moment. Always dated.

Assets − Liabilities = Net Worth

🎬 Cash flow statement

A video — what came in and went out over a period (a month or a year).

Inflows − Outflows = Net Cash Flow

🧠
Valuation rules: assets are carried at fair market value (what they'd sell for today, not what was paid), and liabilities at outstanding principal balance today — not the sum of remaining payments (which would include unaccrued interest).

🗂️ Sort the balance sheet

Assets split into three layers. The invested vs. use distinction matters: use assets (the couch, the car) generally won't be sold to fund retirement, so net worth including them overstates the resources actually available. Sort each item:

Liabilities, meanwhile, split into current (due within a year — card balances, unpaid taxes) and long-term (mortgages, student loans). And don't drop the footnotes — titling, interest rates, and maturities live there, and they're exactly what estate and risk planning need later.

💵 Cash flow: inflows & outflows

Inflows: salary/wages, self-employment income, bonuses, investment and rental income, pensions, Social Security, alimony. Outflows come in three flavors — and taxes and savings both belong here. Sort each outflow:

⚠️
Classic omission: leaving savings out of outflows overstates the true surplus and hides that the client has no savings plan at all. Only variable and discretionary outflows respond quickly to a budget; fixed outflows need a structural change (refinance, move, sell a vehicle).

🧮 Worked example — building the statements

Balance sheet

Cash $18,000 · Invested $145,000 · Use assets $310,000
Current liabilities $9,000 · Long-term $242,000

Assets $473,000 − Liabilities $251,000 = Net worth $222,000

Cash flow (monthly)

Inflows $9,500 · Outflows $9,150

Net cash flow = +$350/month surplus

Reading: solvent with a positive but thin surplus — $350/month is all that's available to fund every recommendation. And use assets are 65% of total assets, so net worth overstates goal-funding capacity. The number that matters for future goals is the $145,000 invested base.

Why is a liability recorded at outstanding principal rather than the sum of remaining payments?
The remaining payments include interest that hasn't accrued yet. The obligation today is the principal balance — what it would take to extinguish the debt now.
A client has net worth of $600,000 and a monthly deficit of $700. Which finding drives the plan?
The deficit. Net worth is a snapshot; a persistent deficit means it's shrinking — funded by drawing down assets or adding debt. Correcting it precedes almost every other recommendation.
Why separate invested assets from use assets?
Use assets are consumed rather than held for return and generally won't be liquidated to fund goals. Including them in net worth overstates the resources actually available for retirement, education, and the like.
⚠️
Common pitfalls: recording assets at purchase price · listing the sum of remaining payments as the liability · omitting savings from outflows · reading net worth as available resources · undated balance sheets · dropping footnotes.

Chapter 2.2 · Financial Situation Analysis

📐 The money ratios (memorize these!)

Statements produce raw numbers; ratios turn them into judgments. The three lending ratios everyone quotes:

≤28%
Front-end / housing — PITI (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) ÷ gross monthly income
≤36%
Back-end / total debt — all debt service ÷ gross monthly income
≤20%
Consumer debt — non-mortgage payments ÷ NET (take-home) income
🧠
Memory hook — "28 / 36 / 20": house first (28 of gross), everything (36 of gross), consumer junk (20 of NET). Note the sneaky switch — the 20% ratio uses net income!

⚠️
Treat guidelines as starting points, not pass/fail thresholds. The liquidity target flexes up for single-income, commissioned, or self-employed clients, and down for dual stable incomes with disability coverage in place. Same 3.5-month reserve, different verdict depending on income stability.

⚖️ Gross vs. net & the worked ratios

Which income base a ratio takes is not arbitrary — reporting a ratio on the wrong base produces a number that looks plausible and is wrong. Sort each ratio by the income it uses:

Lenders standardize on gross income, so the mortgage ratios use gross. The consumer-debt ratio asks what share of the money you actually take home is spoken for — so it uses net.

Why does the liquidity ratio exclude retirement accounts?
They aren't truly liquid — accessing them before 59½ generally triggers income tax plus a 10% penalty and undermines a separate goal. The ratio measures resources available for an emergency without damaging the plan.
Which ratios take gross income, and why?
Front-end, back-end, and savings ratios. The first two are lender underwriting conventions standardized on gross. The consumer-debt ratio is the exception, using net because it asks what share of spendable income is committed.
A client shows a back-end ratio of 41%. What follows?
It exceeds the 36% guideline, so debt service is consuming too much income. The client will struggle to qualify for new credit and has little room for savings. Focus on retiring debt before adding obligations.
⚠️
Common pitfalls: counting retirement accounts as liquid · using total instead of non-discretionary outflows in the liquidity ratio · applying gross income to the consumer-debt ratio · omitting taxes/insurance from housing costs · excluding employer match from the savings ratio.

Chapter 2.3 · Credit & Debt

⚖️ Good debt vs. risky debt — and the 5 Cs

Debt is a tool — neither good nor bad in itself. The useful line is between borrowing that acquires an appreciating or income-producing asset and borrowing that funds consumption.

🌱 Debt that can build wealth

A reasonable mortgage · student loans that raise income · a business loan with a plan. Buys assets that can grow or earn.

🔥

Debt that burns wealth

Revolving card balances at 20%+ · payday loans · financing things that lose value fast. High rates, no asset left behind.

🧠
Compounding in reverse: a $5,000 balance at 20% APR paid at a 2% minimum takes decades to retire and costs more in interest than the original purchase. The same math that builds wealth works against a carried balance.

🆘 Bankruptcy & paydown strategy

Bankruptcy is a legal process for debts that can't be repaid — the last option, with lasting credit consequences. Refer clients to a bankruptcy attorney; this is squarely legal practice. The two chapters to know — sort each feature into the right one:

🧠
Memory hook: 7 letters in "LIQUIDS" → Chapter 7 = liquidation (up to 10 years on the report). "13" looks like a calendar page → Chapter 13 = payment plan over 3–5 years (up to 7 years).
🚫 Not dischargeable: most student loansrecent taxeschild support & alimonycourt finesdebts from fraud

🏔️ Avalanche

Highest interest rate first. Mathematically optimal — minimizes total interest.

⛄ Snowball

Smallest balance first. Suboptimal arithmetically, but early wins sustain motivation — and a plan the client finishes beats one they abandon.

Why can closing an old unused credit card lower a score?
It reduces total available credit, raising utilization (~30% of the score), and can shorten average account age (~15%). Both move the score down.
Two clients each carry $30,000 — one a 4% mortgage, one 22% cards. Same recommendation?
No. The mortgage bought an appreciating asset at a low rate and may not warrant accelerated payoff. The card balance is consumption debt above any reasonable expected return, so retiring it takes priority over most investing beyond an employer match.
What's the risk in consolidating credit card debt into a HELOC?
The rate falls, but unsecured debt becomes secured by the home — nonpayment now risks foreclosure rather than collection. It also frees card capacity, so without a behavior change the client may re-accumulate on top of the HELOC.
When does buying an auto clearly beat leasing?
When the client keeps vehicles well beyond the loan term — the savings come from the payment-free years after payoff. A client who trades every three years never reaches them and may find leasing competitive.

Chapter 2.4 · Budgeting

🧰 Building a budget & the emergency fund

A cash flow statement records what happened; a budget states what should happen — so it can be compared against actuals, which is the whole source of its value. Budgets fail for behavioral reasons far more than analytical ones, so the goal is the most sustainable budget, not the most precise. Put the four creation steps in order:

🧠
The one reframe that matters most — "pay yourself first": treat savings as a fixed obligation funded before discretionary spending, not as whatever remains at month end. Nothing ever remains at month end. Then automate the transfer on payday to neutralize status-quo and present bias.

🔁 Monitoring, variances & sinking funds

Compare actual against budgeted on a regular cycle. But diagnose before correcting — the same overage means different things. Match each variance to its response:

⚠️
The #1 cause of budget failure — irregular expenses: insurance premiums, property taxes, holidays, car maintenance, annual subscriptions. They aren't monthly, so they get omitted, then blow up the month they land. The fix is a sinking fund — divide the annual cost by twelve and set it aside monthly, so it's already funded when it arrives.

🧮 Worked example — reaching 10% savings & a 6-month reserve

Client from 2.1/2.2: $9,500 gross, $350 surplus, 8% savings, 3.5-month reserve. To hit 10% of gross needs +$190/month to savings; the reserve gap is $13,200 (6 × $5,200 − $18,000). That leaves only $160/month for the reserve — ~82 months. Too slow.

Best realistic move without lifestyle change: the auto loan's $520 payment ends in 14 months — redirect it and the reserve gap closes in about two years. Lesson: a scheduled payoff is a future cash-flow raise. Deciding where it goes before it frees up is what stops it from being absorbed into spending.

Why does "pay yourself first" outperform saving what's left over?
Spending expands to consume available income, so nothing reliably remains. Making savings a fixed obligation funded first — ideally automatically — removes the monthly decision and the biases that defeat it.
What's a sinking fund and which failure does it prevent?
Setting aside a monthly fraction of a known irregular expense so it's funded when due. It prevents the common failure where non-monthly expenses are omitted and then break the budget when they arrive.
Why does the emergency fund generally precede accelerated debt paydown?
Without a reserve, the next unexpected expense goes on credit or forces an early retirement withdrawal — reversing paydown progress and adding cost. A small starter reserve alongside attacking high-rate debt is the common compromise.
A client is over budget in every category every month. Diagnosis?
Not a single-category discipline problem — either income is genuinely insufficient or the budget was aspirational rather than built from actual spending. The response is structural: raise income, cut fixed costs, or revise goals.

🤝 Operating & succession

Running a closely held business raises recurring planning issues — tap each to reveal why it matters:

⚠️
The personal-guarantee trap: forming an LLC does not protect personal assets against a loan the owner personally guaranteed — which is most small-business bank debt. The shield still covers other liabilities (tort claims, trade creditors), but the guarantee waives it for that specific debt.
Two questions distinguish most business forms. What are they?
Whether the form provides limited liability protecting personal assets, and whether income is taxed at the entity level or passes through to the owners' returns.
What is double taxation and which form has it?
The C corporation. Corporate income is taxed at the entity level, and dividends to shareholders are taxed again on their personal returns — the same earnings taxed twice.
What's the S corporation's potential self-employment tax advantage, and its limit?
An owner-employee pays payroll tax only on reasonable salary; remaining distributions aren't subject to SE tax. The limit is the reasonableness requirement — an artificially low salary is a recognized audit target.
A client forms an LLC, then personally guarantees the bank loan. What protection remains?
The LLC still shields personal assets from other business liabilities — tort claims, trade creditors, other contracts. But the personal guarantee waives protection for that specific debt, so the lender can pursue personal assets on default.
Why is a funded buy-sell agreement important for a closely held business?
It establishes in advance who buys a departing or deceased owner's interest and at what price, and the funding — often life insurance — ensures the money exists when needed. Without one, heirs may be stuck with an illiquid interest or forced into business with the survivors.
⚠️
Common pitfalls: assuming an entity protects against personally guaranteed debt · treating an LLC as a tax classification rather than a legal form · a limited partner losing the shield by managing · running a partnership with no written agreement · commingling personal and business funds · advising on entity selection rather than coordinating with the client's attorney and CPA.
