Chapter 1.1 · Planning & Financial Goals

🧩 Planning is coordination, not products

Anyone can sell a mutual fund or a term policy. Planning is the discipline of making the fund, the policy, the mortgage, the 401(k), and the will all point the same direction — and not quietly undermine each other. A tax move that wrecks a financial-aid application, or a Roth conversion that spikes a retiree's Medicare premium, is only visible when someone holds the entire picture at once.

🧠
Quick test: if a recommendation could have been made without knowing anything else about the client, it probably wasn't planning. Real recommendations depend on bracket, horizon, dependents, risk tolerance, and existing coverage.

The field splits into a handful of overlapping domains — the overlap is the whole point. Match each planning area to the central question it answers:

🚫 Why people don't plan

Most of a planner's early work is overcoming resistance. Name these plainly and you get further than another projection does:

🌀 Perceived complexity

It feels like it needs expertise they lack.

⏳ Procrastination

The cost of delay is invisible; the effort is immediate.

💰 "Planning is for the wealthy"

Backwards — less margin means less room for error.

🙈 Avoidance

Money is tangled with fear, shame, and family history.

📄 "I already have a plan"

Owning a 401(k) gets mistaken for having a plan.

🎯 Goals give the plan its shape

Without goals there's no standard to judge a recommendation against. A workable goal specifies four things — miss one and it can't be funded or measured:

What
The outcome in concrete terms
How much
A dollar amount — today's dollars or inflated
When
A target date, which sets the time horizon
Priority
Where it ranks when resources are scarce
🧠
Old-school hook — "PTA": Purpose, Time frame, Amount. The book adds Priority as the fourth, because nearly every client has goals competing for the same dollars — and the planner's job is often helping them decide what matters most, not finding more money.

⏱️ Horizon drives strategy — sort each goal

Short-horizon money belongs somewhere stable and liquid (no time to recover a loss); long-horizon money can accept volatility for expected return. Mismatching the two is one of the costliest errors in personal finance. Drop each goal into its horizon:

📖 Definitions worth keeping straight

TermMeaning
Time horizonThe interval between now and when a goal must be funded
Risk toleranceThe volatility a client is emotionally willing to accept
Risk capacityThe loss a client can financially absorb without derailing goals
LiquidityHow fast an asset converts to cash without losing value
⚠️
Exam trap — tolerance ≠ capacity. A young high earner may have huge capacity but panic in a drawdown; a fearless retiree may be unable to afford a bad return sequence. Good planning respects the lower of the two.

🧮 Worked example — same client, opposite advice

A client, 34, wants to retire at 65 and buy a home in three years.

🏠 Home down payment — 3-year horizon

Short-duration instruments, money market, or CDs. Certainty of principal is the requirement; a 20% drawdown in year two has no time to recover.

🌴 Retirement — 31-year horizon

Equity-weighted. Volatility is tolerable, and three decades of compounding let the return differential dominate.

Same risk tolerance, opposite recommendations — because the horizons differ. That's why goals are defined before strategy is chosen.

Why is a goal without a date effectively unusable?
The date sets the time horizon, and the horizon drives asset allocation, required savings rate, and appropriate risk. Without it, there's no way to compute how much to save or what to invest in.
A client is "comfortable with aggressive investing," but a 25% loss would delay retirement a decade. What's the conflict?
Risk tolerance is high but risk capacity is low. Follow capacity — the client can't financially absorb the loss no matter how they feel about volatility.
Give an example of two planning areas producing contradictory advice in isolation.
Maximizing pretax retirement contributions is ideal tax planning, but if it leaves no emergency reserve, a job loss forces an early withdrawal with penalty and tax — worse than the deduction was worth.
⚠️
Common pitfalls: treating a product sale as a plan · setting goals with no amount or date · ignoring inflation on a long-horizon goal · letting stated tolerance override actual capacity · optimizing one area at another's expense.

Chapter 1.2 · The Seven-Step Process

🗺️ The 7-step journey

This is the profession's standard procedural framework, from the CFP Board's Practice Standards. Two things to fix in mind: the order isn't arbitrary — each step feeds the next — and it's a loop, not a line. Step 7 feeds back into Step 1, because lives change and a plan that isn't revisited goes stale. Click any step to explore it:

1

🤝 Understand the client's personal & financial circumstances

Gather facts (quantitative) and feelings (qualitative). Check the data is complete and consistent. Identify the client's other advisers — attorney, CPA, insurance agent — early.

2

🎯 Identify & select goals

Elicit, quantify, and prioritize. Surface unachievable goal combinations now, not at step 5. This is where trade-offs become explicit.

3

🔎 Analyze the current course & alternatives

The analytical core. Evaluate the current course (does the gap close if nothing changes?), then compare alternatives — including doing nothing.

4

📋 Develop the recommendations

Specific, sequenced, and traceable: this recommendation, for this goal, chosen over these alternatives, for these reasons.

5

🗣️ Present the recommendations

Communication, not computation. Invite pushback — objections often reveal information that sends you back to step 1. That's the loop working.

6

🚀 Implement

Establish what gets done, who does it, and when. Unassigned actions are where plans die.

7

🔄 Monitor progress & update

Review on a schedule; revise on triggers — marriage, birth, death, job change, inheritance, illness, business sale, a move, tax-law changes. Loops back to step 1.

🧠
Memory hook: "Umbrellas In A Downpour Prevent Immense Mess" → Understand, Identify, Analyze, Develop, Present, Implement, Monitor. Silly = sticky!

🎮 Lock in the order

The exam LOVES asking what happens at each step and in what sequence. Tap the steps in order — first step first:

Why does the process loop rather than terminate?
Circumstances, goals, markets, and tax law all change. Step 7 detects those changes and returns to step 1 with updated information, keeping the plan aligned with reality rather than the day it was written.
At which step does prioritization of goals occur, and why there?
Step 2. Prioritization must precede analysis, because when resources can't fund every goal, step 3's analysis needs to know which goals give way.
During step 5 a client reveals they intend to support an aging parent — never mentioned before. What happens?
Return to step 1. It's new material information: it changes the data set, likely changes goals in step 2, and requires re-analysis. Proceeding would build on a picture now known to be incomplete.

📊 Two kinds of client data

Step 1 gathers both halves of the picture — and the qualitative half is routinely underweighted and routinely decisive. Two clients with identical balance sheets can need entirely different plans because one has a disabled child and the other intends to sell a business in four years. Sort each item into the right kind:

🔢 Quantitative = countable

Facts and numbers: income, balances, debts, ages, coverage, tax returns.

Quantity → numbers.

💗 Qualitative = qualities

Feelings and values: risk attitude, health, family dynamics, dreams, fears.

Quality → how they feel.

Why is it useful to identify the client's other advisers in step 1?
It clarifies who owns which part of the plan, avoids contradictory advice, enables coordination on legal/tax items, and marks the boundary of what the paraplanner may appropriately handle.

Chapter 1.3 · Knowledge Required

🧠 The knowledge map: every domain feeds step 3

Analysis is only as good as the technical knowledge behind it — and that knowledge spans every remaining module. The recurring theme: a recommendation optimal within one domain is frequently suboptimal once another is considered. Tap each domain to see what it contributes:

🧠
Two anchors: Cash management (Module 2) is foundational — everything else is funded from cash flow. Time value of money (Module 3) is the engine — retirement needs, education funding, loan comparisons, and savings rates are all present-value/future-value problems in different clothing.

💥 Where domains collide

None of these have universal answers — they resolve only against a specific client's goals, bracket, horizon, and priorities, which is exactly why steps 1 and 2 come before step 3:

DecisionDomains in tension
Max pretax retirement contributionsTax (favorable) vs. cash flow & liquidity (reserve may be inadequate)
Pay off a low-rate mortgage earlyCash-flow certainty vs. investment opportunity cost vs. lost deduction
Buy permanent rather than term lifeRisk transfer & tax-deferred cash value vs. cost per dollar of coverage
Roth conversion in a low-income yearTax (long-term benefit) vs. retirement (current bracket, Medicare surcharges)
Gift appreciated assets to childrenEstate (removes future appreciation) vs. tax (no step-up in basis at death)

🚧 Scope of practice: know your lane

Knowing the boundary is itself part of the required knowledge. The professional response to an out-of-scope question is referral and coordination, not an approximate answer. Sort each task into what a paraplanner may do vs. what must go to a licensed professional:

Why is cash management treated as foundational relative to the other domains?
Every other recommendation is funded from cash flow. Without positive cash flow and an adequate reserve, premiums lapse, contributions stop, and assets get liquidated at bad times — so other recommendations aren't sustainable.
A client wants to gift appreciated stock to an adult child. Which two domains conflict?
Estate and tax. Gifting removes future appreciation from the estate, but a lifetime gift carries over the donor's basis, whereas holding until death generally gets a step-up. The child may face a large capital gain that holding would have eliminated.
A client asks you to draft a "simple" will since their situation is straightforward. Correct response?
Decline and refer to an attorney. Drafting legal documents is the unauthorized practice of law regardless of apparent simplicity. Identify the need, gather facts, and coordinate with the client's attorney.
⚠️
Common pitfalls: optimizing a single domain in isolation · comparing options pretax when the client experiences after-tax results · deferring risk management until after accumulation · treating estate planning as a late-life concern · answering a legal or tax question that should be referred.

Chapter 1.4 · Client Behaviors

🧠 Behavioral finance: why clients act against their own interests

Plans fail for human reasons far more often than technical ones. Technical analysis tells you what the client should do; behavioral understanding tells you what they will do; counseling skill narrows the gap. Biases fall into two families:

🧩 Cognitive errors

Faulty reasoning — gaps in understanding, information-processing slips, memory errors. Good news: education and coaching can help fix these.

e.g. anchoring · confirmation · mental accounting · overconfidence · recency

❤️ Emotional biases

Rooted in feelings, impulses, and intuition — not conscious thought. Harder to overcome; the planner often has to accommodate them rather than correct them.

e.g. loss aversion · status quo · familiarity/home bias · herding

🧠
Exam distinction: a cognitive error is a thinking/knowledge mistake you can teach away; an emotional bias comes from feelings and usually has to be worked around.

🃏 Name that bias

Biases are systematic, predictable deviations from rational decisions — they affect everyone, including the planner. Match each bias to what it looks like in practice:

The goal isn't to eliminate every bias but to prevent the damaging ones and occasionally harness the harmless ones — e.g. using labeled "goal" accounts to exploit mental accounting constructively, or pre-committing to automatic rebalancing so the calm client binds the panicked one.

💬 Communication & counseling skills

Data-gathering is a conversation, not an interrogation. Beneath biases sit durable values (security, independence, generosity) and attitudes (toward debt, risk, advice) — often formed before the client ever met you. A client raised through instability may hold "too much" cash; that's a value about security, and a spreadsheet won't move it. Acknowledge it and plan around it.

🪞 Nondirective — draw it out

Dominant in steps 1 & 2. You learn what the client actually thinks without steering it.

attending · active listening · open-ended questions · clarifying · paraphrasing · reflecting feeling · summarizing · silence

🧭 Directive — guide it

Dominant in step 5. The client came for a recommendation, not a survey of possibilities.

leading · advising/recommending · confronting · informing/educating · interpreting

🧠
Rule of thumb: nondirective early, directive late. Directing the conversation during data-gathering contaminates the information — the client tells you what you want to hear. Being nondirective during the presentation abdicates the planner's role.

Why use nondirective skills in step 1 and directive skills in step 5?
Step 1 aims to gather accurate, complete information; directing biases what the client reveals. Step 5 aims to secure understanding and action on a specific recommendation, which requires guidance rather than exploration.
A client won't sell a stock down 40%, saying they'll sell "when it gets back to what I paid." Which biases?
Anchoring on the purchase price (which has no bearing on future prospects) and loss aversion — realizing the loss feels worse than continuing to hold. Together, the disposition effect.
How does mental accounting explain carrying card debt while holding a labeled savings account?
The client treats the two as separate mental buckets rather than one balance sheet. Using the savings to retire much higher-rate debt is clearly better, but the "vacation fund" is mentally categorized as untouchable.

Chapter 1.5 · Ethics & Regulation

⚖️ The BIG one: standards of care

The single most consequential distinction in the module: who must put the client first, and how much?

🛡️ Fiduciary standard

Act in the client's best interest, placing the client above yourself. Held by RIAs (Investment Advisers Act of 1940), CFP® professionals giving financial advice, and ERISA fiduciaries.

🤝 Suitability standard

Weaker: the recommendation must be suitable — appropriate to objectives, situation, and risk tolerance — but need not be the best available option. Historically applied to broker-dealers.

The three fiduciary duties — tap to reveal what each requires:

⚠️
Why it matters — the two-fund test: two nearly identical funds, one paying the adviser a commission. Under suitability, recommending the commission fund can be defensible if it suits the client. Under a fiduciary standard, choosing the pricier fund because it pays you is a breach of loyalty. The client usually can't tell which standard they're getting — which is exactly why disclosure exists.

Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI), effective 2020, raised the bar for broker-dealer recommendations to retail customers — requiring them to act in the retail customer's best interest and not place their own interests ahead — though it remains distinct from the full fiduciary duty under the Advisers Act.

📚 Key statutes & Form ADV

🏛️ The 1940 acts & friends

Securities Act of 1933 (new-issue disclosure) · Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (created the SEC) · Investment Company Act of 1940 · Investment Advisers Act of 1940 (registration + fiduciary duty) · Gramm-Leach-Bliley (1999) (privacy) · Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) & Dodd-Frank (2010).

📄 Form ADV

The investment adviser's registration and primary client disclosure document — services, fees, conflicts, disciplinary history, business practices. Delivered to clients so they can evaluate the adviser and its conflicts.

State the practical difference between fiduciary and suitability standards.
A fiduciary must act in the client's best interest, putting the client above themselves. Suitability only requires the recommendation be appropriate — so a higher-cost product that pays the adviser can satisfy suitability while breaching fiduciary duty.
Which statute imposes fiduciary duty on registered investment advisers?
The Investment Advisers Act of 1940.
Why does the competence principle require declining certain engagements?
Competence obligates having the knowledge and skill the work requires and recognizing where that ends. Taking on work outside your competence breaches the principle regardless of outcome; the correct response is referral.
What is Form ADV for?
It's the adviser's registration and primary disclosure document — services, fees, conflicts, disciplinary history, business practices — delivered to clients so they can evaluate the adviser.
⚠️
Common pitfalls: assuming all professionals owe the same standard · treating fine print as "disclosure" · confusing suitability with best interest · implying a credential confers authority it doesn't · answering legal/tax questions rather than referring · failing to document why a recommendation was made.
