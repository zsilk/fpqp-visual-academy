# 4.1 — Principles of Risk Management

## Overview

Risk management asks a question that comes before insurance: what could go wrong,
how badly, and who should bear the cost? Insurance is only one of four possible
answers, and it isn't always the right one.

The organizing insight is that risks differ along two dimensions — **how likely**
they are and **how severe** they'd be — and the correct response depends almost
entirely on where a given risk falls on those two axes. Insuring a
high-frequency, low-severity risk is expensive and pointless. Retaining a
low-frequency, high-severity risk is how families get destroyed financially.

## Types of Personal Risk

The exposures a personal plan has to address:

| Risk | Nature | Primary tool |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Loss of income | The client's earning capacity is usually their largest asset | Disability insurance, emergency reserve |
| Unemployment | Temporary income interruption | Emergency reserve, unemployment benefits |
| Disability | Income stops while expenses often rise | Disability income insurance |
| Death | Income stops permanently; dependents lose support | Life insurance |
| Divorce | Assets divided, two households from one income base | Legal planning, prenuptial agreements |
| Property loss | Damage or destruction of owned assets | Property insurance |
| Liability | Legal responsibility for injury or damage to others | Liability and umbrella coverage |
| Longevity | Outliving assets | Annuities, retirement planning |
| Health care costs | Medical expenses, potentially catastrophic | Health insurance |
| Long-term care | Extended custodial care | LTC insurance, self-funding |

Two of these are chronically underweighted by clients. **Disability** is more
likely than death during working years, yet gets a fraction of the attention. And
**liability** is the risk with no natural ceiling — a judgment can exceed
everything the client owns and reach future income.

### Loss probability vs. loss severity

| | Low severity | High severity |
|---|-------------|---------------|
| **High frequency** | Retain — budget for it | Avoid — the exposure is untenable |
| **Low frequency** | Retain | **Transfer** — this is what insurance is for |

Insurance is economically sensible precisely in the low-frequency,
high-severity quadrant: events unlikely enough that premiums stay affordable, but
severe enough that the client can't absorb them.

This explains why extended warranties on small electronics are poor value — high
frequency, low severity, a risk the client should simply retain — while liability
coverage is essential.

## Risk Management Techniques

Four responses, often remembered as **avoid, reduce, retain, transfer**:

| Technique | Meaning | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| **Avoidance** | Don't engage in the activity | Not owning a trampoline or a boat |
| **Reduction** (control) | Lower frequency or severity | Smoke detectors, deadbolts, defensive driving, sprinklers |
| **Retention** | Accept the loss yourself | Deductibles, self-insuring small risks, emergency reserve |
| **Transfer** | Shift the financial consequence to another party | Insurance; also indemnity clauses in contracts |

These combine rather than compete. A homeowner reduces risk with smoke detectors,
retains the first $1,000 through a deductible, and transfers the remainder to an
insurer.

### Rules of risk management

Three principles that resolve most coverage decisions:

1. **Don't risk more than you can afford to lose.** This is what makes liability
   and disability coverage non-negotiable while extended warranties are optional.
2. **Consider the odds.** Don't pay a large premium for a remote risk with modest
   consequences.
3. **Don't risk a lot for a little.** Never leave a catastrophic exposure
   uninsured to save a small premium — the classic error is carrying minimum
   liability limits to save $150 a year.

### Self-insurance

Deliberately retaining a risk and funding it from your own resources. Legitimate
only when the potential loss is genuinely affordable and the client has actually
set money aside for it. "Self-insurance" without designated funds is not a
strategy — it's an uninsured exposure with a reassuring name.

Raising a deductible is a partial, sensible form of retention: it lowers premiums
by retaining the small, frequent portion of a risk while transferring the
catastrophic portion.

## Principles of Insurance

### How insurance works

Insurers pool many similar exposures. Individual losses are unpredictable, but
aggregate losses across a large pool are quite predictable — the **law of large
numbers**. That predictability lets the insurer price coverage so that premiums
collected cover expected claims plus expenses and profit.

### Peril vs. hazard

A distinction the program tests directly:

- A **peril** is the cause of loss — fire, windstorm, theft, collision.
- A **hazard** is a condition that increases the likelihood or severity of a
  peril.

| Hazard type | Definition | Example |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| **Physical** | A tangible condition | Frayed wiring, an unfenced pool, icy steps |
| **Moral** | Dishonesty — a deliberate intent to cause or exaggerate loss | Arson for insurance proceeds; inflating a claim |
| **Morale** | Carelessness arising from having coverage | Leaving a car unlocked because it's insured |

Moral versus morale is the classic exam trap. **Moral = dishonesty. Morale =
carelessness.** The first is intentional; the second is indifference.

### Requirements of an insurable risk

Not every risk can be insured. An insurable risk generally must be:

- **Measurable and determinable** — the loss can be quantified, and its time and
  cause established
- **Accidental and unintentional** from the insured's standpoint
- **Part of a large homogeneous pool** of similar exposures
- **Not catastrophic to the insurer** — losses shouldn't strike the whole pool at
  once, which is why flood and earthquake are often excluded from standard
  policies and handled separately
- **Economically feasible** — the premium must be affordable relative to the risk

Speculative risk — where gain is possible as well as loss — is not insurable.
Insurance covers **pure risk**, where the only outcomes are loss or no loss.
Investment losses are speculative, which is why no one insures them.

### Adverse selection

Those most likely to suffer a loss are most likely to seek coverage. Left
unmanaged, this drives up claims and prices, pushing lower-risk insureds out.
Insurers counter it through underwriting, exclusions, waiting periods, and
pre-existing condition provisions.

### Principle of indemnity

Insurance restores the insured to their pre-loss financial position — no better.
The insured should not profit from a loss. Several provisions enforce this:

- **Insurable interest** — the insured must stand to suffer genuine financial loss.
  For property, required at the time of loss; for life insurance, required at
  policy inception.
- **Actual cash value vs. replacement cost** — see below
- **Subrogation** — after paying a claim, the insurer takes over the insured's
  right to recover from the at-fault party, preventing double recovery
- **Coordination of benefits** — prevents collecting the same loss twice from
  multiple policies

### Valuing a loss

| Basis | Definition | Effect |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| **Actual cash value (ACV)** | Replacement cost − depreciation | Lower payout; insured absorbs depreciation |
| **Replacement cost** | Cost to replace with like kind and quality, no depreciation deduction | Higher payout, higher premium |
| **Agreed value / stated value** | Amount fixed in advance | Used for art, collectibles, unique property |

The ACV/replacement cost distinction is where clients get unpleasantly surprised.
A ten-year-old roof settled at ACV pays a fraction of what a new roof costs.

## The Insurance Contract

### Distinguishing legal characteristics

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|----------------|---------|
| **Aleatory** | Values exchanged are unequal — a small premium may yield a large benefit, or none |
| **Adhesion** | Drafted by the insurer and offered take-it-or-leave-it; ambiguities are construed against the drafter |
| **Unilateral** | Only the insurer makes an enforceable promise; the insured may stop paying |
| **Conditional** | The insurer's obligation depends on the insured meeting conditions |
| **Personal** | Insures the person, not the property — generally not assignable without consent |
| **Utmost good faith** | Both parties rely on the other's honest disclosure |

The adhesion characteristic has real consequences: because the insured had no
part in drafting, ambiguous language is interpreted in the insured's favor.

### Elements of a valid contract

Offer and acceptance, consideration, competent parties, and legal purpose — plus
the insurance-specific requirement of insurable interest.

### Policy sections

Nearly every property and casualty policy has the same five parts:

1. **Declarations** — the customized page: insured's name, property described,
   policy period, limits, deductibles, premium
2. **Definitions** — how key terms are used in the contract
3. **Insuring agreement** — the insurer's core promise; what is covered
4. **Conditions** — duties of both parties: paying premiums, reporting losses,
   cooperating with investigation, proof of loss
5. **Exclusions** — what is *not* covered

Exclusions are where coverage disputes concentrate, and reading them first is the
efficient way to understand a policy. Common property exclusions include flood,
earthquake, war, nuclear hazard, intentional acts, wear and tear, and
business activities conducted from the home.

**Endorsements** (riders) modify the base policy — adding coverage, removing it,
or scheduling specific high-value items.

### Named perils vs. open perils

- **Named perils** — covers only perils specifically listed. The **insured** must
  show the loss came from a listed peril.
- **Open perils** (all-risk) — covers all perils *except* those excluded. The
  **insurer** must show an exclusion applies.

Open perils is broader coverage and shifts the burden of proof to the insurer,
which is why it costs more.

## Insurance Distribution

### Two types of companies

- **Stock companies** — owned by shareholders, operated for profit. Policyholders
  are customers.
- **Mutual companies** — owned by policyholders, who may receive dividends
  representing a return of excess premium.

### Agents and brokers

| Role | Represents | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **Captive/exclusive agent** | One insurer | Binding authority for that company |
| **Independent agent** | Multiple insurers | Can compare across carriers |
| **Broker** | The **client**, not the insurer | Shops the market on the client's behalf |

An agent's authority matters legally: knowledge given to an agent is generally
imputed to the insurer, and an agent acting within apparent authority can bind
coverage.

### Evaluating an insurer

Financial strength determines whether claims get paid decades from now. Ratings
from A.M. Best, Standard & Poor's, Moody's, and Fitch are the standard reference.
State guaranty associations provide limited backup if an insurer fails, but the
limits are modest — the rating matters more.

## Common Pitfalls

- Insuring high-frequency, low-severity risks while leaving catastrophic exposures uncovered
- Confusing moral hazard (dishonesty) with morale hazard (carelessness)
- Assuming a policy pays replacement cost when it's written at ACV
- Treating "self-insurance" as a plan without designating funds
- Overlooking exclusions — flood and earthquake are not in a standard homeowners policy
- Carrying minimum liability limits to save a small premium
- Choosing an insurer on price without checking financial strength ratings

## Self-Check

**1. Distinguish peril, physical hazard, moral hazard, and morale hazard.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A peril is the cause of loss (fire, theft). A physical hazard is a tangible
condition increasing risk (frayed wiring). A moral hazard is dishonesty — intent
to cause or exaggerate a loss (arson for proceeds). A morale hazard is
carelessness because coverage exists (leaving a car unlocked).
</details>

**2. Which quadrant of frequency and severity is insurance designed for, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Low frequency, high severity. Low frequency keeps premiums affordable; high
severity means the client couldn't absorb the loss. High-frequency, low-severity
risks are cheaper to retain than to insure, since the premium must cover the
expected claims plus the insurer's costs.
</details>

**3. Why can't speculative risk be insured?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Speculative risk carries the possibility of gain as well as loss. Insurance
covers pure risk, where the only outcomes are loss or no loss. Insuring a chance
of gain would violate indemnity and create obvious moral hazard.
</details>

**4. What is subrogation and which principle does it serve?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
After paying a claim, the insurer assumes the insured's right to recover from the
at-fault party. It serves the principle of indemnity by preventing the insured
from collecting twice for the same loss.
</details>

**5. Under an open perils policy, who bears the burden of proof, and why does that
make it more valuable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The insurer, which must demonstrate that an exclusion applies. Under named perils
the insured must prove the loss came from a listed peril. Shifting that burden,
plus covering everything not excluded, makes open perils substantially broader.
</details>
