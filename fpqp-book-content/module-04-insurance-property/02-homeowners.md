# 4.2 — Homeowners Insurance

## Overview

A homeowners policy is a *package* policy: it bundles property coverage and
liability coverage into one contract. That structure is the key to understanding
it — the policy divides into **Section I** (property, Coverages A through D) and
**Section II** (liability, Coverages E and F).

Learn the six coverage letters and what each does, because nearly every question
about homeowners insurance reduces to identifying which coverage responds.

## Section I — Property Coverages

| Coverage | Covers | Typical limit |
|----------|--------|---------------|
| **A — Dwelling** | The house and structures attached to it | The chosen amount; drives the others |
| **B — Other structures** | Detached structures — garage, shed, fence | 10% of Coverage A |
| **C — Personal property** | Contents, wherever located | 50–70% of Coverage A |
| **D — Loss of use** | Additional living expenses while the home is uninhabitable | 20–30% of Coverage A |

Coverages B, C, and D are usually expressed as **percentages of Coverage A**,
which is why setting Coverage A correctly matters so much — it cascades through
the entire policy.

### Coverage A — Dwelling

The house itself plus attached structures. Should be set at the **replacement
cost of the structure**, not market value and not the purchase price. Those three
numbers differ, sometimes dramatically: market value includes land, which cannot
burn down. In high-land-value markets, insuring to market value substantially
over-insures; in depressed markets it can under-insure.

### Coverage B — Other structures

Detached structures — a freestanding garage, storage shed, fence, or gazebo.
Typically 10% of Coverage A, which is often inadequate where there's a
substantial detached garage or workshop. It can be increased by endorsement.

Structures used for business are generally excluded.

### Coverage C — Personal property

Contents — furniture, clothing, electronics, appliances. Follows the insured, so
property is generally covered off-premises too, often at a reduced limit.

Two features clients routinely misunderstand:

**Settlement basis.** Personal property is frequently covered at **actual cash
value** by default, even when the dwelling is covered at replacement cost. A
replacement cost endorsement on contents is inexpensive and usually worth
recommending.

**Special sublimits.** Certain categories carry internal caps far below the
Coverage C limit, regardless of how much total coverage is purchased:

| Category | Typical treatment |
|----------|-------------------|
| Jewelry, watches, furs | Low sublimit for theft |
| Silverware, goldware | Low sublimit for theft |
| Firearms | Low sublimit for theft |
| Cash and precious metals | Very low sublimit |
| Securities, deeds, manuscripts | Low sublimit |
| Business property on premises | Low sublimit |

The remedy is a **scheduled personal property endorsement** — listing specific
items with appraised values. Scheduling usually also broadens the covered perils
and may waive the deductible.

A client with a $12,000 engagement ring and a $250,000 Coverage C limit is not
covered for that ring beyond the sublimit. This is one of the most valuable
practical points a paraplanner can raise.

### Coverage D — Loss of use

Pays **additional** living expenses when the home is uninhabitable after a
covered loss — hotel, restaurant meals above normal food costs, temporary rental.

The operative word is *additional*: it covers the increase over normal living
costs, not total costs. It may also cover lost fair rental value if part of the
home was rented out.

## Section II — Liability Coverages

| Coverage | Covers |
|----------|--------|
| **E — Personal liability** | Bodily injury or property damage to others for which the insured is legally liable |
| **F — Medical payments to others** | Medical expenses for non-residents injured on the premises, **regardless of fault** |

### Coverage E — Personal liability

Responds when the insured is legally responsible for injury or damage to someone
else — a guest falls on the steps, the insured's dog bites someone, a child
breaks a neighbor's window. It covers worldwide personal activities, not just
incidents at the home.

Critically, it includes the insurer's **duty to defend**, and defense costs are
generally paid *in addition to* the policy limit. Legal defense is often the
larger expense in a liability claim, so this matters.

Base limits are commonly $100,000–$300,000, which is low relative to real
judgment exposure — the reason umbrella coverage exists (see
[4.3](03-auto-umbrella.md)).

### Coverage F — Medical payments to others

A small no-fault benefit, typically $1,000–$5,000, paying medical expenses for
injured guests without any determination of liability. Its purpose is practical:
paying a modest medical bill promptly often prevents a minor incident from
escalating into a liability claim.

It does **not** cover the insured or regular residents of the household — their
injuries are a health insurance matter.

## Policy Forms

Standard forms differ by property type and breadth of coverage:

| Form | For | Dwelling perils | Contents perils |
|------|-----|----------------|-----------------|
| **HO-2** | Homeowner, basic | Named perils (broad) | Named perils |
| **HO-3** | Homeowner — **most common** | Open perils | Named perils |
| **HO-4** | Renter | N/A (no dwelling) | Named perils |
| **HO-5** | Homeowner, most comprehensive | Open perils | **Open perils** |
| **HO-6** | Condominium owner | Limited — interior/improvements | Named perils |
| **HO-8** | Older home, replacement cost exceeds market value | Named perils, modified settlement | Named perils |

The **HO-3 versus HO-5** distinction is the one to know: both cover the dwelling
on an open perils basis, but HO-5 extends open perils to *contents* as well. HO-3
is the market standard; HO-5 is the upgrade.

The condo form (HO-6) covers what the association's master policy doesn't —
generally interior finishes, improvements, and personal property. Reviewing the
master policy is necessary to identify the gap.

## How Much Insurance Is Needed?

### The 80% coinsurance requirement

The provision that produces the most unpleasant surprises. To collect **full
replacement cost** on a partial loss, the dwelling must be insured to at least
**80% of its full replacement cost**. Fall below that, and the insurer pays the
greater of ACV or a proportionally reduced amount:

> **Payment = Loss × (Amount of insurance carried ÷ (0.80 × Replacement cost))**

### Worked example

A home with a replacement cost of $400,000. The required minimum is
0.80 × $400,000 = **$320,000**.

**Case 1 — insured for $320,000**, partial loss of $60,000.
Requirement met, so the loss is paid in full (less deductible): **$60,000**.

**Case 2 — insured for $240,000**, same $60,000 loss.

```
$60,000 × ($240,000 ÷ $320,000) = $60,000 × 0.75 = $45,000
```

The insurer pays **$45,000**, and the client absorbs $15,000 plus the deductible
— despite carrying $240,000 of coverage on a $60,000 loss. Under-insurance
penalizes *partial* losses, which are the overwhelming majority of claims.

Note also that a total loss is capped at the policy limit regardless: a client
insured for $240,000 on a $400,000 home who suffers a total loss recovers
$240,000, leaving a $160,000 gap.

### Keeping Coverage A current

Replacement costs rise with construction costs, which have at times risen much
faster than general inflation. An **inflation guard endorsement** adjusts the
limit automatically each year. Renovations should trigger a coverage review —
a finished basement or addition raises replacement cost immediately.

An **extended** or **guaranteed replacement cost** endorsement pays above the
policy limit (a stated percentage, or fully) when rebuilding costs exceed
expectations — valuable protection after widespread disasters, when local
construction costs spike.

## Common Exclusions and Endorsements

Standard homeowners policies **exclude**:

- **Flood** — requires separate coverage, typically through the NFIP or a private
  insurer
- **Earthquake** — available by endorsement or separate policy
- Wear and tear, deterioration, and maintenance issues
- Insect, vermin, and rodent damage
- Intentional acts by the insured
- Business activities conducted from the residence
- War and nuclear hazard
- Neglect to protect property after a loss

Flood is the most consequential. Clients routinely assume it's covered, and it
isn't — including for water backing up through sewers or drains, which needs its
own endorsement.

Useful endorsements:

| Endorsement | Adds |
|-------------|------|
| Scheduled personal property | Itemized high-value property above sublimits |
| Replacement cost on contents | Removes depreciation from Coverage C settlement |
| Water/sewer backup | Coverage for backup through drains and sump overflow |
| Inflation guard | Automatic annual limit increases |
| Extended/guaranteed replacement cost | Rebuilding costs above the Coverage A limit |
| Home business | Limited business property and liability |
| Identity theft | Recovery expenses |

## Common Pitfalls

- Setting Coverage A to market value or purchase price rather than replacement cost
- Failing the 80% coinsurance requirement and discovering it at a partial loss
- Assuming contents settle at replacement cost when the default is often ACV
- Overlooking sublimits on jewelry, cash, and collectibles
- Assuming flood or sewer backup is covered
- Never updating limits after renovations
- Carrying base liability limits without considering an umbrella
- Condo owners not reviewing the association master policy to identify the gap

## Self-Check

**1. Match the coverage letters to what they insure.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A — dwelling and attached structures. B — other, detached structures. C —
personal property/contents. D — loss of use / additional living expenses. E —
personal liability. F — medical payments to others.
</details>

**2. A home has a $500,000 replacement cost and is insured for $300,000. A
$100,000 partial loss occurs. What does the insurer pay, ignoring the deductible?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Required minimum is 0.80 × $500,000 = $400,000, so the requirement isn't met.
Payment = $100,000 × ($300,000 ÷ $400,000) = **$75,000**. The client absorbs
$25,000.
</details>

**3. Why is Coverage A set to replacement cost rather than market value?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Market value includes land, which can't be destroyed, and reflects location and
market conditions unrelated to rebuilding cost. Insurance is meant to rebuild the
structure, so the correct basis is what construction would cost — which may be
well above or below market value.
</details>

**4. A client owns a $15,000 painting and has $300,000 of Coverage C. Are they
covered?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Not fully. Fine art and similar categories carry internal sublimits well below
the Coverage C limit, and open perils coverage may not apply. The remedy is a
scheduled personal property endorsement listing the appraised item, which also
typically broadens perils and may waive the deductible.
</details>

**5. Distinguish Coverage E from Coverage F.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Coverage E is personal liability — it responds when the insured is legally liable
for injury or damage to others, and includes defense costs generally paid above
the limit. Coverage F is medical payments to others — a small no-fault benefit
paying a guest's medical expenses regardless of fault, designed to settle minor
incidents before they become liability claims.
</details>
