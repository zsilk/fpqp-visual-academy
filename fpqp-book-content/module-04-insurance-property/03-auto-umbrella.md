# 4.3 — Automobile and Umbrella Liability Insurance

## Overview

Auto insurance is where most households encounter their largest liability
exposure, because driving is the routine activity most likely to injure someone
seriously. It's also where clients most often economize in exactly the wrong
place — buying low liability limits to save premium while paying for a low
collision deductible.

The principle from 4.1 applies directly: **don't risk a lot for a little.** The
premium difference between minimum liability limits and substantial ones is
usually modest; the difference in protection is enormous.

Umbrella coverage then sits above both auto and homeowners, addressing the risk
with no natural ceiling.

## The Personal Auto Policy

### Coverage parts

| Part | Coverage | Protects |
|------|----------|----------|
| **A** | Liability — bodily injury and property damage | Others, when the insured is at fault |
| **B** | Medical payments | The insured and passengers, regardless of fault |
| **C** | Uninsured / underinsured motorist | The insured, when the at-fault driver has no or insufficient coverage |
| **D** | Damage to your auto — collision and other than collision | The insured's own vehicle |

Parts A through C address people; Part D addresses the car. Clients tend to focus
on Part D because it's the visible, frequent claim, while Part A carries the
catastrophic exposure.

### Part A — Liability

Pays for injury and property damage the insured is legally responsible for, and
includes the insurer's **duty to defend**, with defense costs generally paid
above the limit.

Limits appear in two formats:

**Split limits** — three numbers, e.g. **100/300/50**:

- $100,000 bodily injury per person
- $300,000 bodily injury per accident (all people combined)
- $50,000 property damage per accident

**Combined single limit (CSL)** — one number covering all bodily injury and
property damage per accident, e.g. $500,000. More flexible, since it isn't
subdivided, and generally preferable when available.

State minimum limits are set for political feasibility, not adequacy. Minimums
in many states wouldn't cover a single serious injury, and any judgment above
the limit reaches the insured's assets and future income through wage
garnishment.

### Part B — Medical payments

Pays medical expenses for the insured and passengers regardless of fault, up to a
modest limit. Coordinates with health insurance and pays quickly, without waiting
for a liability determination.

### Part C — Uninsured / underinsured motorist

Frequently undervalued. It covers the insured's own injuries when the at-fault
driver has **no** insurance (UM) or **insufficient** insurance (UIM).

The logic is straightforward: a large share of drivers are uninsured or carry
state minimums. If such a driver causes serious injury, the insured's own UM/UIM
coverage is the only meaningful source of recovery. Carrying high liability
limits protects *other people*; carrying high UM/UIM limits protects the
*client*. Matching UM/UIM to the liability limit is the standard recommendation.

### Part D — Damage to your auto

Two components:

- **Collision** — impact with another vehicle or object, or overturning
- **Other than collision** (comprehensive) — theft, fire, hail, flood, vandalism,
  falling objects, animal strikes, glass breakage

Both carry deductibles, and both settle at **actual cash value** — the vehicle's
depreciated value, not what it costs to replace. This is the source of the **gap
exposure**: when a financed vehicle is totaled and the loan balance exceeds ACV,
the insured owes the difference. **Gap insurance** covers it, and matters most in
the early years of a long loan with a small down payment.

Collision and comprehensive are optional and generally not worth carrying on an
older vehicle whose ACV approaches the deductible. Raising deductibles is
efficient retention — it lowers premium by absorbing small, frequent losses while
preserving coverage for large ones.

## Cost of Auto Insurance

Rating factors typically include driving record, annual mileage and use, vehicle
make and model (repair cost and theft frequency), garaging location, age and
driving experience, coverage limits and deductibles chosen, and — where permitted
by state law — credit-based insurance scores. Some states restrict or prohibit
certain factors.

Ways to reduce premium without reducing protection meaningfully:

- Raise deductibles on collision and comprehensive
- Drop collision/comprehensive on low-value vehicles
- Bundle auto with homeowners for a multi-policy discount
- Claim discounts for safety features, defensive driving, good student status, low mileage
- Improve credit where it's a permitted rating factor

Note that none of these involve cutting liability limits, which is the one
economy that should not be made.

## Tort vs. No-Fault

Two liability systems, varying by state:

| System | How it works |
|--------|--------------|
| **Tort (fault)** | The at-fault driver's insurer pays; the injured party may sue for economic and non-economic damages |
| **No-fault** | Each driver's own insurer pays their economic losses (personal injury protection) regardless of fault, with limits on the right to sue |

No-fault systems aim for faster payment and less litigation over minor claims.
They typically restrict lawsuits unless injuries exceed a monetary or verbal
threshold. States vary considerably, and some are hybrids.

Related negligence concepts:

- **Contributory negligence** — a plaintiff even slightly at fault recovers
  nothing. Harsh; retained in only a few jurisdictions.
- **Comparative negligence** — recovery is reduced by the plaintiff's share of
  fault. *Pure* comparative allows recovery at any fault level; *modified*
  comparative bars recovery at 50% or 51% fault.

## Umbrella Liability Insurance

### What it does

A separate policy providing liability coverage **above** the limits of the
underlying auto and homeowners policies, typically in increments of $1 million.

Its purpose is to address the exposure with no natural ceiling. Property loss is
bounded by what the client owns; a liability judgment is bounded only by what a
court awards, and can reach current assets *and* future earnings.

### How it works

The umbrella sits on top of underlying coverage and requires the client to
maintain specified **minimum underlying limits** — commonly something like
250/500/100 on auto and $300,000 on homeowners liability. If the client lets
underlying coverage drop below the requirement, the umbrella treats the shortfall
as though it were still in place, leaving the client personally responsible for
the gap.

Above the underlying limits, the umbrella responds up to its own limit.

### Worked example

A client carries auto liability of 250/500/100 and a $1 million umbrella. They
cause an accident resulting in a $900,000 judgment for one injured person.

- Auto policy pays its per-person limit: **$250,000**
- Umbrella pays the remainder: **$650,000**
- Client out of pocket: **$0**

Without the umbrella, the client would owe $650,000 personally — satisfied from
savings, home equity, and garnished future wages.

### Why it's efficient

Umbrella premiums are low relative to the coverage — often a few hundred dollars
a year for $1 million — because the underlying policies absorb the frequent
claims and the umbrella only responds to rare severe ones. This is exactly the
low-frequency, high-severity quadrant where insurance is economically sensible.

It also typically provides **broader** coverage than the underlying policies,
often including personal injury offenses such as libel, slander, and defamation
that homeowners liability may exclude.

### Who especially needs one

- Anyone with meaningful assets or substantial future earning capacity
- Households with teenage drivers
- Owners of pools, trampolines, dogs, boats, or recreational vehicles
- Landlords and rental property owners
- People serving on nonprofit boards
- Anyone with public visibility or elevated exposure to defamation claims

The general recommendation is umbrella coverage at least equal to net worth, and
higher for clients with substantial future income to protect — a young
professional with few assets but high earnings still has something a judgment
can reach.

## Common Pitfalls

- Carrying state minimum liability limits to save a small premium
- Setting UM/UIM below liability limits, protecting others better than the client
- Assuming ACV settlement equals the loan balance on a financed vehicle
- Carrying collision on a vehicle worth barely more than the deductible
- Letting underlying limits fall below the umbrella's requirement, creating a gap
- Assuming a homeowners policy alone provides adequate liability protection
- Treating an umbrella as a luxury for the wealthy rather than protection for future income

## Self-Check

**1. Decode split limits of 100/300/50.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
$100,000 bodily injury per person, $300,000 bodily injury per accident across all
injured people, and $50,000 property damage per accident.
</details>

**2. Why should UM/UIM limits match liability limits?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Liability coverage protects other people from the insured's negligence. UM/UIM
protects the insured when the at-fault driver has no or inadequate coverage.
Since a substantial share of drivers are uninsured or carry minimums, matching
the limits means the client is as well protected as the strangers they might
injure.
</details>

**3. A client owes $28,000 on a car with an ACV of $21,000, and it's totaled.
Result?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The insurer pays ACV of $21,000 less the deductible, and the client still owes
roughly $7,000 plus the deductible on a car they no longer have. Gap insurance
covers this difference.
</details>

**4. A client with 250/500/100 auto limits and a $1M umbrella faces a $900,000
judgment to one person. Who pays what?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The auto policy pays its $250,000 per-person limit; the umbrella pays the
remaining $650,000. The client pays nothing out of pocket. Without the umbrella
they would personally owe $650,000.
</details>

**5. Why does a young professional with few assets still benefit from an umbrella?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A judgment reaches future income, not just current assets. Wage garnishment can
follow someone for years, so high future earning capacity is itself the asset
being protected — and umbrella premiums are low precisely because severe claims
are rare.
</details>
