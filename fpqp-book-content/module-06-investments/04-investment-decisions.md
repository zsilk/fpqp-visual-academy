# 6.4 — Investment Decisions

## Overview

This chapter assembles the prior three into a decision process: given the asset
classes, the risk framework, and the vehicles, how is an actual portfolio built?

The governing finding is that **asset allocation** — how a portfolio is divided
among asset classes — explains the large majority of the variability in portfolio
returns over time. Security selection and market timing account for far less than
most investors assume. The practical implication is where to spend effort: the
allocation decision deserves the most care, and it's also the one most under the
planner's control.

## Asset Allocation

### Time horizon drives allocation

The single most important input, for the reason established in
[1.1](../module-01-financial-planning-process/01-planning-and-goals.md): a longer
horizon allows recovery from losses, so it supports more volatility.

| Horizon | Typical emphasis | Rationale |
|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| Under 1 year | Cash equivalents | No time to recover; principal certainty required |
| 1–3 years | Cash and short-term bonds | Limited recovery window |
| 3–10 years | Balanced stocks and bonds | Moderate recovery capacity |
| 10+ years | Equity-weighted | Volatility is tolerable; return differential dominates |

The two classic errors are symmetric and both costly: holding a near-term goal in
equities (a drawdown arrives with no time to recover) and holding a long-term
goal in cash (inflation erodes it while the return differential compounds
against the client).

### Other inputs

- **Risk capacity** — what loss the client can financially absorb
- **Risk tolerance** — what they can emotionally sustain; plan to the lower of the two
- **Goals and required return** — the return the plan actually needs. If a
  conservative allocation funds the goal, taking additional risk is unnecessary
- **Tax situation** — drives asset location across account types
- **Liquidity needs** — money that may be needed can't be committed to illiquid assets
- **Existing concentrations** — employer stock, a closely held business, or real
  estate already dominating the balance sheet

That fourth bullet deserves emphasis: a client who has already won doesn't need to
keep playing. If the required return is achievable with less risk, the
recommendation is less risk.

### Strategic vs. tactical

**Strategic allocation** sets long-term target weights based on goals and risk
profile, holding them through market cycles with periodic rebalancing. This is
the default and the defensible approach.

**Tactical allocation** makes short-term deviations from targets based on market
views. It depends on forecasting ability that is difficult to demonstrate, and it
is where costs and taxes accumulate.

### Rebalancing

Over time, appreciation pushes the portfolio away from target weights. A 60/40
portfolio after a strong equity run may become 72/28 — meaningfully riskier than
intended, arrived at without any decision being made.

**Rebalancing** restores targets by selling what's grown and buying what's
lagged. Two triggers are common:

- **Calendar** — review at set intervals, such as annually
- **Threshold** — rebalance when an asset class drifts beyond a band, such as ±5
  percentage points

Rebalancing is genuinely difficult behaviorally, because it requires selling
recent winners and buying recent losers — the opposite of what recency bias and
herding urge. Committing to a written rule in advance is what makes it happen,
which is the pre-commitment technique from
[1.4](../module-01-financial-planning-process/04-client-behaviors.md).

In taxable accounts, rebalancing realizes gains, so prefer rebalancing with new
contributions, directing distributions, and using tax-advantaged accounts for the
trades.

## Investment Strategies

### Buy-and-hold

Purchase and hold through market cycles rather than trading on market movements.

Advantages: minimal transaction costs, deferred capital gains, no dependence on
timing ability, and immunity to the largest behavioral errors. The evidence
favoring it is strong — investors who trade more tend to earn less, and missing a
small number of the best market days materially reduces long-run returns.

Buy-and-hold is not the same as neglect. It is compatible with periodic
rebalancing and with selling when the underlying reason for holding changes.

### Dollar-cost averaging

Investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals regardless of price. More
shares are purchased when prices are low and fewer when high, so the average cost
per share is below the average price.

Its genuine value is behavioral and practical: it's how payroll retirement
contributions work naturally, and it removes the paralysis of deciding when to
invest. Against a lump sum available immediately, investing all at once has
historically produced better expected outcomes, since markets rise more often
than they fall — but dollar-cost averaging reduces regret risk and is often the
plan a client will actually follow.

### Value and growth investing

| | Value | Growth |
|---|-------|--------|
| Focus | Securities priced below intrinsic worth | Companies with above-average earnings growth |
| Typical metrics | Low P/E, low price-to-book, higher dividend yield | High revenue and earnings growth, often reinvesting rather than paying dividends |
| Thesis | The market has underpriced the company | Growth will justify the current price |
| Risk | The discount may be deserved — a "value trap" | Growth may disappoint; high valuations fall hard |

Neither dominates across all periods; they lead in alternating cycles. Most
diversified portfolios hold both — a **blend** approach — which avoids
concentrating in whichever style is currently out of favor.

### Other approaches

- **Indexing** — accept market returns at minimal cost, per
  [6.3](03-mutual-funds.md)
- **Core and satellite** — an indexed core with smaller active positions
- **Sector rotation** — shifting among industries based on economic cycle views;
  timing-dependent
- **Laddering** — staggered bond or CD maturities to manage rate and reinvestment risk

## The Investment Policy Statement

A written document governing how a portfolio is managed. It's the investment
analogue of the financial plan itself, and it exists mainly to bind the calm
investor against the panicked one.

### Typical contents

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| Objectives | Goals, required return, time horizon |
| Risk parameters | Tolerance, capacity, acceptable volatility |
| Asset allocation | Target weights and permitted ranges per class |
| Constraints | Liquidity needs, taxes, legal issues, holdings to avoid |
| Selection criteria | Standards for choosing securities and funds |
| Rebalancing policy | Triggers and method |
| Monitoring | Review frequency, benchmarks for evaluation |
| Responsibilities | Who does what — client, planner, other advisers |

### Why it matters

Written *before* volatility arrives, the IPS establishes what the client agreed
to when calm. When markets fall and the impulse to sell surfaces, the IPS is the
reference point: this decline is within the range we planned for, and the policy
says rebalance rather than liquidate.

It also creates accountability in both directions and documents the basis for
recommendations — which connects to the documentation obligations in
[1.5](../module-01-financial-planning-process/05-ethics-regulation.md).

### Sketch of an IPS

For a client, 42, retiring at 65, with moderate risk tolerance:

- **Objective**: fund retirement at 65; required return 6.5% nominal
- **Horizon**: 23 years to retirement, 30+ years through retirement
- **Allocation targets**: 70% equity (45% U.S., 18% international, 7% real
  estate), 30% fixed income
- **Ranges**: ±5 percentage points per class
- **Rebalancing**: annual review; rebalance on a 5-point breach, using new
  contributions first
- **Constraints**: maintain six months' expenses in cash outside the portfolio;
  no employer stock beyond 5%
- **Selection**: broad, low-cost index funds; expense ratios under 0.20%
- **Review**: annually, and on any material change in circumstances

## SWOT Analysis

A structured way to evaluate an investment or a client's overall position across
four dimensions:

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---------|---------|
| **Internal** | **Strengths** | **Weaknesses** |
| **External** | **Opportunities** | **Threats** |

### Applied to a client's investment position

- **Strengths** — high savings rate, long horizon, stable income, employer match,
  low costs
- **Weaknesses** — concentrated employer stock, no IPS, high-cost funds, thin
  emergency reserve
- **Opportunities** — unused Roth or HSA capacity, tax-loss harvesting, an
  increased match
- **Threats** — market volatility, inflation, job instability, tax law changes,
  behavioral reaction to a drawdown

The value of the framework is that it forces attention to the **external**
dimension. Clients and planners naturally focus on the portfolio itself
(internal) and underweight the environment it operates in.

## Putting It Together

A workable sequence:

1. Confirm goals, horizons, and priorities (steps 1–2 of the planning process)
2. Assess risk tolerance and risk capacity; plan to the lower
3. Determine the **required return** the plan actually needs
4. Set a strategic allocation consistent with 1–3
5. Decide **asset location** — tax-inefficient holdings in tax-deferred accounts,
   tax-efficient in taxable
6. Select low-cost vehicles that implement the allocation
7. Document it in an IPS
8. Rebalance per policy
9. Monitor and revise on material changes — step 7 of the planning process

Note that vehicle selection appears at step 6, not step 1. Clients and salespeople
both tend to start with products; the process starts with goals.

## Common Pitfalls

- Selecting investments before determining the allocation
- Taking more risk than the required return justifies
- Letting drift silently raise portfolio risk by skipping rebalancing
- Rebalancing in a taxable account without regard to realized gains
- Confusing tactical allocation with a strategy, absent forecasting ability
- Treating dollar-cost averaging as superior to lump-sum investing on expected-return grounds
- Operating without a written IPS, leaving decisions to be made under stress
- Ignoring existing concentrations, especially employer stock
- Placing tax-inefficient assets in taxable accounts

## Self-Check

**1. Why does asset allocation receive more attention than security selection?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Allocation across asset classes explains the large majority of variability in
portfolio returns over time, while security selection and market timing explain
much less. Effort is best spent where it has the greatest effect.
</details>

**2. A 60/40 portfolio drifts to 72/28 after a strong equity run. What's the
problem?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The portfolio is now meaningfully riskier than intended, and the client arrived
there without deciding to. Rebalancing back to 60/40 restores the risk level the
plan called for — which requires selling recent winners, the behaviorally hard
part.
</details>

**3. What is the purpose of an investment policy statement?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
To document objectives, risk parameters, target allocation, constraints, and
rebalancing policy in advance. Its main practical function is providing an
agreed-upon reference during volatility, so decisions follow policy set while
calm rather than impulse under stress.
</details>

**4. When is dollar-cost averaging the right recommendation, given lump-sum
investing has better expected outcomes?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
When the client would otherwise delay investing entirely, or when regret risk
would cause them to abandon the strategy after a poorly-timed lump sum. It's also
the natural mechanism for ongoing payroll contributions. The justification is
behavioral rather than mathematical.
</details>

**5. A client's required return is 5% and their allocation targets 9%. What
should the planner raise?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Whether the additional risk is necessary. If a more conservative allocation funds
the goals reliably, taking extra volatility adds risk of failure without adding
required benefit. The client may still choose growth for other reasons, but it
should be a deliberate choice.
</details>

**6. What does the external half of SWOT add?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It forces consideration of opportunities and threats outside the portfolio —
inflation, tax law changes, job stability, market conditions — which clients and
planners tend to underweight relative to the internal strengths and weaknesses of
the holdings themselves.
</details>
