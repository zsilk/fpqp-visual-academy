# 6.3 — Mutual Funds and ETFs

## Overview

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and buys a portfolio of securities.
Each investor owns shares of the fund rather than the underlying holdings
directly.

The economic point is access: a client with $5,000 can obtain diversification
across hundreds of securities that would be impossible to assemble individually.
Since [6.2](02-risk.md) established that unsystematic risk is uncompensated and
diversifiable, pooled vehicles are the practical mechanism for capturing that
benefit at small scale.

The counterweight is **cost**. Fund expenses are certain and recurring, while
outperformance is not, so cost analysis is the most reliable lever an investor
controls.

## Advantages

- **Diversification** at low minimums
- **Professional management** and research
- **Liquidity** — mutual fund shares are redeemable at the next computed NAV
- **Convenience** — automatic investment, reinvested dividends, recordkeeping
- **Economies of scale** in trading costs
- **Regulatory oversight** under the Investment Company Act of 1940, including
  mandated disclosure

## Costs

Costs are where fund selection is won or lost, because they're the one variable
known in advance.

### Ongoing costs

**Expense ratio** — annual operating costs as a percentage of assets, deducted
from fund assets automatically. It's never billed separately, which is exactly
why investors underweight it.

Components include the management fee, administrative costs, and any **12b-1
fee** (an annual distribution and marketing charge, capped by rule).

The effect compounds. Two funds earning identical gross returns of 7% over 30
years on a $100,000 investment:

| Expense ratio | Net return | Ending value |
|---------------|-----------|--------------|
| 0.10% | 6.90% | **$740,169** |
| 1.10% | 5.90% | **$558,314** |

A one-percentage-point difference costs roughly **$181,855** — about 25% of the
final balance — with no difference in gross performance. This calculation is the
strongest argument in favor of low-cost investing, and it's worth running for
clients rather than asserting.

### Sales charges (loads)

| Share class | Charge structure | Typically suits |
|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Class A** | **Front-end load** deducted at purchase; lower ongoing 12b-1 | Larger, long-horizon investments |
| **Class B** | **Back-end load** (CDSC) declining over years; higher 12b-1 | Largely phased out |
| **Class C** | Little or no front load; **higher ongoing** 12b-1, small CDSC early | Shorter holding periods |
| **No-load** | No sales charge | Self-directed investors |

**Breakpoints** reduce front-end loads at higher investment amounts, and a
**letter of intent** or **rights of accumulation** can secure a breakpoint over
time. Failing to claim an available breakpoint is a recognized compliance
problem.

The structural point: Class A costs more up front and less annually; Class C
costs less up front and more forever. Over a long horizon Class C's ongoing drag
typically exceeds Class A's initial load.

### Other charges

Redemption fees (discouraging short-term trading), exchange fees, and account
maintenance fees.

## Net Asset Value

> **NAV = (Total assets − Total liabilities) ÷ Shares outstanding**

Computed once per day, after market close. Mutual fund orders execute at the
**next** computed NAV — **forward pricing** — not at a price known when the order
is placed. This is a genuine difference from ETFs and from stocks.

## The Prospectus

Funds must deliver a prospectus disclosing objectives, strategies, risks, fees
and expenses, performance history, management, and purchase and redemption
procedures.

A **summary prospectus** presents the key information in a short standardized
format, with the full document available on request. The **statement of
additional information (SAI)** contains further detail.

Practical reading order: the **fee table** first, then objectives and strategy,
then risks, then performance in the context of the fund's benchmark. Fee tables
are standardized precisely so funds can be compared directly.

## Types of Mutual Funds

### By objective

| Category | Objective | Typical holdings |
|----------|-----------|------------------|
| Money market | Preservation and liquidity | Short-term debt |
| Bond / income | Income | Government, municipal, corporate bonds |
| Balanced / hybrid | Growth and income | Stocks and bonds |
| Growth | Capital appreciation | Companies with earnings growth |
| Value | Undervalued securities | Low price relative to fundamentals |
| Growth and income | Both | Dividend-paying growth companies |
| Sector | Concentrated exposure | A single industry |
| International / global | Foreign exposure | Non-U.S. securities |
| Target date | Age-based glide path | Diversified, shifting conservative over time |
| Index | Track a benchmark | The index's constituents |

### Active vs. passive

**Actively managed** funds attempt to outperform a benchmark through security
selection and timing. They cost more and introduce manager risk.

**Index (passive)** funds replicate a benchmark, with much lower expense ratios
and turnover.

The evidence consistently shows that a majority of active managers underperform
their benchmarks over long periods, largely because of costs, and that past
outperformance is a weak predictor of future outperformance. This doesn't make
active management indefensible, but it does place the burden of justification on
it.

### Open-end vs. closed-end

| | Open-end | Closed-end |
|---|---------|------------|
| Shares | Created and redeemed continuously | Fixed number, IPO then trades |
| Pricing | At NAV, forward-priced | Market price, may trade at a **premium or discount** to NAV |
| Trading | With the fund | On an exchange |

## Exchange-Traded Funds

ETFs hold a portfolio like a mutual fund but **trade on an exchange throughout
the day** like a stock.

### Advantages

- **Intraday trading** at market prices, with limit and stop orders available
- **Low expense ratios**, particularly for broad index ETFs
- **Tax efficiency** — see below
- Transparency, with holdings typically disclosed daily
- No minimum investment beyond one share

### Tax efficiency

The most important structural advantage, and worth understanding rather than
memorizing.

When a mutual fund investor redeems, the fund may have to **sell securities** to
raise cash. Realized gains are then distributed to **all remaining shareholders**,
who owe tax on gains they didn't choose to trigger. An investor can owe capital
gains tax in a year the fund lost money.

ETFs largely avoid this through an **in-kind creation and redemption** mechanism
with authorized participants, which transfers securities rather than selling
them, generating few realized gains.

The consequence for planning: ETFs and index funds are generally preferable in
**taxable** accounts, while the distinction matters far less inside tax-deferred
accounts, where distributions aren't currently taxed. This is a core **asset
location** decision.

### Costs and considerations

ETFs have no loads but incur **brokerage commissions** where applicable and a
**bid-ask spread** on every trade. Thinly traded ETFs can have wide spreads,
which functions as a hidden cost. Market price can also deviate slightly from
NAV, though arbitrage keeps broad ETFs close.

Intraday tradability is a double-edged feature: it enables efficient execution
and it invites overtrading, which is a behavioral risk more than a structural
one.

### Mutual funds vs. ETFs

| | Mutual fund | ETF |
|---|------------|-----|
| Trading | Once daily at NAV | Intraday at market price |
| Pricing | Forward-priced | Continuous |
| Minimum | Fund-specified | One share |
| Tax efficiency | Lower — capital gains distributions | **Higher** |
| Transaction cost | Possible loads | Commissions and spreads |
| Automatic investing | Straightforward | Less so, though improving |

## Taxation of Fund Distributions

| Distribution | Treatment |
|--------------|-----------|
| Qualified dividends | Preferential long-term capital gains rates |
| Ordinary dividends | Ordinary income |
| Short-term capital gain distributions | Ordinary income |
| Long-term capital gain distributions | Long-term capital gains rates |
| Return of capital | Not immediately taxable; reduces basis |

Two points that regularly surprise investors:

- **Reinvested distributions are taxable** in the year distributed, even though no
  cash was received. They also **increase basis**, and failing to track that
  causes the same gain to be taxed twice on eventual sale.
- Buying a fund **shortly before a distribution date** means receiving — and
  paying tax on — a distribution that is economically just a return of the
  purchase price. Checking distribution dates before a large year-end purchase in
  a taxable account is a genuine planning point.

## Evaluating a Fund

1. **Objective fit** — does it match the role intended in the allocation?
2. **Cost** — expense ratio and loads, compared to category peers
3. **Performance versus its benchmark** over full market cycles, not recent quarters
4. **Risk measures** — standard deviation and beta relative to peers
5. **Turnover** — high turnover raises trading costs and taxable distributions
6. **Manager tenure** — a long record under a different manager says little
7. **Fund size** — very large funds can face capacity constraints in small-cap strategies
8. **Tax efficiency**, if held in a taxable account

Cost and objective fit are the most reliable of these. Past performance is the
least reliable and receives the most attention — a mismatch worth naming
explicitly with clients.

## Common Pitfalls

- Selecting funds on past performance, which predicts poorly
- Ignoring expense ratios because they're never billed visibly
- Buying Class C shares for a long horizon, where ongoing fees exceed a front load
- Missing available breakpoints on Class A purchases
- Holding tax-inefficient funds in taxable accounts and efficient ones in IRAs
- Purchasing a fund just before a capital gain distribution in a taxable account
- Failing to add reinvested distributions to basis, double-taxing the gain
- Assuming many funds equals diversification when holdings overlap heavily
- Trading ETFs actively simply because intraday trading is possible

## Self-Check

**1. Why are ETFs generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Mutual funds may sell securities to meet redemptions, realizing gains that are
distributed to all remaining shareholders. ETFs use in-kind creation and
redemption, transferring securities rather than selling them, so few gains are
realized and distributed.
</details>

**2. A fund's expense ratio is 1.15% versus 0.15% for an index alternative. Why
does this matter more than it appears?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The 1% difference compounds annually over the entire holding period. On $100,000
at 7% gross over 30 years it costs roughly $182,000 — about a quarter of the
ending balance — and unlike returns, the cost difference is certain in advance.
</details>

**3. What is NAV and when does a mutual fund order execute?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
NAV = (total assets − total liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding, computed once
daily after market close. Orders execute at the next computed NAV — forward
pricing — so the exact price isn't known when the order is placed.
</details>

**4. Why is buying a fund just before a distribution unwise in a taxable account?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The investor receives a distribution that is economically a return of part of the
purchase price, and the share price drops by the distribution amount — but the
distribution is taxable. They pay tax with no economic gain.
</details>

**5. When does Class A generally beat Class C?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Over long holding periods. Class A charges a front-end load once and lower
ongoing fees; Class C charges little up front but higher 12b-1 fees every year.
Given enough years, Class C's recurring drag exceeds Class A's initial load.
</details>

**6. Why must reinvested distributions be tracked?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They're taxable in the year distributed and they increase the investor's basis.
Failing to add them to basis means reporting an inflated gain at sale and paying
tax twice on the same amount.
</details>
