# 5.1 — Life Insurance

## Overview

Life insurance transfers the financial consequence of a premature death. The key
word is *financial* — the question is never what a life is worth, but what income
and services would need replacing if the insured died tomorrow.

Two decisions structure everything: **how much** coverage, and **what type**. The
first is an analytical question with a defensible answer. The second depends on
how long the need lasts and whether the client has a use for the savings element
that permanent policies carry.

## Types of Life Insurance

### Term life insurance

Pure death protection for a specified period, with no cash value.

- **Level term** — premium and death benefit stay constant for the term
  (10, 20, 30 years). The most common form.
- **Annual renewable term** — renews yearly at an increasing premium
- **Decreasing term** — death benefit declines over time; sometimes paired with a
  mortgage

Characteristics: lowest cost per dollar of death benefit, no cash value, and
coverage that ends when the term does. Most term policies are **convertible** to
permanent coverage without new evidence of insurability — a genuinely valuable
feature, since it preserves the client's ability to obtain permanent coverage if
their health deteriorates.

Term suits **temporary needs**: replacing income during working years, covering a
mortgage, protecting until children are independent. Most needs are temporary,
which is why term is the right answer for most clients.

### Whole life insurance

Permanent coverage with a guaranteed death benefit, guaranteed level premium, and
guaranteed minimum cash value growth.

- Premiums are level and substantially higher than term at the same face amount
- Cash value accumulates on a guaranteed schedule and grows tax-deferred
- Participating policies may pay **dividends** (a return of excess premium, not
  taxable as income to the extent they don't exceed premiums paid)
- The insurer controls the investment; the client bears little investment risk

The premium is level because early-year premiums exceed the true cost of
insurance, and the excess funds later years when mortality cost rises. That
overpayment is what builds cash value.

### Universal life insurance

Permanent coverage with **flexible premiums** and an adjustable death benefit.

- Premiums are credited to a cash value account, from which the insurer deducts
  the cost of insurance and expenses
- Cash value earns a declared interest rate subject to a guaranteed minimum
- The client can vary premium payments within limits, and adjust the death benefit
- Transparency is a genuine advantage — the mortality and expense charges are
  disclosed separately

The corresponding risk: flexibility permits underfunding. If the cash value is
insufficient to cover ongoing charges, the policy can **lapse**, often decades
after purchase and at exactly the age when replacing coverage is expensive or
impossible. UL policies require periodic review of in-force illustrations, which
clients rarely do on their own.

### Variable life insurance

Permanent coverage where cash value is invested in **separate accounts** —
subaccounts resembling mutual funds — chosen by the policyowner.

- The policyowner bears the investment risk
- Death benefit and cash value fluctuate with investment performance, though a
  minimum death benefit is typically guaranteed
- Because it's a security as well as insurance, sale requires **both** an
  insurance license and a securities registration, and delivery of a prospectus

**Separate accounts** are held apart from the insurer's general account, so they
aren't subject to the insurer's general creditors — the structural reason the
policyowner rather than the insurer bears investment results.

### Variable universal life (VUL)

Combines UL's premium flexibility with variable life's investment choice. The
most flexible and the most complex, carrying both lapse risk and investment risk.
Requires the same dual licensing.

### Comparison

| | Term | Whole life | Universal life | Variable life | VUL |
|---|------|-----------|----------------|---------------|-----|
| Duration | Fixed term | Permanent | Permanent | Permanent | Permanent |
| Cash value | None | Guaranteed | Interest-sensitive | Investment-based | Investment-based |
| Premium | Level for term | Fixed level | **Flexible** | Fixed | **Flexible** |
| Investment risk | N/A | Insurer | Insurer (with floor) | **Policyowner** | **Policyowner** |
| Relative cost | Lowest | Highest fixed | Moderate | Moderate | Moderate |
| Securities license | No | No | No | **Yes** | **Yes** |

## Group Life Insurance

Employer-provided coverage, typically term, often at one or two times salary.

- Frequently free or low-cost to the employee, with little or no underwriting —
  valuable for those with health conditions
- Employer-paid coverage above $50,000 creates **imputed income** taxable to the
  employee
- Coverage generally ends at termination, though conversion rights may exist,
  usually at unfavorable rates

Group coverage is a supplement, not a plan. Because it's tied to employment and
usually insufficient in amount, clients relying solely on it are exposed at
exactly the moment of a job loss.

## Term or Permanent?

The decision turns on the **duration of the need** and whether the client has a
use for the cash value.

**Term generally fits when:**

- The need is temporary — income replacement during working years, a mortgage,
  children's dependency
- Budget is constrained and the priority is adequate coverage amount
- The client has other, more tax-efficient savings vehicles available

**Permanent may fit when:**

- The need is genuinely permanent — a special needs dependent, estate liquidity,
  a funded buy-sell agreement
- The client has already maxed out tax-advantaged retirement accounts
- Estate planning calls for a policy owned by an irrevocable trust
- The client will not save reliably without a forced structure

The frequent error is buying permanent coverage on a limited budget, producing a
death benefit far below the actual need. **Adequate coverage amount matters more
than policy type.** A client needing $1,000,000 is far better served by
$1,000,000 of term than $250,000 of whole life.

## Policy Provisions

| Provision | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| **Ownership / assignment** | The owner controls the policy — beneficiary changes, loans, surrender. Owner, insured, and beneficiary may be three different parties, which matters for estate taxation |
| **Beneficiary designation** | Directs proceeds; supersedes the will. Primary and contingent beneficiaries should both be named and kept current |
| **Grace period** | Typically 31 days after a missed premium before lapse |
| **Reinstatement** | Restores a lapsed policy within a stated period, subject to evidence of insurability and back premiums |
| **Incontestability** | After a stated period (commonly two years), the insurer cannot contest the policy for misstatements on the application — except typically for fraud |
| **Suicide clause** | Excludes suicide within a stated period (commonly two years); premiums are usually refunded |
| **Misstatement of age or sex** | Benefit adjusted to what the premium would have purchased at the correct age |
| **Policy loan** | The owner may borrow against cash value; interest accrues, and an unpaid loan reduces the death benefit |
| **Nonforfeiture options** | On surrender: cash, reduced paid-up insurance, or extended term |
| **Settlement options** | How proceeds are paid: lump sum, interest only, fixed period, fixed amount, or life income |
| **Waiver of premium** | Rider waiving premiums if the insured becomes disabled |
| **Accelerated death benefit** | Access to part of the death benefit on terminal or chronic illness |

### Viatical and life settlements

Selling an existing policy to a third party for more than its cash surrender
value but less than the death benefit. **Viatical** settlements involve a
terminally ill insured; **life settlements** involve an insured who is older but
not terminally ill.

The buyer becomes owner and beneficiary, pays remaining premiums, and collects at
death. Viatical proceeds may be received income-tax-free for a terminally ill
insured meeting statutory requirements. These transactions warrant careful
scrutiny — pricing varies widely and the client surrenders coverage permanently.

### Policy dividends

Paid on participating policies, generally treated as a **return of premium**
rather than taxable income, so long as cumulative dividends don't exceed
cumulative premiums paid. Common options: take in cash, reduce premiums,
accumulate at interest, purchase paid-up additions, or purchase one-year term.

Paid-up additions are usually the most efficient choice for a client who doesn't
need the cash, since they increase both death benefit and cash value.

## Taxation Essentials

- **Death benefits** are generally received income-tax-free by the beneficiary
- **Cash value growth** is tax-deferred while inside the policy
- **Policy loans** are generally not taxable while the policy remains in force —
  but if the policy lapses or is surrendered with a loan outstanding, gain becomes
  taxable, sometimes unexpectedly and substantially
- **Surrender** produces taxable ordinary income to the extent proceeds exceed
  basis (premiums paid)
- **Estate inclusion**: proceeds are included in the insured's gross estate if the
  insured held *incidents of ownership* at death. Ownership by an irrevocable
  life insurance trust (ILIT) is the standard technique for avoiding this
- **Transfer-for-value rule**: transferring a policy for valuable consideration can
  make death proceeds taxable, subject to exceptions

## How Much Insurance Is Needed?

### Human life value (income-based) method

Estimates the present value of the insured's future earnings net of
self-consumption and taxes.

Steps: project remaining working years, estimate annual income, subtract taxes
and personal consumption, then discount the remainder to present value at an
assumed rate.

Simple and useful for a quick estimate, but it ignores existing resources,
specific obligations, and non-income contributions — so it can substantially
over- or under-state the need.

### Needs-based method

The preferred approach. Total the actual financial needs at death, subtract
existing resources, and insure the difference.

**Needs typically include:**

| Category | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| Final expenses | Funeral, medical, administration costs |
| Debt repayment | Mortgage, consumer debt, loans |
| Income replacement | Present value of support for dependents, over the years needed |
| Education funding | Children's expected costs |
| Emergency reserve | For the surviving household |
| Special needs | Care for a dependent with a disability |
| Estate liquidity | Taxes and settlement costs, where applicable |

**Resources typically include** existing life insurance (individual and group),
liquid and invested assets, retirement accounts, Social Security survivor
benefits, and the surviving spouse's earning capacity.

> **Need = Total needs − Available resources**

### Worked example

A client, age 40, married with two children aged 8 and 10.

**Needs:**

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Final expenses | $20,000 |
| Mortgage payoff | $285,000 |
| Other debt | $32,000 |
| Income replacement — $60,000/yr for 18 years, PV at 4% real | $759,557.82 |
| Education — two children, $120,000 each | $240,000 |
| Emergency reserve | $30,000 |
| **Total needs** | **$1,366,557.82** |

**Resources:**

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Existing group life (2× salary) | $190,000 |
| Individual policy | $250,000 |
| Investment assets available | $145,000 |
| **Total resources** | **$585,000** |

> **Additional coverage needed = $1,366,557.82 − $585,000 = $781,557.82**

Round to **$800,000**, most sensibly as level term matching the dependency period
(rounding up rather than down, since the estimate omits inflation on several
components)
— roughly 20 years, until the younger child completes education and the mortgage
is substantially retired.

Note how the analysis drives the type: the need is clearly temporary, so term is
indicated, and the required amount would be unaffordable as permanent coverage
for most households at this income.

Note also what's excluded here — Social Security survivor benefits, which would
reduce the income replacement need materially for a family with minor children.
Including them is more accurate; omitting them is conservative. Either is
defensible if disclosed.

## Underwriting

Insurers classify applicants by expected mortality: preferred plus, preferred,
standard, and substandard (rated), with separate tobacco classifications. Factors
include age, health history, family history, build, tobacco use, occupation,
avocations (aviation, diving, climbing), driving record, and foreign travel.

Underwriting requires an application, often a paramedical exam and lab work, an
attending physician statement where indicated, and a **MIB** (Medical Information
Bureau) check.

The practical implication: health changes make coverage more expensive or
unobtainable. Buying coverage while healthy, and preserving convertibility on
term policies, are both worth emphasizing.

## Common Pitfalls

- Choosing policy type before determining the required amount
- Buying permanent coverage on a budget that yields inadequate death benefit
- Relying solely on group coverage, which ends at termination
- Stale beneficiary designations — a former spouse still named after divorce
- Naming a minor child directly as beneficiary, forcing guardianship proceedings
- Letting a UL policy underfund and lapse without reviewing in-force illustrations
- Surrendering a policy with an outstanding loan, triggering unexpected taxable gain
- The insured owning the policy when estate inclusion is a concern

## Self-Check

**1. Why does the duration of the need drive the term-vs-permanent decision?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Term is cheapest per dollar of death benefit but expires; permanent costs more
but lasts for life. A temporary need — income replacement during working years, a
mortgage, children's dependency — is efficiently covered by term. A genuinely
permanent need, such as a special needs dependent or estate liquidity, requires
coverage that won't expire.
</details>

**2. What's the main risk of universal life's premium flexibility?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Underfunding. If the cash value can't cover the ongoing cost of insurance and
expenses, the policy lapses — often decades later, when the insured is old and
replacing coverage is costly or impossible. Periodic in-force illustration
reviews are needed to catch it.
</details>

**3. State the needs-based formula and why it's preferred over human life value.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Need = total financial needs at death − available resources. It's preferred
because it reflects the client's actual obligations and existing assets, rather
than deriving an amount from income alone, which ignores both what the family
actually needs and what they already have.
</details>

**4. When are death benefits included in the insured's gross estate, and what's
the standard fix?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
When the insured possessed incidents of ownership at death — the right to change
the beneficiary, borrow against the policy, or surrender it. The standard
technique is ownership by an irrevocable life insurance trust, so the insured
holds no incidents of ownership.
</details>

**5. Why is convertibility on a term policy valuable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It allows conversion to permanent coverage without new evidence of insurability.
If the insured's health deteriorates during the term, they retain access to
permanent coverage they could no longer qualify for on the open market.
</details>
