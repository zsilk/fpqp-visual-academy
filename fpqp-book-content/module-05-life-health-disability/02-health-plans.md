# 5.2 — Health Care Plans and Health Savings Accounts

## Overview

Health insurance addresses a risk that is simultaneously high-frequency (routine
care) and potentially catastrophic (serious illness). That dual character is why
health plans look different from other insurance: they combine cost-sharing for
predictable expenses with true catastrophic protection through an out-of-pocket
maximum.

For planning purposes the essential skills are reading a plan's cost-sharing
structure, understanding the trade-off between premium and out-of-pocket
exposure, and knowing the tax treatment of the account types that pair with these
plans — particularly the HSA, which is the most tax-advantaged account in the
entire code.

## Cost-Sharing Mechanics

Five terms define what a plan actually costs:

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Premium** | Paid regardless of whether care is used |
| **Deductible** | Amount paid out of pocket before the plan begins paying |
| **Copayment** | Flat dollar amount per service (e.g., $30 per office visit) |
| **Coinsurance** | Percentage split after the deductible (e.g., plan 80% / insured 20%) |
| **Out-of-pocket maximum** | Annual cap on the insured's total cost-sharing; the plan pays 100% thereafter |

The **out-of-pocket maximum is the most important number** in a health plan,
because it defines the worst case. A plan with a high deductible but a modest
out-of-pocket maximum may protect better than one with a low deductible and a
high maximum.

### Worked example

A plan with a $3,000 deductible, 20% coinsurance, and a $7,000 out-of-pocket
maximum. The insured incurs $60,000 of covered in-network charges.

- Insured pays the deductible: **$3,000**
- Remaining charges: $60,000 − $3,000 = $57,000
- Insured's 20% coinsurance would be $11,400 — but the out-of-pocket maximum caps
  total cost-sharing at $7,000
- Insured has already paid $3,000, so coinsurance is capped at **$4,000**
- **Total insured cost: $7,000.** The plan pays $53,000

Premiums are paid on top of this and don't count toward the out-of-pocket
maximum.

## Plan Types

| Type | Network | Primary care referral | Out-of-network | Relative cost |
|------|---------|----------------------|----------------|---------------|
| **HMO** | Restricted | Usually required | Generally not covered except emergencies | Lowest |
| **PPO** | Preferred network, out-of-network allowed | Not required | Covered at reduced benefit | Higher |
| **EPO** | Restricted | Usually not required | Generally not covered | Moderate |
| **POS** | Hybrid | Usually required | Covered at reduced benefit | Moderate |
| **HDHP** | Varies | Varies | Varies | Lower premium, higher deductible |

The trade-off is consistent: tighter network restrictions and more gatekeeping
buy lower premiums. The right choice depends on whether the client has
established physicians they want to keep, and on their expected utilization.

### High-deductible health plans

An HDHP has a deductible above a statutory minimum and an out-of-pocket maximum
below a statutory ceiling, both indexed annually. Its defining feature for
planning purposes is that it's the **only** plan type that permits HSA
contributions.

HDHPs suit clients who are relatively healthy, have cash flow to absorb the
deductible, and can fund an HSA — the tax benefits frequently outweigh the higher
deductible. They suit poorly clients with chronic conditions and predictable high
utilization, or those without liquidity to cover the deductible.

## Health Savings Accounts

The HSA is the most tax-favored account available, and the reason is its **triple
tax advantage**:

1. **Contributions are deductible** (or pre-tax through payroll)
2. **Growth is tax-free**
3. **Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free**

No other account offers all three. A traditional IRA gives deduction and deferral
but taxes withdrawals; a Roth gives tax-free growth and withdrawals but no
deduction. The HSA gives everything.

### Requirements and rules

- Must be enrolled in a qualifying HDHP
- Must not have other disqualifying coverage, and must not be enrolled in Medicare
- Cannot be claimed as another person's dependent
- Annual contribution limits (self-only and family) are set by statute and indexed
- **Catch-up contributions** are permitted beginning at age 55
- Payroll contributions also avoid FICA, an additional benefit
- **No use-it-or-lose-it** — balances roll over indefinitely
- The account is **portable**, staying with the individual across jobs

### Withdrawal treatment

| Purpose | Tax treatment |
|---------|---------------|
| Qualified medical expenses | Tax-free at any age |
| Non-qualified, before 65 | Ordinary income **plus a 20% penalty** |
| Non-qualified, at or after 65 | Ordinary income, **no penalty** |

That last row is why the HSA functions as a supplemental retirement account.
After 65 it behaves like a traditional IRA for non-medical withdrawals, and
remains entirely tax-free for medical ones — and medical expenses in retirement
are substantial and certain.

### The receipt-saving strategy

A sophisticated technique worth knowing: qualified medical expenses can be
reimbursed from an HSA **in any later year**, provided the expense was incurred
after the HSA was established and wasn't otherwise deducted or reimbursed.

A client can therefore pay medical costs out of pocket, keep the receipts, let
the HSA grow invested and untouched for decades, and reimburse themselves
tax-free much later. This maximizes the tax-free compounding window.

It requires disciplined recordkeeping, which is the practical constraint.

## FSAs and HRAs

| | Health FSA | HSA | HRA |
|---|-----------|-----|-----|
| Who funds | Employee (and employer) | Employee, employer, or anyone | **Employer only** |
| HDHP required | No | **Yes** | No |
| Portable | No — forfeited at termination | **Yes** | No |
| Rollover | Limited grace period or small carryover | **Unlimited** | Employer-determined |
| Ownership | Employer | **Individual** | Employer |

**Health FSA**: funded by salary reduction, pre-tax, subject to a "use-it-or-lose-it"
rule softened by either a grace period or a limited carryover, at the employer's
election. The full annual election is available from the start of the plan year,
regardless of contributions made to date — a real advantage for a predictable
large expense.

A **dependent care FSA** is a separate account for childcare and eldercare costs,
with its own limit.

**HRA**: employer-funded and employer-owned, reimbursing qualified expenses under
the employer's terms.

## Other Coverage Issues

### COBRA

Federal continuation coverage after a qualifying event — termination, reduced
hours, divorce, a dependent aging out. Generally available for 18 months (longer
in certain circumstances) from employers above a size threshold.

The catch: the individual pays the **full** premium plus a small administrative
charge, so the cost is typically far higher than the employee's prior share. It's
valuable for continuity and for those with conditions making other coverage
difficult, but marketplace coverage is often cheaper — worth comparing rather
than defaulting to COBRA.

### Marketplace coverage

ACA exchange plans are offered in metal tiers (bronze, silver, gold, platinum)
reflecting actuarial value, with premium tax credits available based on
household income. Plans must cover essential health benefits and cannot deny
coverage or price on pre-existing conditions.

### Medicare

Coverage generally beginning at 65. Covered in detail in Module 7, since it's
integral to retirement planning. The key interaction here: **enrolling in
Medicare ends HSA contribution eligibility**, which requires planning for clients
working past 65 who want to keep funding an HSA.

### Long-term care

Neither health insurance nor Medicare covers extended custodial care in any
meaningful way — a gap clients consistently misjudge. Addressed separately in
[5.4](04-long-term-care.md).

## Choosing a Plan

A workable comparison method:

1. Estimate expected annual utilization based on history and known conditions
2. Compute **total expected cost** = annual premium + expected out-of-pocket, for
   each plan
3. Compute **worst case** = annual premium + out-of-pocket maximum, for each plan
4. Confirm the client's physicians and medications are in network and on formulary
5. For HDHPs, add the HSA tax benefit to the comparison — it frequently reverses
   the ranking
6. Confirm the client has liquidity to cover the deductible if it's high

Steps 3 and 6 are where clients get hurt. A plan can be cheapest in expectation
and still be the wrong choice if the client couldn't fund the deductible in a bad
year.

## Common Pitfalls

- Comparing plans on premium alone, ignoring the out-of-pocket maximum
- Choosing an HDHP without liquidity to fund the deductible
- Not funding an HSA when eligible — forgoing the best tax treatment available
- Over-electing a health FSA and forfeiting the balance
- Assuming Medicare or health insurance covers long-term custodial care
- Defaulting to COBRA without comparing marketplace premiums
- Continuing HSA contributions after Medicare enrollment
- Overlooking that out-of-network care may not count toward the out-of-pocket maximum

## Self-Check

**1. What is the HSA's triple tax advantage?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Contributions are deductible or pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for
qualified medical expenses are tax-free. No other account offers all three.
</details>

**2. A plan has a $2,500 deductible, 20% coinsurance, and a $6,000 out-of-pocket
maximum. The insured incurs $80,000 in covered charges. What do they pay?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
$6,000. They pay the $2,500 deductible, then 20% of the remaining $77,500 would
be $15,500 — but the out-of-pocket maximum caps total cost-sharing at $6,000, so
coinsurance stops at $3,500. Premiums are additional and don't count toward the
maximum.
</details>

**3. Why does the HSA work as a retirement account?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Balances roll over indefinitely and can be invested. After 65, non-qualified
withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income without penalty — the same as a
traditional IRA — while qualified medical withdrawals remain entirely tax-free.
Since retirement medical costs are large and certain, much of it is likely to be
withdrawn tax-free.
</details>

**4. Key differences between an FSA and an HSA?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The HSA requires HDHP enrollment, is individually owned and portable, and rolls
over without limit. The FSA needs no HDHP but is employer-tied, generally
forfeited at termination, and subject to use-it-or-lose-it with only a limited
grace period or carryover.
</details>

**5. Why might COBRA not be the best option after job loss?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The individual pays the full premium plus an administrative charge, with no
employer subsidy, so it often costs far more than the prior payroll deduction.
Marketplace coverage with premium tax credits may cost substantially less,
though COBRA preserves continuity of network and deductible progress.
</details>
