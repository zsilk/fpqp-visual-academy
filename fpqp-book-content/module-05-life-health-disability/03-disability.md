# 5.3 — Disability Income Insurance

## Overview

For most working clients, the largest asset isn't the house or the portfolio —
it's the ability to earn income. A 35-year-old earning $80,000 with thirty
working years ahead controls several million dollars of future earnings, and
that asset is usually uninsured.

Disability is also more probable than death during working years, yet receives a
fraction of the attention. The reason is partly psychological: clients can
picture dying and can't easily picture a long disability, even though the second
is worse in one specific respect — income stops **and** expenses often rise.

## Sources of Coverage

### Government programs

**Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI)** requires an inability to engage in
any substantial gainful activity, due to a condition expected to last at least 12
months or result in death. That is a very strict standard, denials are common,
and there is a five-month waiting period. Benefits are modest.

SSDI should be treated as a backstop, not a plan. A client whose disability
planning consists of "I'll get Social Security" is effectively uninsured.

**Workers' compensation** covers only work-related injury and illness — a large
exclusion, since most disabilities are not work-related. **State disability
programs** exist in a handful of states, typically short-term and capped.

### Group coverage

Employer-provided, often split into short-term and long-term:

- **Short-term disability** — brief elimination period, benefits typically for
  weeks to a few months
- **Long-term disability** — longer elimination period, benefits potentially to
  retirement age

Group coverage is inexpensive and requires little underwriting, but it has real
limitations: benefits are typically capped at 60% of base salary and subject to a
monthly maximum, bonus and commission income is often excluded, the definition of
disability is usually less favorable, coverage ends at termination, and benefits
are **taxable when the employer paid the premium**.

That last point compounds the others. A "60% benefit" that is fully taxable
replaces materially less than 60% of the client's spendable income.

### Individual policies

Purchased directly, portable, medically underwritten, and generally offering
stronger definitions and features. Benefits are **tax-free when the insured paid
the premium with after-tax dollars** — the decisive advantage.

Individual coverage is commonly layered on top of group coverage to close the
gap.

### Age makes a difference

Premiums rise steeply with age and health issues accumulate, so coverage
purchased young is both cheaper and more likely to be obtainable. Occupation
class matters too: insurers rate by occupational risk, and a physically demanding
occupation costs more and may face a less favorable definition.

## Policy Features

### Definition of disability

The single most important provision, and where policies differ most.

| Definition | Standard | Favorability |
|-----------|----------|--------------|
| **Own occupation** | Unable to perform the material duties of *your own* occupation | Most favorable |
| **Modified own occupation** | Own occupation, and not working in another | Moderate |
| **Any occupation** | Unable to perform *any* occupation for which you're reasonably suited by education, training, experience | Least favorable |
| **Split definition** | Own occupation for an initial period (commonly 2–5 years), then any occupation | Common compromise |

The difference is stark for specialized professionals. A surgeon who loses fine
motor control cannot operate but could teach or consult. Under **own occupation**
they collect benefits; under **any occupation** they likely don't. For clients
with specialized, high-earning skills, own occupation coverage is worth its
premium.

**Split definition** policies are the market standard — own occupation for the
first few years, then a stricter test.

### Elimination period

The waiting period between the onset of disability and the first benefit payment
— a deductible measured in time. Common options are 30, 60, 90, 180, or 365 days.

Longer elimination periods lower the premium substantially. The right length is
governed by the client's **emergency reserve**: a client with six months of
expenses saved can accept a 180-day elimination period and capture the premium
savings. A client with no reserve cannot.

This is a clean example of coordinating risk retention with risk transfer — the
reserve from Module 2 directly determines the efficient policy design.

### Benefit period

How long benefits continue once payable: two years, five years, to age 65, or to
age 67 / normal retirement age.

Since the purpose is protecting the income stream that funds retirement, a
benefit period **to retirement age** is generally recommended. A two-year benefit
period addresses a temporary setback but not the catastrophic case, which is the
one insurance should be covering.

### Benefit amount

Insurers limit benefits to roughly **60–70% of gross income**, deliberately. Full
replacement would remove the financial incentive to return to work — a moral
hazard problem — so no insurer offers it.

Because of the tax difference, the *effective* replacement rate depends on who
paid the premium:

- Employer-paid group benefit: taxable, so a 60% benefit may net closer to 45%
- Individually-paid benefit: tax-free, so 60% of gross may approximate the
  client's actual take-home

This is why layering an individual policy over group coverage is a standard
recommendation, and why an employee sometimes should elect to pay group premiums
with after-tax dollars where the plan permits.

### Important riders and provisions

| Feature | Effect |
|---------|--------|
| **Residual / partial disability** | Pays a proportional benefit when the insured can work but at reduced capacity or income. Very valuable — most disabilities aren't total |
| **Own occupation rider** | Upgrades the definition |
| **Cost-of-living adjustment (COLA)** | Indexes benefits during a long claim, protecting purchasing power |
| **Future increase option** | Allows increases without new medical underwriting as income rises |
| **Non-cancelable** | Insurer cannot cancel, change the premium, or alter benefits — strongest guarantee |
| **Guaranteed renewable** | Insurer cannot cancel, but may raise premiums by class |
| **Waiver of premium** | Premiums waived while disabled |
| **Presumptive disability** | Automatic total disability for specified losses (sight, hearing, limbs), often waiving the elimination period |
| **Recurrent disability** | A relapse within a stated period resumes the original claim rather than restarting the elimination period |

**Non-cancelable versus guaranteed renewable** is a frequent exam distinction:
non-cancelable locks the premium; guaranteed renewable locks only the coverage.

## Taxation of Benefits

The rule follows premium dollars:

| Who paid the premium | Benefit taxation |
|---------------------|------------------|
| Employer, as a deductible business expense | **Taxable** to the employee |
| Employee, with after-tax dollars | **Tax-free** |
| Employee, with pre-tax dollars (cafeteria plan) | **Taxable** |
| Split employer/employee | Proportional |

The planning implication: paying premiums with after-tax dollars costs a little
now and produces materially more spendable benefit during a claim — precisely
when the client needs it. Given that a disability claim can last decades, this is
usually the right trade.

## How Much and What Type?

A practical method:

1. Determine **required** monthly income during disability — non-discretionary
   outflows plus any expenses that would *rise*, such as medical costs or in-home
   help
2. Identify existing coverage: group short-term and long-term, and realistically
   discount SSDI given its strict standard
3. Adjust group benefits for **taxes** if employer-paid
4. Compute the gap
5. Set the elimination period to match the emergency reserve
6. Choose a benefit period to retirement age
7. Add residual coverage and, for specialized professionals, own occupation

### Worked example

A client earns $8,000/month gross, $5,900 net. Non-discretionary monthly outflows
are $4,600. Group LTD provides 60% of base salary, employer-paid, with a 90-day
elimination period, to age 65. The client holds a four-month emergency reserve.

- Group benefit: 60% × $8,000 = **$4,800/month gross**
- Employer-paid, so it's taxable. At a 22% effective rate: 4,800 × 0.78 =
  **$3,744 net**
- Required: **$4,600**
- **Gap: $856/month**

Recommendation: an individual policy of roughly $900–$1,000/month, paid with
after-tax dollars so the benefit is tax-free. A 90-day elimination period aligns
with the existing group coverage and is comfortably covered by the four-month
reserve. Include a residual benefit and a future increase option so coverage can
grow with income.

Note that the group benefit alone appeared adequate at 60% and was not — the tax
adjustment is what revealed the gap. Skipping step 3 produces the wrong answer.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming group LTD is sufficient without adjusting for taxation
- Treating SSDI as meaningful coverage given its strict any-occupation standard
- Overlooking that group coverage ends at termination
- Choosing an elimination period unrelated to the emergency reserve
- Selecting a short benefit period, covering the temporary case but not the catastrophic one
- Omitting residual coverage when most disabilities are partial
- Ignoring that bonus and commission income is often excluded from group benefits
- Deferring purchase until health issues make coverage costly or unavailable

## Self-Check

**1. Why is own occupation more favorable than any occupation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Own occupation pays when the insured cannot perform their own occupation's
material duties, even if capable of other work. Any occupation pays only if they
can't perform any job suited to their background. A specialized professional who
loses the ability to practice but could teach collects under own occupation and
likely not under any occupation.
</details>

**2. How does an employer-paid group LTD benefit get taxed, and why does it matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It's taxable income to the employee, because the employer deducted the premium.
It matters because a stated 60% replacement rate nets substantially less — closer
to 45% at typical rates — so the benefit may be well below what the client
actually needs.
</details>

**3. How should the elimination period be chosen?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
By matching it to the emergency reserve. A client with six months of expenses can
take a 180-day elimination period and capture the premium savings. A client
without a reserve needs a shorter one, since they can't self-fund the gap.
</details>

**4. What does a residual disability benefit do, and why does it matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It pays a proportional benefit when the insured can work but at reduced capacity
or income. It matters because most disabilities are partial rather than total —
without it, a client working at reduced capacity may collect nothing.
</details>

**5. Distinguish non-cancelable from guaranteed renewable.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Non-cancelable means the insurer can't cancel the policy, change the premium, or
alter benefits — the strongest guarantee. Guaranteed renewable means the insurer
can't cancel, but may raise premiums for an entire class of insureds.
</details>
