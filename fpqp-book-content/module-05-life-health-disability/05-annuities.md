# 5.5 — Annuities

## Overview

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. In its purest form the client
pays a sum and the insurer promises income for life — which makes it the mirror
image of life insurance:

- **Life insurance** protects against dying **too soon**, leaving dependents unsupported
- **An annuity** protects against living **too long**, outliving your assets

The risk an annuity transfers is **longevity risk**, and it's the one risk in
retirement that cannot be diversified away. No portfolio strategy eliminates the
possibility of living to 100. Only pooling mortality across many people does, and
that pooling is what an insurer provides.

Everything else about annuities — the accumulation features, the tax deferral,
the riders — is secondary to that core function, though it's often what gets
sold.

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Owner** | Holds the contract and controls it |
| **Annuitant** | The measuring life whose lifespan determines payments |
| **Beneficiary** | Receives any death benefit |
| **Accumulation phase** | Period during which value builds |
| **Annuitization** | Converting the accumulated value into an income stream |
| **Payout / distribution phase** | Period during which income is received |
| **Surrender charge** | Fee for early withdrawal, typically declining over a schedule |

Owner and annuitant are frequently the same person but need not be.

## Classification

Annuities are described along three independent dimensions, and combining them
describes any contract.

### 1. When payments begin

**Immediate annuity (SPIA)** — purchased with a single premium; income begins
almost at once, typically within a year. This is the pure longevity-transfer
product, used at retirement to convert assets into guaranteed income.

**Deferred annuity** — value accumulates for a period before income begins. Used
for tax-deferred accumulation, with annuitization optional and in practice often
never elected.

A **deferred income annuity** (longevity annuity) is purchased now with income
starting well in the future — often in the eighties — and is an efficient way to
insure against extreme longevity at relatively low cost.

### 2. How the value grows

**Fixed annuity** — the insurer credits a guaranteed rate. Principal is
protected, subject to the insurer's claims-paying ability. Predictable, and
carries inflation risk over long periods.

**Variable annuity** — the client allocates among separate account subaccounts
resembling mutual funds. The client bears the investment risk; value fluctuates.
Because it's a security, sale requires a securities registration and delivery of
a prospectus.

**Equity-indexed (fixed indexed) annuity** — credits interest based on an
external index's performance, subject to a **floor** (typically 0%, protecting
against loss) and limits on the upside. Those limits take several forms:

| Limiting feature | Effect |
|------------------|--------|
| **Participation rate** | Credits only a stated percentage of the index gain |
| **Cap rate** | Caps the credited return at a maximum |
| **Spread / margin** | Subtracts a stated percentage from the index gain |

Index crediting typically **excludes dividends**, which is a substantial and
frequently undisclosed drag — a meaningful share of long-run equity return comes
from dividends.

The pitch is "market upside with no downside." The accurate framing is limited
upside in exchange for a floor, inside a contract with surrender charges and
complexity. It's neither a scam nor a free lunch, but it is routinely oversold,
and the crediting method must be examined specifically.

### 3. How payments are made

| Payout option | Description | Relative payment |
|---------------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Straight life** | Payments for the annuitant's life; nothing after death | **Highest** |
| **Life with period certain** | Life, but guaranteed for a minimum period (e.g., 10 years) | Lower |
| **Life with refund** | Life, with any unrecovered premium returned to beneficiaries | Lower |
| **Joint and survivor** | Continues over two lives, often reduced after the first death | Lowest |
| **Period certain only** | Fixed number of years, no life contingency | Varies |

Straight life pays most because the insurer's obligation ends at death, with no
guarantee to heirs. Every guarantee added reduces the payment. That trade-off —
higher income versus protection for heirs — is the central annuitization
decision, and it should be made explicitly rather than by default.

## Taxation

### Deferred annuities

- **Non-qualified** contracts are funded with **after-tax** dollars, so the client
  has basis. Growth is **tax-deferred**
- Withdrawals before annuitization are treated as **last-in, first-out** —
  earnings come out **first** and are taxed as **ordinary income**, not capital
  gains, regardless of how the growth was generated
- A **10% penalty** applies to the taxable portion of withdrawals before age 59½,
  subject to exceptions
- On annuitization, each payment splits between a tax-free return of basis and
  taxable earnings, determined by an **exclusion ratio**

> **Exclusion ratio = Investment in the contract ÷ Expected total return**

Once basis is fully recovered, subsequent payments are fully taxable.

The ordinary income treatment matters. Gains that would be long-term capital
gains in a taxable brokerage account become ordinary income inside an annuity —
which can make a low-turnover taxable portfolio more tax-efficient than a
variable annuity for a client in a high bracket.

### Death benefit

Annuity death benefits do **not** receive a step-up in basis. Beneficiaries owe
ordinary income tax on the gain — a meaningful contrast with appreciated
securities held in a taxable account, and an important estate planning point.

### Annuities inside retirement accounts

Placing a tax-deferred annuity inside an IRA or qualified plan is generally
redundant, since the account already provides deferral. Doing so adds annuity
costs without adding a tax benefit. It can be justified only where the annuity's
guarantees or income features are genuinely wanted for their own sake.

## Costs and Surrender Charges

Variable annuities in particular carry layered costs:

- **Mortality and expense (M&E) charge** — for insurance guarantees
- **Administrative fees**
- **Subaccount expenses** — the underlying fund costs
- **Rider charges** — for guaranteed income or death benefits
- **Surrender charges** — for early withdrawal

**Surrender charges** typically start high and decline over a schedule of several
years. During that period the contract is substantially illiquid, which conflicts
directly with liquidity needs. Most contracts allow a limited free withdrawal
annually.

Total costs on a fully-optioned variable annuity can be several times those of a
comparable low-cost portfolio, which must be weighed against the guarantees
purchased.

## Guaranteed Death Benefit on Variable Annuities

Most variable annuities guarantee that beneficiaries receive at least the total
premiums paid (sometimes stepped up to a prior high-water value), even if the
subaccounts have fallen below that.

This is genuine protection with a real cost — the M&E charge funds it. Its value
depends on the client's actual concern about leaving a minimum amount to heirs,
and it should be evaluated against simply holding term insurance, which is often
cheaper for the same objective.

## Annuity Selection

Questions that determine suitability:

1. **What is the objective?** Guaranteed lifetime income argues for an immediate
   or deferred income annuity. Tax-deferred accumulation argues for a deferred
   annuity — but only after 401(k) and IRA capacity is used, since those are
   cheaper and often deductible
2. **Is other tax-advantaged space already maxed out?** If not, use it first
3. **What is the liquidity need?** Surrender schedules make annuities poor
   vehicles for money that may be needed soon
4. **How is the insurer rated?** Guarantees depend entirely on the insurer's
   claims-paying ability, which may need to hold for decades
5. **What are the total costs**, and what specifically do the guarantees buy?
6. **Is the client's time horizon long enough** for deferral to outweigh the
   ordinary income treatment on withdrawal?

### When annuities genuinely fit

- A retiree wanting guaranteed income covering essential expenses, so the
  remaining portfolio can be invested for growth
- A client who has maxed all other tax-advantaged accounts and wants further
  deferral
- Someone specifically worried about outliving assets, especially with family
  longevity
- A client who needs the behavioral discipline of an income stream

### When they usually don't

- Inside an IRA or qualified plan, absent a specific reason
- For a client with unused 401(k) or IRA capacity
- Where liquidity may be needed within the surrender period
- Where a low-cost taxable portfolio would deliver better after-tax results
- Where the client doesn't understand the contract — complexity is itself a risk

## Annuities vs. Life Insurance

| | Life insurance | Annuity |
|---|---------------|---------|
| Risk addressed | Dying too soon | Living too long |
| Underwriting favors | Good health | Poor health can improve payout in some cases |
| Payment direction | Pays **at** death | Pays **until** death |
| Death benefit tax | Generally income-tax-free | Gain taxable as ordinary income |
| Basis step-up | N/A | **No step-up** |

The symmetry is worth holding onto — it's the cleanest way to remember which
product addresses which risk, and it's directly testable.

## Common Pitfalls

- Buying an annuity inside an IRA without a specific justification
- Choosing an annuity before exhausting 401(k) and IRA capacity
- Overlooking surrender charges when liquidity may be needed
- Assuming indexed annuity crediting includes dividends
- Ignoring participation rates, caps, and spreads when evaluating indexed products
- Expecting capital gains treatment on annuity growth
- Assuming beneficiaries receive a step-up in basis
- Defaulting to straight life without considering whether heirs need protection
- Failing to check the insurer's financial strength ratings

## Self-Check

**1. What risk does an annuity transfer, and why can't investing solve it?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Longevity risk — outliving your assets. No investment strategy eliminates the
possibility of a very long life; only pooling mortality across many people does,
which is what an insurer provides.
</details>

**2. Why does straight life pay more than joint and survivor?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The insurer's obligation ends at the single annuitant's death, with no guarantee
to heirs or a second life. Joint and survivor covers two lives, extending the
expected payout period, so each payment must be smaller.
</details>

**3. How are withdrawals from a non-qualified deferred annuity taxed before
annuitization?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Last-in, first-out — earnings come out first and are taxed as ordinary income,
not capital gains. A 10% penalty applies to the taxable portion before age 59½,
subject to exceptions.
</details>

**4. State the exclusion ratio and its purpose.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Investment in the contract ÷ expected total return. It determines what portion of
each annuitized payment is a tax-free return of basis versus taxable earnings.
Once basis is fully recovered, further payments are fully taxable.
</details>

**5. Why is an annuity inside an IRA usually redundant?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The IRA already provides tax deferral, so the annuity's primary tax advantage
adds nothing while its costs — M&E charges, rider fees, surrender schedules —
still apply. It's justifiable only if the client specifically wants the income
guarantees for their own sake.
</details>

**6. Why can an indexed annuity underperform the index it tracks?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Crediting is limited by participation rates, caps, or spreads, and typically
excludes dividends — a substantial portion of long-run equity return. The floor
protecting against loss is paid for by giving up part of the upside.
</details>
