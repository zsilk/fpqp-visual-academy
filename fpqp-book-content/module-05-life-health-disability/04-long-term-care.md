# 5.4 — Long-Term Care Insurance

## Overview

Long-term care means extended assistance with everyday living — help bathing,
dressing, eating, moving — rather than medical treatment aimed at curing
something. That distinction is the entire subject, because it's exactly the
distinction health insurance and Medicare draw when deciding what they'll pay
for. They cover *skilled medical* care. Long-term care is largely **custodial**,
and custodial care is where the coverage gap sits.

Clients almost universally misjudge this. The belief that Medicare covers nursing
home care is one of the most common and most expensive misconceptions in personal
finance.

## Sources of Coverage

### Medicare — very limited

Medicare covers skilled nursing facility care only under narrow conditions:
following a qualifying hospital stay, only when *skilled* care is required, and
only for a limited number of days — with full coverage for an initial period,
substantial daily coinsurance thereafter, and coverage ending entirely well
before a long stay concludes.

Medicare does **not** cover custodial care, which is the overwhelming majority of
long-term care need. It also covers limited home health care, again only where
skilled care is required.

### Medicaid — the payer of last resort

Medicaid does cover long-term custodial care, and pays for a large share of
nursing home care nationally. But it is a **means-tested welfare program**,
requiring the client to have spent down assets and income to state-set limits.

Key features:

- Strict asset and income limits, varying by state
- A **look-back period** (generally five years) during which asset transfers are
  scrutinized; gifts within it can trigger a penalty period of ineligibility
- **Spousal impoverishment** rules allow a community spouse to retain a limited
  share of assets and income
- **Estate recovery**: states must attempt to recover costs from the recipient's
  estate after death
- Facility choice is narrower, since not all accept Medicaid

Relying on Medicaid means impoverishment first. It's a genuine safety net, not a
plan, and any Medicaid planning belongs with an elder law attorney.

### Individual long-term care policies

Private insurance covering custodial care across settings — nursing home,
assisted living, adult day care, and home care.

### Other sources

- **Self-funding** — viable for clients with substantial assets, and the default
  for many. Requires honest arithmetic about multi-year costs
- **Family caregiving** — the largest source of long-term care in practice,
  usually uncompensated and with real costs to the caregiver's own career and
  health
- **Hybrid life/LTC or annuity/LTC products** — see below

## Common Policy Features

### Benefit triggers

What makes benefits payable. Tax-qualified policies use a standard test — the
insured must be either:

1. Unable to perform **at least two of the six activities of daily living
   (ADLs)** for an expected period of at least 90 days, **or**
2. Suffering **severe cognitive impairment** requiring substantial supervision

The six ADLs:

| ADL |
|-----|
| Bathing |
| Dressing |
| Eating |
| Transferring (bed to chair) |
| Toileting |
| Continence |

Memorize these — they're directly testable. The cognitive trigger matters
independently: someone with dementia may be physically capable of every ADL and
still require constant supervision, so without that second trigger a large share
of real need would go uncovered.

### Daily or monthly benefit

The maximum the policy pays per day or per month. Should be set against **local**
care costs, which vary enormously by region and by setting — home care, assisted
living, and skilled nursing differ substantially in price.

Monthly benefit designs are more flexible than daily ones, since they allow
uneven usage within the month.

### Elimination period

A waiting period, commonly 30, 60, 90, or 100 days, during which the insured pays
out of pocket. Functions as a deductible measured in time, and longer periods
lower the premium.

Because the client self-funds this window, the elimination period should be sized
against available liquid assets — the same coordination principle as disability
coverage.

### Benefit period

How long benefits continue: two, three, five years, or lifetime. Lifetime
coverage has become rare and expensive.

A **pool of money** design is now standard: rather than a strict time limit, the
policy provides a total dollar pool (daily benefit × benefit period) that can be
drawn down at any rate. Spending below the daily maximum extends the pool's
duration — more flexible and generally more favorable than a rigid period.

### Inflation protection

The most important optional feature, and the one clients most often decline to
save premium.

Coverage bought at 55 may not be used until 85. Over thirty years, care costs
compound substantially — a benefit adequate today can be badly inadequate by the
time it's claimed. Options include simple inflation adjustment, compound
adjustment (stronger and more expensive), or periodic purchase offers.

For a client buying young, **compound inflation protection is close to
essential**. Without it, the policy may cover a fraction of the eventual cost.

### Other provisions

| Feature | Effect |
|---------|--------|
| **Guaranteed renewable** | Insurer can't cancel, but can raise premiums by class — and LTC insurers have imposed significant increases historically |
| **Waiver of premium** | Premiums waived while receiving benefits |
| **Shared care** | Spouses can access one another's benefit pool |
| **Nonforfeiture** | Some residual benefit if the policy lapses |
| **Care coordination** | Insurer-provided help arranging services |
| **Return of premium** | Refunds premiums at death, at added cost |

The premium increase risk deserves emphasis. Many older LTC blocks were
underpriced, and insureds have faced substantial rate increases decades in — a
real planning risk, since the increases arrive when the client is retired and
least able to absorb them.

## Tax Treatment

**Tax-qualified policies** meeting statutory requirements receive favorable
treatment:

- Premiums count as deductible medical expenses, subject to age-based limits and
  the AGI threshold for medical deductions
- Benefits are generally received income-tax-free, up to a per-diem limit for
  indemnity-style policies
- Self-employed clients may deduct premiums more favorably
- HSA funds may be used for LTC premiums, up to the same age-based limits

## Hybrid Products

Combination life insurance or annuity contracts with a long-term care rider have
grown as standalone LTC sales have declined.

**Advantages**: they address the "use it or lose it" objection, since unused LTC
benefits pass as a death benefit; premiums are frequently guaranteed, avoiding
the rate increase risk; and single-premium designs suit clients with an existing
lump sum.

**Disadvantages**: more expensive per dollar of LTC benefit, they tie up capital,
and the tax treatment differs from standalone qualified policies.

For clients with an underused existing policy or a lump sum earmarked for
contingencies, hybrids are often a reasonable fit.

## Who Should Consider Coverage?

The standard framing sorts clients into three groups by asset level:

| Assets | Typical approach |
|--------|------------------|
| **Low** | Insurance is unaffordable; Medicaid is the realistic path |
| **Middle** | The group insurance most protects — enough assets to lose, not enough to self-fund |
| **High** | Self-funding is viable; coverage may still be chosen to preserve a legacy or avoid liquidating assets |

Middle-asset clients are the core market, because they face genuine ruin: assets
substantial enough to be consumed entirely, but insufficient to absorb several
years of care.

**Timing.** Premiums rise with age and health, and underwriting declines are
common after conditions develop. Mid-fifties to early sixties is the usual window
— early enough to qualify and price reasonably, late enough to avoid paying
premiums for decades before any realistic need.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming Medicare covers long-term custodial care
- Treating Medicaid as a plan rather than a means-tested last resort
- Declining inflation protection, leaving a benefit inadequate by the time it's used
- Setting the daily benefit without reference to local care costs
- Choosing an elimination period the client can't actually self-fund
- Overlooking the cognitive impairment trigger when assessing coverage
- Failing to warn about the possibility of premium increases on guaranteed renewable policies
- Making asset transfers within the look-back period without elder law counsel
- Waiting until health problems make coverage unobtainable

## Self-Check

**1. Why doesn't Medicare solve the long-term care problem?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Medicare covers skilled nursing care only briefly and only after a qualifying
hospital stay, with substantial coinsurance after an initial period and coverage
ending well before a long stay concludes. It doesn't cover custodial care, which
is most long-term care need.
</details>

**2. State the two standard benefit triggers.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Inability to perform at least two of the six ADLs — bathing, dressing, eating,
transferring, toileting, continence — for an expected period of at least 90 days;
or severe cognitive impairment requiring substantial supervision.
</details>

**3. Why is the cognitive trigger necessary alongside the ADL trigger?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Someone with dementia may remain physically able to perform all six ADLs while
needing constant supervision for safety. Without a separate cognitive trigger, a
large share of genuine long-term care need would fail the benefit test.
</details>

**4. Why is inflation protection so important for a client buying at 55?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Coverage bought at 55 may not be claimed until 85 or later. Over thirty years
care costs compound substantially, so a benefit adequate at purchase can cover
only a fraction of actual cost by the time it's needed. Compound inflation
protection preserves the coverage's real value.
</details>

**5. Which asset group most needs LTC insurance, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Middle-asset clients. Low-asset clients can't afford premiums and will rely on
Medicaid; high-asset clients can self-fund. Middle-asset clients have enough to
lose entirely but not enough to absorb several years of care, so they face the
greatest risk of financial ruin.
</details>
