# 7.1 — Retirement Opportunities and Challenges

## Overview

Retirement planning has a peculiar difficulty: the client must fund an expense
they cannot precisely size, beginning on a date they may not control, lasting for
a duration nobody can predict. Every input is uncertain, and the cost of
underestimating is borne at the age when correction is least possible.

That structure explains why the discipline emphasizes conservative assumptions,
early starts, and periodic revision. It also explains why the behavioral obstacles
matter as much as the arithmetic — the arithmetic is not hard, and yet
under-saving is close to universal.

## What Are We Saving For?

Clients frequently cannot answer this concretely, which is itself the first
planning problem. "Retirement" covers arrangements that differ enormously in
cost: full cessation of work at 62, phased part-time work into the seventies,
a second career, relocation abroad, or continuing to work indefinitely by choice.

Useful questions to force specificity:

- At what age, and is that a preference or a constraint?
- Full stop, or reduced work?
- Where will you live, and does that change the cost?
- What will you actually *do* — travel and hobbies cost money; some retirements
  cost more than working years, at least initially
- Who else depends on you — a spouse, aging parents, adult children?

### The replacement ratio

A common starting heuristic: retirees need roughly **70–85%** of pre-retirement
income to maintain their standard of living.

The logic behind the discount is that some costs stop — payroll taxes, retirement
contributions, commuting, work clothing, and often a mortgage. Some costs rise,
notably health care and, early on, travel and leisure.

Treat it as a first approximation only. A client who plans extensive travel, or
who carries a mortgage into retirement, or who faces high health costs, can need
100% or more. Building the estimate from **actual projected expenses** is
substantially more reliable than applying a ratio to income, and it's what a real
plan should rest on.

## Why People Delay

The behavioral obstacles, which mirror the general resistance to planning covered
in [1.1](../module-01-financial-planning-process/01-planning-and-goals.md):

- **Present bias** — immediate consumption is concrete; a distant retirement is
  abstract. People discount their future selves steeply
- **Competing priorities** — mortgage, childcare, education funding, and debt all
  make legitimate claims on the same dollars
- **The belief there's time** — true early on, and it stops being true silently
- **Complexity and avoidance** — plan choices and account types feel technical, so
  the decision gets deferred
- **Overreliance on Social Security** — which was designed to supplement, not
  replace, retirement income
- **Underestimating longevity** — planning to average life expectancy means
  roughly half of clients outlive the plan

### The cost of delay

The reason delay is so expensive is compounding, and the effect is stark. Two
clients each save $500/month at 7% until age 65:

| Start age | Years | Total contributed | Value at 65 |
|-----------|-------|-------------------|-------------|
| 25 | 40 | $240,000 | **$1,312,406.70** |
| 35 | 30 | $180,000 | **$609,985.50** |

The earlier saver contributed $60,000 more and ended with roughly **$702,000**
more. Ten years of delay cost more than twice the total additional contributions.

Put differently: the first decade of saving does more work than the last two
combined, because those dollars compound the longest. This calculation is worth
running for a young client, because it is far more persuasive than any general
exhortation to start early.

## Longevity

### Calculating longevity

Life expectancy tables give an average, and averages mislead here in a specific
way. Life expectancy **at 65** is considerably longer than life expectancy at
birth, because someone who has already reached 65 has survived the risks that
lowered the birth figure.

More importantly, half the population exceeds the average by definition. Planning
to average life expectancy produces roughly a coin flip on running out of money —
not an acceptable risk profile for an outcome this consequential.

Standard practice is to plan to **age 90–95 or beyond**, adjusted upward for good
health and family history of longevity. For a couple, the relevant figure is the
**joint** life expectancy — the probability that *at least one* spouse survives to
a given age is considerably higher than for either individually, and the plan must
support the survivor.

### Longevity risk

The risk of outliving assets. Its distinguishing feature is that it **amplifies
every other retirement risk**: a longer retirement means more years of inflation,
more exposure to market volatility, and a higher chance of needing long-term care.

It's also the risk that cannot be diversified away by investing, which is the
argument for guaranteed lifetime income — see
[5.5](../module-05-life-health-disability/05-annuities.md).

## Changing Demographics

Several structural shifts have moved retirement risk onto individuals:

**From defined benefit to defined contribution.** Traditional pensions promised a
lifetime benefit, with the employer bearing investment and longevity risk. They
have largely been replaced by 401(k)-type plans, in which the **employee** bears
investment risk, longevity risk, and the responsibility for deciding how much to
save. This is the single most consequential change in retirement planning, and
it shifted a professionally-managed obligation onto individuals with no training
in it.

**Increasing life expectancy.** Retirements lasting 25–35 years are now common,
against the much shorter periods the system was originally designed around.

**Social Security funding pressure.** A declining ratio of workers to
beneficiaries strains the pay-as-you-go structure. Trustees' projections
anticipate that, absent legislative change, trust fund reserves will be depleted
at some point, after which incoming payroll taxes would cover only a portion of
scheduled benefits. Some legislative adjustment is widely expected. The prudent
planning posture is neither to assume benefits vanish nor to assume they're
untouched — build in conservatism, particularly for younger clients.

**Rising health care costs**, historically inflating faster than general prices,
and the near-absence of coverage for long-term custodial care.

**Longer working lives**, partly by preference and partly by necessity.

## The Retirement Planning Process

Consistent with the seven-step process in
[1.2](../module-01-financial-planning-process/02-seven-step-process.md):

1. Define the retirement the client actually wants — age, lifestyle, location
2. Estimate expenses in **today's dollars**, from projected spending rather than a
   ratio
3. Inflate to the retirement date and **through** retirement
4. Project income from Social Security, pensions, and other sources
5. Compute the gap that assets must fund
6. Determine required capital at retirement
7. Determine the savings rate needed to accumulate it
8. Choose accounts and allocation
9. Monitor and revise

Steps 2 and 3 are where most errors originate. An expense estimate that ignores
inflation, or applies it only up to the retirement date and not through a
30-year retirement, understates the requirement severely — see
[3.3](../module-03-time-value-of-money/03-interest-rate-assumptions.md).

## Common Pitfalls

- Planning to average life expectancy, leaving roughly half of clients short
- Using an individual rather than joint life expectancy for a couple
- Applying a replacement ratio instead of building from projected expenses
- Applying inflation only up to retirement, not through it
- Assuming taxes will necessarily be lower in retirement
- Treating Social Security as the primary source rather than a supplement
- Ignoring health care and long-term care costs
- Deferring the start, which compounding punishes disproportionately

## Self-Check

**1. Why is planning to average life expectancy inadequate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
By definition roughly half of people exceed the average, so it amounts to a coin
flip on outliving the plan. Standard practice is to plan to age 90–95 or beyond,
and to use joint life expectancy for couples since the plan must support the
surviving spouse.
</details>

**2. Two clients save $500/month at 7%; one starts at 25, the other at 35. Explain
the difference at 65.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The earlier saver accumulates about $1,312,407 versus about $609,986 — roughly
$702,000 more from only $60,000 in additional contributions. The earliest dollars
compound the longest, so the first decade of saving contributes more to the final
balance than the last two.
</details>

**3. What did the shift from defined benefit to defined contribution plans change?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It moved investment risk, longevity risk, and the savings decision itself from
the employer to the employee. A professionally managed lifetime promise became an
individual responsibility for people with no training in managing it.
</details>

**4. Why does longevity risk amplify other retirement risks?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A longer retirement means more years exposed to inflation eroding purchasing
power, more market cycles to survive, and a higher probability of needing
long-term care. It multiplies the duration over which every other risk operates.
</details>

**5. What's the limitation of the 70–85% replacement ratio?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It's a rough starting point derived from income rather than actual spending.
Clients with substantial travel plans, a remaining mortgage, or high health costs
may need 100% or more. An estimate built from projected expenses is far more
reliable.
</details>
