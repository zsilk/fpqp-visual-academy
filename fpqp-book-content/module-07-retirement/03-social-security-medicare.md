# 7.3 — Social Security and Medicare

## Overview

Social Security and Medicare form the public layer of retirement provision. Both
are near-universal, both involve consequential decisions with lasting effects,
and both are widely misunderstood by the people relying on them.

Two decisions dominate: **when to claim Social Security**, which permanently sets
the benefit level, and **how to enroll in Medicare on time**, where missing a
deadline creates lifetime penalties.

> **Note on figures.** Benefit amounts, earnings limits, premiums, and thresholds
> are indexed annually. This chapter describes structure and mechanics — verify
> all current figures against the Social Security Administration and Medicare
> before relying on them.

## Social Security Background

### Origins and structure

Established by the Social Security Act of 1935 during the Depression, the program
was designed as social insurance — a floor of protection against poverty in old
age, disability, and the death of a family earner. It was never intended to be
the sole source of retirement income, a point worth making explicitly to clients
who plan as though it were.

The formal name, **OASDI**, captures its scope: **O**ld-**A**ge, **S**urvivors,
and **D**isability **I**nsurance.

### Funding

Funded on a **pay-as-you-go** basis: current workers' payroll taxes fund current
beneficiaries' payments. It is not a system of individual accounts, despite the
common assumption.

**FICA** taxes fund it, split between employee and employer:

| Component | Applies to |
|-----------|-----------|
| Social Security (OASDI) portion | Wages up to an annually indexed **wage base**; no tax above it |
| Medicare (HI) portion | **All** wages, with no cap |
| Additional Medicare tax | Wages above a threshold, employee only |

**Self-employed** individuals pay both halves as self-employment tax, with an
offsetting income tax deduction for the employer-equivalent portion.

The wage base cap is worth understanding: a high earner stops paying the Social
Security portion partway through the year, while the Medicare portion continues
on all earnings.

### Funding outlook

A declining ratio of workers to beneficiaries pressures a pay-as-you-go system.
Trustees' reports project that, without legislative change, trust fund reserves
would be depleted at some point, after which ongoing payroll tax revenue would
cover a substantial but reduced share of scheduled benefits.

The sensible planning posture is in the middle: don't assume benefits vanish, and
don't assume they're untouched. For younger clients, building in some
conservatism is prudent — a sensitivity analysis showing the plan under reduced
benefits is more useful than a single assumption either way.

## Social Security Basics

### Coverage and eligibility

Most employment is covered. Notable exceptions include certain state and local
government employees under alternative systems, some railroad workers under the
Railroad Retirement system, and certain religious groups.

Eligibility is earned through **credits** (formerly "quarters of coverage"). A
worker earns up to **four credits per year** based on earnings, and **40 credits**
— generally ten years of work — confers fully insured status for retirement
benefits. Disability and survivor benefits may require fewer credits, depending
on age.

### Benefits provided

| Benefit | Recipient |
|---------|-----------|
| Retirement | Insured worker |
| Spousal | Spouse of a retired or disabled worker |
| Survivor | Widow(er), dependent children, sometimes dependent parents |
| Disability (SSDI) | Insured worker meeting the strict disability standard |
| Dependent | Certain children of a retired, disabled, or deceased worker |

A **divorced spouse** may claim on an ex-spouse's record if the marriage lasted at
least **10 years**, the claimant is currently unmarried, and both are at least 62.
The ex-spouse's benefit is unaffected and need not consent — a fact many divorced
clients don't know and that can be worth a great deal.

### Benefit calculation

Three steps in principle:

1. Index the worker's lifetime earnings for wage growth
2. Average the **highest 35 years** of indexed earnings to produce the **AIME**
   (Average Indexed Monthly Earnings)
3. Apply a progressive formula with **bend points** to the AIME to produce the
   **PIA** (Primary Insurance Amount) — the benefit at full retirement age

Two consequences matter for planning. Because the formula is **progressive**,
lower earners receive a higher percentage of prior earnings replaced. And because
it uses **35 years**, working fewer than 35 years means zeros are averaged in — so
additional working years replacing a zero or a low year can raise the benefit
meaningfully.

## Claiming Decisions

### Full retirement age

**FRA** depends on birth year, ranging from 66 to **67** for those born in 1960 or
later. The PIA is payable in full at FRA.

### Adjustments for early or delayed claiming

| Claiming age | Effect on benefit |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **62** (earliest) | Permanently **reduced** — roughly 25–30% below PIA depending on FRA |
| Between 62 and FRA | Reduced on a graduated basis |
| **FRA** | 100% of PIA |
| After FRA to **70** | **Delayed retirement credits** increase the benefit by roughly 8% per year |
| After 70 | No further increase — claiming past 70 is simply lost income |

The spread between claiming at 62 and at 70 is substantial — the age-70 benefit
can be roughly **75–77% higher** than the age-62 benefit. Both adjustments are
**permanent**, apart from cost-of-living adjustments applied thereafter.

### How to think about the decision

The naive framing is a breakeven calculation: at what age does delaying overtake
claiming early? That's incomplete, because it treats the decision as an investment
bet rather than as insurance.

The better framing: **delaying purchases longevity insurance.** A larger inflation-adjusted
benefit for life protects against the scenario that most threatens a retiree —
living a long time and depleting assets. The relevant question isn't which choice
wins on average, but which leaves the client better off in the *bad* case.

Factors favoring **delay**:

- Good health and family longevity
- Continued employment, or sufficient assets to bridge the gap
- Being the **higher earner in a couple** — see below
- Concern about outliving assets

Factors favoring **early claiming**:

- Poor health or short life expectancy
- Immediate income need with no alternative
- No spouse who would benefit from a larger survivor benefit
- Being the substantially lower earner in a couple

### Spousal and survivor benefits

**Spousal benefit**: a spouse may receive up to **50% of the worker's PIA** at the
claimant's FRA, reduced if claimed earlier. Notably, the spousal benefit does
**not** grow with delayed retirement credits, so there's no advantage to a spouse
delaying past their own FRA for the spousal benefit specifically.

**Survivor benefit**: a surviving spouse may receive up to **100%** of what the
deceased worker was receiving or entitled to — including any delayed retirement
credits the deceased earned.

That last point drives one of the most valuable planning insights in this
chapter: **the higher earner's decision to delay affects two lifetimes.** Delaying
increases not only their own benefit but the survivor benefit their spouse may
collect for years afterward. For a married couple, the standard recommendation is
that the higher earner delay as long as feasible, while the lower earner may
claim earlier if income is needed.

### Earnings test

If a beneficiary claims **before FRA** and continues working, benefits are
withheld above an annual earnings limit — a partial withholding below FRA, with a
more generous limit in the year FRA is reached.

Two clarifications that clients consistently get wrong:

- The earnings test **ends entirely at FRA** — after FRA, earnings never reduce
  benefits
- Withheld amounts are **not lost**. The benefit is recomputed at FRA to credit
  the withholding, so it's a deferral rather than a forfeiture

### Taxation of benefits

Up to **85%** of Social Security benefits may be included in taxable income,
depending on **provisional income** (roughly AGI + tax-exempt interest + half of
Social Security benefits) measured against thresholds that are **not indexed for
inflation** — so an increasing share of retirees are affected over time.

This creates a planning interaction: additional income from IRA withdrawals or
Roth conversions can push more of the Social Security benefit into taxation,
producing an effective marginal rate higher than the nominal bracket. **Roth**
distributions don't count toward provisional income, which is one of the more
valuable arguments for tax diversification.

### The earnings and benefit statement

The SSA provides an earnings record and benefit estimate through an online
account. Clients should be encouraged to review it — errors in the earnings
record do occur, and correcting them is far easier close to the year in question
than decades later.

## Medicare

Federal health insurance, generally beginning at **65**, and also available to
those receiving SSDI for a qualifying period or with ESRD or ALS.

### The parts

| Part | Covers | Premium |
|------|--------|---------|
| **A — Hospital** | Inpatient hospital, skilled nursing (limited), hospice, some home health | Generally **premium-free** with 40 credits |
| **B — Medical** | Physician services, outpatient care, preventive services, durable equipment | Monthly premium, income-adjusted |
| **C — Medicare Advantage** | Private plan alternative bundling A, B, and usually D | Varies; often network-restricted |
| **D — Prescription drugs** | Outpatient prescriptions | Monthly premium, income-adjusted |

**Medigap** (Medicare Supplement) policies are standardized private plans
covering gaps in Original Medicare — deductibles and coinsurance. Medigap works
with Original Medicare, **not** with Medicare Advantage; a beneficiary chooses one
path or the other.

### Enrollment and penalties

The **Initial Enrollment Period** spans seven months — the three months before the
65th birthday month, that month, and the three months after.

Missing it without qualifying coverage produces **lifetime late enrollment
penalties** for Part B and Part D. This is among the most consequential and most
avoidable errors in retirement planning, because the penalty is permanent.

A **Special Enrollment Period** is available for those covered by an active
employer group plan past 65, allowing deferral without penalty. Important
qualification: **COBRA and retiree coverage do not count** as active employer
coverage for this purpose. Clients on COBRA at 65 who defer Part B incur the
penalty.

### Gaps in Medicare coverage

Clients consistently overestimate what Medicare provides. It does not cover:

- **Long-term custodial care** — the largest gap, addressed in
  [5.4](../module-05-life-health-disability/04-long-term-care.md)
- Most routine dental, vision, and hearing care under Original Medicare
- Care outside the United States, generally

And Original Medicare has **no out-of-pocket maximum**, which is why Medigap or
Medicare Advantage coverage is effectively necessary rather than optional.

### IRMAA

**Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount** raises Part B and Part D premiums for
higher-income beneficiaries, based on modified AGI from a return generally **two
years prior**.

The two-year lookback is a real planning trap: a large Roth conversion or capital
gain at 63 raises Medicare premiums at 65. IRMAA also operates as a **cliff** — a
single dollar over a threshold moves the beneficiary into the next tier entirely,
so managing income near a threshold has outsized value.

### HSA interaction

Enrolling in Medicare **ends HSA contribution eligibility**. Clients working past
65 who want to continue funding an HSA must delay Medicare enrollment, which is
only possible with qualifying active employer coverage. Note also that Social
Security enrollment triggers automatic Part A enrollment, which itself ends HSA
eligibility — an interaction that catches people who claim benefits while still
working.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating the claiming decision as a breakeven bet rather than longevity insurance
- Having the higher earner claim early, permanently reducing the survivor benefit
- Believing benefits withheld under the earnings test are lost
- Assuming the earnings test continues after FRA
- Failing to check the earnings record for errors while they're still correctable
- Missing the Medicare Initial Enrollment Period and incurring lifetime penalties
- Assuming COBRA or retiree coverage permits penalty-free Part B deferral
- Overlooking the two-year IRMAA lookback when planning conversions or asset sales
- Continuing HSA contributions after Medicare enrollment
- Assuming Medicare covers long-term custodial care
- Not knowing a divorced client may claim on a 10-year marriage record

## Self-Check

**1. Why should the higher earner in a couple generally delay claiming?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The survivor benefit equals up to 100% of what the deceased worker was receiving,
including delayed retirement credits. Delaying raises both the worker's own
lifetime benefit and the amount the surviving spouse may collect for years
afterward — the decision affects two lifetimes.
</details>

**2. What happens to benefits withheld under the earnings test?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They aren't lost. The benefit is recomputed at FRA to credit the withheld
amounts, so the effect is a deferral rather than a forfeiture. The earnings test
also stops applying entirely once FRA is reached.
</details>

**3. How is the PIA derived, and why do 35 years matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Lifetime earnings are indexed for wage growth, the highest 35 years are averaged
to produce the AIME, and a progressive bend-point formula converts AIME to PIA.
Working fewer than 35 years means zeros enter the average, so additional years
replacing zeros or low-earning years can raise the benefit.
</details>

**4. Why doesn't COBRA permit penalty-free deferral of Part B?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The Special Enrollment Period requires coverage through **active** employment.
COBRA and retiree coverage are not active employer coverage, so a client relying
on them past 65 who defers Part B incurs a lifetime late enrollment penalty.
</details>

**5. What is IRMAA and why does the lookback matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
An income-related surcharge on Part B and D premiums, based on modified AGI from
generally two years earlier. The lookback means income events at 63 — a Roth
conversion, a property sale — raise premiums at 65. It also operates as a cliff,
so one dollar over a threshold moves the beneficiary to a higher tier entirely.
</details>

**6. How can IRA withdrawals raise the effective tax rate beyond the nominal bracket?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Additional income increases provisional income, which can push more of the Social
Security benefit into taxation — up to 85%. Each additional dollar then triggers
tax on itself plus tax on a portion of the benefit, producing an effective
marginal rate above the stated bracket. Roth distributions avoid this, since they
don't count toward provisional income.
</details>
