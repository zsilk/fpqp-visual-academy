# 7.2 — Sources of Retirement Income

## Overview

Retirement income traditionally rests on three sources — Social Security,
employer plans, and personal savings. As defined benefit pensions have receded,
the weight has shifted decisively toward the second and third, both of which
depend on the individual's own decisions.

This chapter maps the account types. The organizing questions for any retirement
account are the same four every time:

1. **Who can contribute**, and how much?
2. **When is it taxed** — going in, or coming out?
3. **When can it be accessed** without penalty?
4. **When must distributions begin?**

Answer those four and you understand any plan.

> **Note on figures.** Contribution limits, phaseout ranges, and thresholds are
> indexed and change annually. This chapter deliberately describes *structure*
> rather than current dollar amounts — verify all figures against current IRS
> publications before relying on them.

## The Central Tax Distinction

Nearly every retirement account is one of two types:

| | Traditional (pretax) | Roth (after-tax) |
|---|---------------------|------------------|
| Contribution | Deductible or pretax | **Not** deductible |
| Growth | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| Qualified withdrawal | **Taxed** as ordinary income | **Tax-free** |
| Lifetime RMDs | Yes | Roth IRA: **no**. Designated Roth accounts in employer plans: no, under current law |

The decision reduces to a single comparison: **the client's marginal tax rate
now versus in retirement.**

- Expecting a **lower** rate later → traditional is generally better
- Expecting a **higher** rate later → Roth is generally better
- Genuinely uncertain → splitting between both hedges the risk

Roth carries additional advantages that go beyond the rate comparison: no lifetime
RMDs on Roth IRAs, tax-free growth over long horizons, favorable treatment for
heirs, and tax diversification that gives flexibility to manage bracket exposure
in retirement.

## Qualified Plans

Employer plans meeting ERISA and Internal Revenue Code requirements, which
receive favorable tax treatment in exchange for nondiscrimination rules,
reporting, fiduciary standards, and creditor protection.

### Defined benefit plans

Promise a specified **benefit** at retirement, usually via a formula based on
salary and years of service.

- The **employer** bears investment risk and funding responsibility
- Benefits are typically paid as a life annuity
- Actuarial determination of funding requirements
- Generally insured by the PBGC within limits
- Increasingly rare in the private sector

### Cash balance plans

A defined benefit plan expressed as a hypothetical individual account. Each
participant's account receives a pay credit and an interest credit. It *looks*
like a defined contribution plan to the employee but remains legally a defined
benefit plan, with the employer bearing investment risk. Benefits are portable
and easier for employees to understand, which is why many employers converted
traditional pensions to this design.

### Defined contribution plans

Specify the **contribution**, not the benefit. The eventual account balance
depends on contributions and investment performance, and the **employee** bears
investment risk.

**Profit sharing plans** allow discretionary employer contributions, which may
vary year to year — useful for businesses with fluctuating profits.

**401(k) plans** are the dominant employer retirement vehicle:

- Employee **elective deferrals**, pretax or designated Roth
- Employer **matching** contributions are common — an immediate, guaranteed return
  and the reason capturing the full match is close to the highest-priority
  recommendation in personal finance
- Annual deferral limits apply, with **catch-up contributions** permitted from age 50
- Loans may be available, subject to plan terms
- **Automatic enrollment** and auto-escalation are increasingly common, and are a
  direct application of overcoming status quo bias

The **safe harbor** design lets an employer satisfy nondiscrimination testing by
making specified fully-vested contributions — common in small businesses where
testing would otherwise limit owner deferrals.

## Plans for Nonprofit and Government Employers

**403(b) plans** serve public schools, certain nonprofits, and religious
organizations. Functionally similar to a 401(k), with investment options
historically limited to annuities and mutual funds. Some plans permit an
additional catch-up based on years of service.

**457(b) plans** serve state and local governments and certain tax-exempt
organizations. One distinctive and valuable feature: governmental 457(b)
distributions are **not subject to the 10% early withdrawal penalty** upon
separation from service, regardless of age. A participant with both a 403(b) and
a 457(b) may generally contribute the full limit to **each**, effectively
doubling deferral capacity.

## Individual Retirement Accounts

### Traditional IRA

- Requires earned income; a spousal IRA is available for a non-working spouse on a
  joint return
- Deductibility depends on whether the taxpayer (or spouse) is covered by an
  employer plan, and on MAGI phaseouts. **Without** employer plan coverage, the
  deduction is generally unrestricted by income
- Growth is tax-deferred; withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income
- Nondeductible contributions create **basis**, tracked on Form 8606, and are
  recovered tax-free pro rata

### Roth IRA

- Contributions are never deductible; qualified distributions are entirely tax-free
- **Direct contribution eligibility phases out at higher MAGI**
- **No lifetime RMDs** for the original owner — a significant planning advantage
- **Contributions** (not earnings) may be withdrawn at any time tax- and
  penalty-free, since they were already taxed

A **qualified** Roth distribution requires both:

1. The **five-year rule** — five tax years since the first Roth contribution, and
2. A qualifying event — age 59½, death, disability, or first-time home purchase
   (subject to a lifetime cap)

**Roth conversions** move traditional IRA assets to a Roth, with the converted
pretax amount taxable in the conversion year. There is no income limit on
conversions. They are most attractive in low-income years — early retirement
before Social Security and RMDs begin is the classic window.

Note the **pro rata rule**: if the taxpayer holds any pretax IRA balances,
conversions are taxed proportionally across all traditional IRA assets. A
conversion of only nondeductible basis while other pretax IRAs exist is not
possible, which frequently surprises people attempting a backdoor Roth.

## Small Business Retirement Plans

| Plan | Who contributes | Notable features |
|------|----------------|------------------|
| **SEP-IRA** | Employer only | Simple to establish and administer; contributions discretionary; must cover eligible employees at a **uniform percentage** of compensation |
| **SIMPLE IRA** | Employee deferrals **plus** required employer contribution | For employers under a size threshold; employer must either match up to a set percentage or make a nonelective contribution; lower limits than a 401(k) |
| **Solo 401(k)** | Owner as both employee and employer | For owner-only businesses; highest contribution capacity at moderate income |

A trap worth knowing: SIMPLE IRA distributions within the first two years of
participation, if not rolled to another SIMPLE, face a **25%** penalty rather
than the usual 10%.

## Nonqualified Deferred Compensation

Arrangements outside qualified plan rules, typically for executives.

- No contribution limits and no nondiscrimination requirements, so they can be
  offered selectively
- The employer's deduction is deferred until the employee recognizes income
- **The critical risk**: assets remain subject to the employer's general
  creditors. If the employer becomes insolvent, the executive is an unsecured
  creditor and may lose the benefit entirely
- Section 409A imposes strict rules on deferral elections and distribution
  timing, with severe penalties for violations

The creditor exposure is the point to communicate. Concentrating deferred
compensation with the same employer that provides salary and possibly stock
compounds an already concentrated risk.

## The 10% Early Withdrawal Penalty

Distributions before **age 59½** are generally subject to a 10% penalty **in
addition to** ordinary income tax.

Common exceptions — note that some apply only to IRAs and others only to
employer plans:

| Exception | IRA | Employer plan |
|-----------|-----|---------------|
| Death | Yes | Yes |
| Total and permanent disability | Yes | Yes |
| Substantially equal periodic payments (72(t)) | Yes | Yes, after separation |
| Unreimbursed medical expenses above the AGI threshold | Yes | Yes |
| Qualified higher education expenses | **Yes** | No |
| First-time home purchase (lifetime cap) | **Yes** | No |
| Health insurance premiums while unemployed | **Yes** | No |
| Separation from service at age 55 or later | No | **Yes** |
| Qualified domestic relations order (QDRO) | No | **Yes** |
| IRS levy | Yes | Yes |
| Qualified birth or adoption expenses | Yes | Yes |

Two asymmetries are directly testable: **education and first-time home purchase
are IRA-only**, while the **age 55 separation from service** exception is
**employer-plan-only**. A client who rolls a 401(k) to an IRA after separating at
56 loses that exception — a genuine planning trap.

## Required Minimum Distributions

The government defers tax on traditional accounts, and RMDs are how it eventually
collects.

- Apply to traditional IRAs, SEPs, SIMPLEs, and employer plans
- **Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs** for the original owner
- The beginning age has been raised by recent legislation and is scheduled to
  rise further — **verify the current age**, since it depends on birth year
- The first distribution may be deferred to April 1 of the year following the
  required beginning year, but doing so forces **two** distributions into one tax
  year, often a poor choice
- Calculated as prior year-end balance ÷ a life expectancy factor from IRS tables
- A **still-working exception** may defer RMDs from a current employer's plan for a
  non-owner employee; it does not apply to IRAs
- The penalty for failing to take an RMD is substantial, though reduced by recent
  legislation and further reduced if timely corrected

**Inherited accounts** follow different rules. Under current law most non-spouse
beneficiaries must empty the account within **10 years**, which concentrates
taxable income and makes beneficiary planning considerably more important than it
once was. Eligible designated beneficiaries — a surviving spouse, minor child of
the account owner, disabled or chronically ill individuals, and those not more
than 10 years younger — receive more favorable treatment.

## Vesting

Vesting determines when **employer** contributions become non-forfeitable.
Employee deferrals are **always immediately 100% vested** — they're the
employee's own money.

Common schedules for defined contribution plans:

- **Cliff** — 0% until a stated year, then 100% at once
- **Graded** — vesting increases in steps over a period of years

Defined benefit plans use their own permitted schedules, generally longer.
Safe harbor and SIMPLE contributions are immediately vested.

Practical relevance: a client considering a job change should know their vesting
status. Leaving shortly before a cliff can forfeit a substantial sum, and the
timing is sometimes negotiable.

## Choosing Among Accounts

A defensible priority order for most clients:

1. **Employer plan up to the full match** — an immediate guaranteed return
   unavailable anywhere else
2. **High-interest debt** — a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate
3. **HSA if eligible** — the only triple-tax-advantaged account
   ([5.2](../module-05-life-health-disability/02-health-plans.md))
4. **Max employer plan or IRA**, choosing Roth or traditional on the rate comparison
5. **Taxable brokerage**, managed for tax efficiency

Steps 1 and 2 may reverse where debt carries a very high rate, but the match is
rarely worth passing up — it's typically a 50–100% immediate return.

## Common Pitfalls

- Failing to capture the full employer match
- Choosing traditional versus Roth without comparing current and expected future rates
- Rolling an employer plan to an IRA after separating at 55+, forfeiting that penalty exception
- Attempting a backdoor Roth while holding pretax IRA balances, triggering pro rata taxation
- Overlooking that a 403(b) and 457(b) permit separate full contributions
- Taking a SIMPLE IRA distribution within two years and incurring the 25% penalty
- Deferring the first RMD to April 1 and stacking two distributions in one year
- Ignoring creditor risk in nonqualified deferred compensation
- Leaving a job just before a vesting cliff without checking the schedule
- Stale beneficiary designations — they override the will

## Self-Check

**1. What single comparison drives the traditional-versus-Roth decision?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The client's marginal tax rate now versus their expected marginal rate when
withdrawing. A lower expected future rate favors traditional; a higher expected
future rate favors Roth. Uncertainty argues for splitting between both.
</details>

**2. Which penalty exceptions are IRA-only, and which are employer-plan-only?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
IRA-only: qualified higher education expenses, first-time home purchase, and
health insurance premiums while unemployed. Employer-plan-only: separation from
service at 55 or later, and QDROs.
</details>

**3. Why can rolling a 401(k) to an IRA at 56 be a mistake?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The age 55 separation-from-service exception to the 10% penalty applies only to
employer plans. Rolling to an IRA forfeits it, so distributions before 59½ would
face the penalty unless another exception applies.
</details>

**4. State the two requirements for a qualified Roth IRA distribution.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Five tax years since the first Roth contribution, **and** a qualifying event —
age 59½, death, disability, or a first-time home purchase within the lifetime
cap. Both conditions must be satisfied.
</details>

**5. What is the main risk in nonqualified deferred compensation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The deferred amounts remain subject to the employer's general creditors. If the
employer becomes insolvent, the executive is an unsecured creditor and may
receive nothing — an exposure that compounds the concentration already present in
salary and employer stock.
</details>

**6. Why does the pro rata rule complicate a backdoor Roth?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Conversions are taxed proportionally across all traditional IRA balances, not
just the nondeductible contribution being converted. A taxpayer with existing
pretax IRA assets cannot convert only the after-tax basis — part of every
conversion is taxable.
</details>
