# 7.4 — Financial Planning for Retirees

## Overview

The accumulation phase and the distribution phase are different problems, and the
difference is not merely that money flows the other way.

During accumulation, volatility is survivable — a decline is recovered by time
and by continued contributions, which actually buy more shares at lower prices.
During distribution, the client is **selling** into declines to fund living
expenses, which locks in losses and permanently reduces the asset base. The same
average return can produce very different outcomes depending on the *order* in
which returns arrive.

That asymmetry is the organizing idea of this chapter, and most retiree-specific
recommendations follow from it.

## Retirement Pitfalls

### Lump sum distributions

Retirees leaving an employer often face a choice between a lump sum and a
lifetime annuity, or must decide what to do with a plan balance.

**The rollover trap.** A distribution paid directly to the participant from an
employer plan is subject to **mandatory 20% withholding**, and to avoid taxation
the participant must deposit the **full** amount — including the withheld 20%,
made up from other funds — into an IRA within **60 days**. Shortfalls are treated
as taxable distributions, with a 10% penalty if under 59½.

A **direct trustee-to-trustee transfer** avoids all of this. It should be close to
automatic advice: never take the check.

**Lump sum versus annuity.** Considerations on each side:

| Favors lump sum | Favors annuity |
|-----------------|----------------|
| Flexibility and control over timing | Guaranteed lifetime income |
| Ability to leave assets to heirs | Protection against longevity risk |
| Poor health or short life expectancy | Protection against the client's own poor decisions |
| Confidence in managing a portfolio | Employer plan annuities often priced favorably |

A middle path is often best: annuitize enough to cover **essential** expenses, and
invest the remainder for growth and flexibility. This secures the floor while
preserving upside.

Note also the **NUA** (net unrealized appreciation) rule for employer stock held
in a plan — under specific conditions, appreciation can receive long-term capital
gain treatment rather than ordinary income. It's easy to destroy inadvertently by
rolling the stock into an IRA, so it warrants attention before any rollover.

### 401(k) and 403(b) loans

Plan loans look attractive — you pay interest to yourself, there's no credit
check, and the rate is usually modest. The problems are real:

- Borrowed funds are **out of the market**, forgoing growth during the loan period
- Repayment is with **after-tax** dollars, and those dollars are taxed **again**
  on eventual withdrawal from a traditional account
- **Separation from employment** typically accelerates repayment. If unpaid by the
  tax filing deadline, the balance is treated as a taxable distribution, plus a
  10% penalty if under 59½
- Many participants reduce or stop contributions while repaying, compounding the
  loss

The default-on-separation risk is the serious one, because job loss and financial
difficulty tend to arrive together — exactly when repaying the balance is hardest.

### Longevity risk

Covered in [7.1](01-opportunities-challenges.md), and it governs distribution
planning specifically. Responses:

- Plan to **age 90–95 or beyond**, using **joint** life expectancy for couples
- **Delay Social Security**, especially for the higher earner — an
  inflation-adjusted lifetime income increase
- Consider **partial annuitization** to secure essential expenses
- Maintain some equity exposure — a 30-year retirement still needs growth to
  outpace inflation. An all-bond portfolio substitutes inflation risk for market
  risk rather than eliminating risk

### Avoiding large losses early in retirement

**Sequence-of-returns risk** is the technical name for the asymmetry described
above: poor returns in the **first several years** of retirement do
disproportionate damage, because withdrawals are being taken from a shrinking
base. Two retirees with identical average returns over 30 years can end up in
completely different positions depending on whether the bad years came first or
last.

Mitigation strategies:

| Strategy | How it works |
|----------|-------------|
| **Cash reserve / bucket approach** | Hold 1–3 years of expenses in cash so withdrawals during downturns don't force selling depressed assets |
| **Flexible withdrawals** | Reduce discretionary spending in poor years — powerfully effective and often overlooked |
| **Rising equity glide path** | Start retirement more conservative, increasing equity over time, so the vulnerable early years carry less exposure |
| **Guaranteed income floor** | Cover essentials with Social Security and annuities so market declines don't threaten necessities |
| **Guardrails** | Adjust withdrawals up or down when the portfolio crosses predetermined bounds |

### Withdrawal rates

The familiar **4% guideline** — withdraw 4% of the initial portfolio in year one,
then adjust that dollar amount for inflation annually — originated as research
into historically sustainable rates over 30-year retirements.

Its limits deserve attention. It assumes a specific allocation and horizon, uses
historical U.S. returns that may not repeat, ignores fees and taxes, and rigidly
mandates inflation-adjusted withdrawals regardless of market conditions — which
no sensible retiree would actually do.

Treat it as a **starting reference**, not a rule. Longer retirements, higher fees,
or lower expected returns argue for a lower initial rate; flexibility to cut
spending in bad years supports a higher one.

### Withdrawal sequencing

Which accounts to draw first has substantial tax consequences. A common default
order:

1. **Taxable accounts** first — using basis, and benefiting from capital gain
   rates rather than ordinary income
2. **Tax-deferred** accounts next
3. **Roth** accounts last, preserving tax-free growth and leaving the most
   tax-favorable asset to heirs

But mechanical application is frequently suboptimal. Better practice is
**bracket management**: in low-income years — especially the window between
retirement and the start of Social Security and RMDs — deliberately realize income
up to the top of a low bracket, whether through traditional withdrawals or Roth
conversions. This fills brackets that would otherwise go unused and reduces later
RMDs, which might otherwise push the client into higher brackets and IRMAA tiers.

Also weigh the interactions covered in
[7.3](03-social-security-medicare.md): additional income can increase taxation of
Social Security benefits and trigger IRMAA surcharges two years later, so the
effective marginal rate frequently exceeds the nominal bracket.

## Avoiding Fraud

Retirees are disproportionately targeted — they hold accumulated assets, may be
socially isolated, and cognitive decline can impair judgment in ways that are
gradual and hard to self-detect.

Common patterns include unsolicited investment offers promising high returns with
low risk, affinity fraud through religious or community groups, impostor scams
claiming to be the IRS or a government agency, high-pressure annuity and reverse
mortgage sales, and grandparent scams exploiting family concern.

Protective measures worth recommending:

- Verify any adviser's registration and disciplinary history through public
  databases (BrokerCheck and the SEC's IAPD)
- Refuse to act on unsolicited contact, and never under time pressure — urgency
  is the near-universal marker
- Name a **trusted contact** on accounts, which allows a firm to reach someone if
  exploitation is suspected
- Consolidate and simplify accounts, making irregularities easier to spot
- Establish a durable power of attorney **while capacity is clear**
- Treat "guaranteed high returns" as definitionally false — risk and return are
  related, and no exception exists

Where diminished capacity is a concern, the planning response is structural:
simplify holdings, automate what can be automated, and put documents in place
early. Waiting until capacity is in question means the tools that would have
helped can no longer be validly executed.

## Other Retiree Issues

- **Health care before 65** — early retirees need a bridge: COBRA, marketplace
  coverage, or a spouse's plan. This is a frequently underestimated cost that can
  determine whether early retirement is feasible
- **Housing** — downsizing releases equity and reduces costs; the primary
  residence capital gain exclusion may shelter much of the gain. Aging-in-place
  modifications are the alternative
- **Relocation** — state income tax treatment of retirement income and Social
  Security varies considerably, as do property taxes and estate or inheritance
  taxes
- **Estate documents** — wills, powers of attorney, health care directives, and
  beneficiary designations should be reviewed at retirement, which is a natural
  trigger point
- **Continued work** — part-time income reduces portfolio withdrawals in the
  vulnerable early years, addressing sequence risk directly while providing
  structure and engagement

## Common Pitfalls

- Taking a plan distribution as a check rather than a direct transfer, triggering withholding and the 60-day trap
- Rolling employer stock into an IRA and forfeiting NUA treatment
- Treating a plan loan as harmless, then separating from employment with a balance outstanding
- Ignoring sequence risk and drawing from equities during an early downturn
- Applying the 4% guideline as a rule rather than a starting reference
- Withdrawing mechanically by account type without managing brackets
- Missing the low-income window between retirement and RMDs for conversions
- Going entirely to bonds at retirement, substituting inflation risk for market risk
- Deferring powers of attorney until capacity is already in question

## Self-Check

**1. What is sequence-of-returns risk, and why is it specific to the distribution phase?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Poor returns early in retirement do disproportionate damage because withdrawals
are taken from a declining base, permanently reducing the assets available to
recover. During accumulation the same decline is survivable, since contributions
continue and buy more shares at lower prices.
</details>

**2. Why should a retiree use a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A distribution paid to the participant triggers mandatory 20% withholding, and
avoiding taxation requires depositing the full amount — including the withheld
20% from other funds — within 60 days. Any shortfall is a taxable distribution,
with a 10% penalty if under 59½. A direct transfer avoids all of it.
</details>

**3. Why is a 401(k) loan riskier than it looks?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Separation from employment typically accelerates repayment; if unpaid by the tax
filing deadline the balance becomes a taxable distribution plus penalty if under
59½. Job loss and financial hardship arrive together, which is exactly when
repayment is hardest. Borrowed funds are also out of the market, and repayment
dollars are taxed twice in a traditional account.
</details>

**4. What is the low-income window and why does it matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The years between retirement and the start of Social Security and RMDs, when
taxable income is often unusually low. Deliberately realizing income — through
traditional withdrawals or Roth conversions — fills low brackets that would
otherwise go unused and reduces later RMDs that could push the client into higher
brackets and IRMAA tiers.
</details>

**5. Why shouldn't a retiree move entirely to bonds?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A retirement lasting 25–35 years faces substantial cumulative inflation, and an
all-bond portfolio may not outpace it. Eliminating market risk substitutes
purchasing power risk rather than removing risk. Some equity exposure is
generally needed for the portfolio to last.
</details>

**6. Why is urgency a reliable fraud marker?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Legitimate investments don't require immediate decisions, and pressure exists
specifically to prevent verification and consultation. Combined with a promise of
high returns at low risk — a relationship that doesn't exist — it's close to
definitive.
</details>
