# 10.2 — Analysis: Ratios, Gaps, and Findings

This is step 3 of the planning process — evaluating the current course of action
and identifying alternatives. Every calculation traces to a prior module, and
figures carry forward from [10.1](01-client-profile.md).

## Ratio Analysis

Applying [2.2](../module-02-cash-management-debt/02-financial-situation-analysis.md).
Gross monthly income $18,666.67; net $14,516.67; non-discretionary outflows $6,700.

| Ratio | Calculation | Result | Guideline | Assessment |
|-------|-------------|--------|-----------|------------|
| Basic liquidity | $46,000 ÷ $6,700 | **6.87 months** | 3–6 | **Strong** — arguably excess |
| Savings rate | ($2,530 + $600 match) ÷ $18,666.67 | **16.77%** | 10–20% | **Good** |
| Front-end (housing) | $2,680 ÷ $18,666.67 | **14.36%** | ≤ 28% | **Excellent** |
| Back-end (total debt) | $3,650 ÷ $18,666.67 | **19.55%** | ≤ 36% | **Excellent** |
| Consumer debt | $970 ÷ $14,516.67 | **6.68%** | ≤ 20% | **Strong** |
| Debt to assets | $368,000 ÷ $1,237,000 | **29.75%** | Context | Reasonable mid-career |

**Finding 1 — The Whitfields are financially healthy.** Every ratio is within or
better than guideline. This matters for how recommendations are framed: this is
not a family in distress needing correction, but one with resources being
deployed suboptimally.

**Finding 2 — Liquidity slightly exceeds need.** At 6.87 months against a 3–6
month guideline, with two stable incomes, roughly **$5,000–$12,000** is available
for redeployment. Given Dana's mother's situation and Dana's risk aversion, a
reserve at the upper end is defensible — but the excess above six months has a
better use.

**Finding 3 — The credit card balance is anomalous.** $6,000 at 19.9% alongside a
$3,607 monthly surplus makes no economic sense. Retiring it is a **guaranteed
19.9% return**, unmatched by any investment
([2.3](../module-02-cash-management-debt/03-credit-and-debt.md)). Its persistence
suggests a behavioral or awareness issue worth raising directly rather than a
capacity problem.

## Retirement Analysis

Applying [7.1](../module-07-retirement/01-opportunities-challenges.md) and
[3.2](../module-03-time-value-of-money/02-tvm-calculations.md). Real rate =
(1.07 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = **3.8835%**.

### Capital required

Target spending $130,000/year in today's dollars, less $46,000 combined Social
Security, leaves an **$84,000 annual gap** to fund from assets for 30 years.

```
PV of $84,000 for 30 years at 3.8835% = $1,473,300.47  (today's dollars)
Inflated to age 65:  $1,473,300.47 × 1.03²¹ = $2,740,772.87  (nominal)
```

### Capital projected

```
Current retirement assets: $268,000 + $151,000 + $48,000 + $62,000 = $529,000
  FV at 7% over 21 years                              = $2,190,357.50

Monthly contributions: $1,200 + $640 + $600 match     = $2,440
  FV at 7%/12 over 252 months                         = $1,393,184.06

TOTAL PROJECTED                                       = $3,583,541.56
```

> **Surplus = $3,583,541.56 − $2,740,772.87 = $842,768.69**

**Finding 4 — Retirement is comfortably on track, with a projected surplus of
roughly $843,000.**

This reframes the entire case. Three implications:

1. **The Whitfields' goal ordering is backwards, but harmlessly so.** They ranked
   college above retirement out of concern that retirement was at risk. It isn't.
   Showing them this projection is likely to change how they feel about
   redirecting resources toward education.
2. **They need not take more risk than the plan requires**
   ([6.4](../module-06-investments/04-investment-decisions.md)). With a surplus
   this large, the required return is well below 7%, which is directly relevant to
   accommodating Dana's lower risk tolerance. A more conservative allocation for
   her accounts costs the plan very little.
3. **Sensitivity matters.** At a 5% return rather than 7%, the surplus narrows
   substantially. The projection should be presented as a range, not a point
   estimate.

## Education Funding Analysis

Education inflation at 5%, 529 return at 6%, $35,000/year current cost.

### Cost projected

| Child | Years to college | Four-year cost (nominal) |
|-------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| Nia (12) | 6 | **$202,159.29** |
| Theo (9) | 9 | **$234,024.65** |
| | **TOTAL** | **$436,183.94** |

### Current trajectory

Current 529 balance $41,000, contributions $400/month at 6%. Measured at the
7.5-year weighted midpoint of the funding period:

```
$41,000 grown 7.5 years at 6%           = $63,471.37
Remaining need                           = $372,712.57
Required monthly contribution            ≈ $3,289.29
Current monthly contribution             = $400
```

**Finding 5 — Education is the material gap.** Funding 100% of both educations
would require roughly **$3,289/month** against a current $400 — nearly the entire
$3,607 surplus, leaving nothing for anything else.

At a **75% funding target** — a defensible goal, with the remainder covered by
student contributions, work, scholarships, or modest borrowing — the requirement
falls to about **$2,327/month**. Still substantial.

This is a genuine trade-off requiring the Whitfields' input, not a technical
problem with a single answer. The options:

| Option | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| Fund 100% of both | Consumes the surplus entirely; nothing toward other goals |
| Fund ~75% | ~$2,327/month; leaves ~$1,280 for other priorities |
| Fund ~50% | ~$1,365/month; substantial room remaining |
| Redirect the retirement surplus | Reduce retirement contributions modestly, given the $843k cushion |

That last option deserves emphasis. Because retirement is over-funded, the
Whitfields could reduce retirement contributions toward education **without
endangering retirement** — but only down to the level that preserves the full
employer match, which should never be forfeited.

## Insurance Analysis

### Life insurance — needs-based method

Applying [5.1](../module-05-life-health-disability/01-life-insurance.md). Analyzing
Marcus's death, the larger exposure:

**Needs**

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Final expenses | $20,000 |
| Mortgage payoff | $312,000 |
| Other debt (auto, student, cards) | $56,000 |
| Income replacement — $70,000/yr for 18 yrs, PV at 3.8835% | $894,601.46 |
| Education (75% of $436,184) | $327,138 |
| Emergency reserve for survivor | $40,000 |
| **Total needs** | **$1,649,739.46** |

**Resources**

| Item | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Group term life | $250,000 |
| Invested assets (excl. 529, earmarked above) | $547,000 |
| **Total resources** | **$797,000** |

> **Additional coverage needed ≈ $1,649,739.46 − $797,000 = $852,739.46**

**Finding 6 — Marcus is underinsured by roughly $850,000.** Dana's analysis is
directionally similar and somewhat smaller given her lower income; a parallel
calculation indicates roughly **$600,000** of additional need.

Both currently rely **solely on group coverage**, which ends at termination and
would disappear precisely when a family is most vulnerable.

### Disability

Both carry employer-paid group LTD at 60% of base salary. Because the employer
pays the premium, **benefits are taxable**
([5.3](../module-05-life-health-disability/03-disability.md)).

Marcus: 60% × $128,000 = $76,800 gross, roughly **$59,900 after tax** at a 22%
effective rate — against household non-discretionary outflows of $80,400
annually. Dana's continued income would cover the difference, so the household
survives either single disability, but with no margin.

**Finding 7 — Disability coverage is adequate but thin**, and the taxability of
group benefits is not understood by the clients.

### Property and liability

**Finding 8 — Homeowners Coverage A is likely inadequate.** Coverage A is
$480,000 against a $520,000 market value. Since market value includes land,
replacement cost may be lower — but this must be **verified**, not assumed. If
replacement cost exceeds $600,000, the 80% coinsurance requirement
([4.2](../module-04-insurance-property/02-homeowners.md)) would be breached and
partial losses would be proportionally reduced.

**Finding 9 — No umbrella policy.** With $869,000 net worth, two teenage drivers
approaching, and auto limits of only 100/300/50, this is the most cost-effective
gap in the case. A $1–2 million umbrella typically costs a few hundred dollars
annually ([4.3](../module-04-insurance-property/03-auto-umbrella.md)).

## Investment Analysis

**Finding 10 — Concentration in employer stock.** Marcus's 401(k) holds **31%** in
his employer's stock. This is uncompensated unsystematic risk
([6.2](../module-06-investments/02-risk.md)), and it compounds an existing
concentration: his salary, his retirement plan, and this position all depend on
one company's fortunes. A common threshold is to hold no more than 10% in any
single security.

**Finding 11 — No investment policy statement.** Nothing documents target
allocation or rebalancing policy, which is exactly the gap that produces panic
selling in a downturn — a live concern given Dana's history of moving to a stable
value fund after a decline.

**Finding 12 — Asset location is unexamined.** With a taxable brokerage account
alongside tax-deferred and Roth accounts, there is likely unrealized value in
placing tax-inefficient holdings appropriately
([8.6](../module-08-tax/06-tax-planning.md)).

## Estate Analysis

**Finding 13 — No estate documents whatsoever.** With two minor children this is
the most urgent finding in the case, and it is not a tax problem:

- **No will** → intestate succession governs, and **a court selects the guardian**
  for Nia and Theo
- **No durable power of attorney** → incapacity would require conservatorship
  ([9.4](../module-09-estate/04-incapacity-planning.md))
- **No health care proxy or living will**
- **No contingent beneficiaries** → if both died together, assets could pass to
  the estate and through probate
- Naming **minors** as beneficiaries would force guardianship proceedings

Federal estate tax is **not** a concern at this asset level. The issues are
guardianship, incapacity, and administration — which is the usual case, and why
focusing estate conversations on tax misses what most clients actually need.

Note the ordering. The clients' top stated priority — education — ranks fourth.
The critical finding is one they never raised, because clients don't know what
they don't know. Surfacing it is the planner's job.

## Common Pitfalls in Analysis

- Presenting point estimates rather than ranges
- Accepting the client's goal priority without testing it against the analysis
- Analyzing only what the client asked about
- Assuming market value equals replacement cost for homeowners coverage
- Failing to tax-adjust group disability benefits
- Treating a healthy ratio set as meaning the plan is complete

## Self-Check

**1. Why does the retirement surplus change how the case is approached?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It removes the constraint the clients assumed was binding. Retirement is
over-funded by roughly $843,000, so resources can be redirected toward education
without endangering it, and the required return is below 7% — which permits a
more conservative allocation suited to Dana's tolerance.
</details>

**2. Why is the $6,000 credit card balance a behavioral finding rather than a
capacity one?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
With a $3,607 monthly surplus, they could retire it in under two months. Paying
19.9% while holding surplus cash isn't an affordability problem — it points to
mental accounting or simple inattention, so the intervention is a conversation
plus automation, not a budget.
</details>

**3. Why does homeowners Coverage A need verification rather than assumption?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Market value includes land, which can't be destroyed, so it may overstate or
understate replacement cost depending on the market. If replacement cost exceeds
$600,000, the $480,000 limit breaches the 80% coinsurance requirement and partial
losses would be proportionally reduced.
</details>

**4. Why is the estate finding critical despite no estate tax exposure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The issue isn't tax — it's that with no will, a court selects the guardian for
two minor children, and with no powers of attorney, incapacity would require
conservatorship. These are among the highest-consequence, lowest-cost gaps in
personal financial planning.
</details>

**5. Why is 31% in employer stock worse than 31% in an unrelated stock?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It compounds an existing concentration. Marcus's salary, job security, and
retirement plan all depend on the same company, so a single adverse event
threatens income and assets simultaneously. The risk is also unsystematic and
therefore uncompensated — the market doesn't pay for risk that diversification
could remove.
</details>
