# 10.1 — Getting to Know Your Clients: The Whitfield Case

## About This Case

This module works one client situation through the entire planning process,
pulling together every prior module. The Whitfields are an **original fictional
case** constructed for these notes — not the case used in the course materials.
Work both: the skill being built is applying the process to whatever facts you're
handed, and a second independent case is better practice than re-reading one.

All figures are internally consistent and verified. Assumptions are stated
explicitly so you can vary them and see what changes — which is itself the
lesson from [3.3](../module-03-time-value-of-money/03-interest-rate-assumptions.md).

## Meet the Whitfields

**Marcus Whitfield**, 44 — senior software engineer, $128,000 salary. Employer
offers a 401(k) with a 50% match on the first 6% of pay. Stable employment, ten
years with the firm.

**Dana Whitfield**, 42 — nurse practitioner at a nonprofit hospital system,
$96,000 salary. Access to a 403(b) with a 3% nonelective employer contribution.

Married 15 years. Combined gross household income: **$224,000**.

**Children**: Nia, 12 (six years to college) and Theo, 9 (nine years to college).

### Extended family

Dana's mother, 71, is in reasonable health but lives alone three hours away. Dana
anticipates she may need support within the next decade — possibly financial,
possibly a move closer. Marcus's parents are financially independent.

Dana expects a modest inheritance from her mother, likely a home worth roughly
$240,000. **The Whitfields have excluded it from planning**, which is the right
call: an inheritance is not a plan, timing is unknown, and assets may be consumed
by long-term care costs.

## Estimated Taxes and After-Tax Income

Filing jointly. Marginal federal bracket in the **22–24%** range depending on
final deductions; they take the **standard deduction**, as itemized deductions
(mortgage interest plus capped SALT) fall below it.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both matter:

- **Charitable gifts and mortgage interest produce no current tax benefit** —
  relevant to any advice premised on deductibility
  ([8.2](../module-08-tax/02-federal-tax-calculation.md))
- **Bunching** deductions via a donor-advised fund could create a benefit in
  alternating years ([8.6](../module-08-tax/06-tax-planning.md))

After-tax monthly income: **$14,516.67**.

## Risk Tolerance

Both completed a risk questionnaire. Results:

- **Marcus** — moderately aggressive. Comfortable with volatility, checks balances
  infrequently, held through prior downturns without selling
- **Dana** — moderate to conservative. Reports anxiety during market declines, and
  once moved a 403(b) balance to a stable value fund after a drop

**Risk capacity** for both is high: long horizon to retirement, stable dual
incomes in resilient professions, healthy surplus.

The gap between Dana's **tolerance** and the household's **capacity** is a real
planning issue, not a formality
([6.2](../module-06-investments/02-risk.md)). Building purely to capacity risks
Dana abandoning the strategy in a drawdown; building purely to her tolerance
forfeits return the plan may need. Separate accounts with different allocations,
plus a written IPS agreed in advance, is the standard way to reconcile this.

## Stated Goals

From the goal-setting conversation, in the Whitfields' own priority order:

1. **Fund both children's college** — they hope to cover "most" of a four-year
   in-state public education
2. **Retire at 65** (Marcus) with roughly **$130,000/year** in today's dollars
3. **Be prepared to help Dana's mother** if needed
4. Pay off the mortgage before retirement
5. Take an extended family trip within three years (~$15,000)

Note the ordering. The Whitfields rank **college above retirement** — extremely
common, and worth examining rather than simply accepting. Retirement cannot be
borrowed for; education can. Whether that ordering survives the analysis is a
question for [10.2](02-analysis.md).

## Common Pitfalls in Data Gathering

- Accepting stated goals without probing the priority order
- Recording only quantitative data and missing qualitative context — Dana's mother, Dana's loss aversion
- Treating an expected inheritance as a planning asset
- Failing to note titling and beneficiary designations during data gathering
- Omitting savings from the cash flow statement, overstating true surplus
- Not identifying that the client takes the standard deduction before giving deduction-dependent advice

## Self-Check

**1. Why exclude Dana's expected inheritance from the plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Timing is unknown, the amount is uncertain, and assets may be consumed by her
mother's long-term care costs or redirected. Planning around it would overstate
resources and could leave the Whitfields short. If it arrives, it's a surplus —
the safer direction to be wrong.
</details>

**2. Net worth is $869,000. Why doesn't that represent goal-funding capacity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Use assets — residence, vehicles, personal property — total $603,000, about 49%
of total assets, and won't be liquidated to fund goals. Invested assets of
$588,000 are the relevant figure, and $41,000 of that is earmarked for education.
</details>

**3. What's the significance of the risk tolerance difference between spouses?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Household capacity is high but Dana's tolerance is lower. Planning purely to
capacity risks her abandoning the strategy during a decline, converting a paper
loss into a permanent one. Reconciling this — separate allocations, a written
IPS agreed while calm — is a real planning task, not a formality.
</details>

**4. Why does the standard deduction matter for advice?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Their itemized deductions fall below the standard deduction, so mortgage interest
and charitable gifts produce no current tax benefit. Advice premised on
deductibility would be wrong, and bunching via a donor-advised fund becomes the
relevant strategy instead.
</details>

**5. Why question the Whitfields' ranking of college above retirement?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Retirement cannot be financed by borrowing; education can, through student loans,
scholarships, work, and lower-cost institutions. Prioritizing college at the
expense of retirement can leave parents dependent on the same children later —
a worse outcome for everyone. The ordering should be examined explicitly with
them, not simply accepted.
</details>
