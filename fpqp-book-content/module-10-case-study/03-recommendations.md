# 10.3 — Recommendations and Implementation

Steps 4 through 7 of the planning process — developing, presenting, implementing,
and monitoring. Findings carry forward from [10.2](02-analysis.md).

## Prioritization Logic

Twelve findings compete for one $3,607 monthly surplus and the clients' limited
attention. Sequencing follows three principles:

1. **Catastrophic exposures first.** A gap that could destroy the plan outranks
   one that merely reduces efficiency. No guardian named and $850,000 of
   uninsured mortality outrank asset location.
2. **Cost-effectiveness next.** Some fixes cost almost nothing and remove large
   risks — an umbrella policy, beneficiary updates. These come early regardless of
   ranking on other dimensions.
3. **Cash-flow-consuming items last.** Education funding is the clients' top
   priority but requires an ongoing commitment, so it follows the one-time
   protective fixes.

Note that this ordering **differs from the clients' stated priorities**, which
means the presentation must explain *why* — the substance of step 5.

## Recommendations

### Priority 1 — Estate documents (immediate)

**Recommendation.** Engage an estate attorney to prepare, for both spouses: wills
naming a **guardian** for Nia and Theo, durable powers of attorney, health care
proxies, living wills, and HIPAA authorizations. Update all beneficiary
designations to add **contingent** beneficiaries.

**Rationale.** With no wills, a court would select the guardian for two minor
children using its own judgment. This is the highest-consequence gap in the case
and among the cheapest to close.

**Alternatives considered.** A revocable living trust was considered and deferred
— probate avoidance offers limited benefit at this asset level and stage, and the
added cost and funding burden aren't yet justified. Revisit if they acquire
out-of-state property.

**Owner:** Whitfields, with attorney referral · **Cost:** roughly $2,000–$3,500 ·
**Due:** 60 days

> **Scope note.** Drafting these documents is legal practice. The paraplanner's
> role is identifying the need, assembling relevant facts, and coordinating with
> counsel ([1.3](../module-01-financial-planning-process/03-knowledge-domains.md)).

### Priority 2 — Umbrella liability policy (immediate)

**Recommendation.** Purchase a **$2,000,000** umbrella policy and raise auto
limits to the underlying minimum the umbrella requires — likely 250/500/100.

**Rationale.** $869,000 net worth plus substantial future earnings are exposed
behind 100/300/50 auto limits. Nia begins driving in about four years, materially
raising the exposure. Cost is a few hundred dollars annually — the best
risk-reduction-per-dollar available in the case
([4.3](../module-04-insurance-property/03-auto-umbrella.md)).

**Owner:** Whitfields, with their P&C agent · **Cost:** ~$400–$600/yr ·
**Due:** 30 days

### Priority 3 — Life insurance (30–60 days)

**Recommendation.** Purchase individually-owned **level term** policies:

| Insured | Amount | Term |
|---------|--------|------|
| Marcus | $850,000 | 20 years |
| Dana | $600,000 | 20 years |

**Rationale.** The needs analysis identified an $852,739 shortfall for Marcus and
roughly $600,000 for Dana. The need is **temporary** — it declines as the mortgage
amortizes, education is funded, and children become independent — so term is
indicated ([5.1](../module-05-life-health-disability/01-life-insurance.md)). A
20-year term carries them to ages 64 and 62, past the dependency period.

**Alternatives considered.** Permanent insurance was rejected: the need is
temporary, the required death benefit would be unaffordable as permanent
coverage, and the Whitfields have unused tax-advantaged savings capacity that
serves accumulation better. Relying on group coverage alone was rejected because
it terminates with employment.

**Specify:** individually owned and portable; **convertible**, preserving access
to permanent coverage if health changes; waiver of premium rider.

**Owner:** Whitfields, with insurance agent · **Cost:** ~$1,900/yr combined,
subject to underwriting · **Due:** 60 days

### Priority 4 — Retire credit card debt (immediate)

**Recommendation.** Pay the $6,000 balance in full from the excess emergency
reserve, leaving roughly 6.0 months of coverage. Automate full statement-balance
payment going forward.

**Rationale.** A guaranteed 19.9% return. Paying from the reserve excess rather
than the surplus preserves cash flow for ongoing commitments, and the reserve
remains within guideline
([2.3](../module-02-cash-management-debt/03-credit-and-debt.md)).

**Owner:** Whitfields · **Cost:** none — reallocation · **Due:** 30 days

### Priority 5 — Verify and correct homeowners coverage (30 days)

**Recommendation.** Obtain a replacement cost estimate from the carrier. If it
exceeds $600,000, raise Coverage A to at least 80% of replacement cost. Add an
**inflation guard** endorsement and consider **extended replacement cost**. Confirm
whether contents are settled at ACV and add replacement cost on contents if not.

**Rationale.** Coverage A of $480,000 may breach the 80% coinsurance requirement,
which would proportionally reduce **partial** losses — the overwhelming majority
of claims ([4.2](../module-04-insurance-property/02-homeowners.md)).

**Owner:** Whitfields, with P&C agent · **Cost:** modest premium increase ·
**Due:** 30 days

### Priority 6 — Education funding decision (60 days)

**Recommendation.** Target **75% funding** of both educations, directing an
additional **$1,900/month** to the 529 plans (raising the total from $400 to
$2,300).

**Rationale.** Full funding would require ~$3,289/month, consuming the entire
surplus. A 75% target requires ~$2,327/month, with the remainder met by student
contributions, work, scholarships, or modest federal borrowing. This is a
**values decision that belongs to the Whitfields**, not a technical
determination — the planner's role is presenting the trade-off clearly.

**Funding source.** The retirement surplus of ~$843,000 provides room. If the
Whitfields want fuller education funding, Dana's 403(b) deferral could be reduced
— **but never below the level capturing her full employer contribution**, which is
an immediate guaranteed return that should never be forfeited.

**Also confirm:** whether their state offers a tax deduction or credit for 529
contributions, which may argue for a specific plan
([8.6](../module-08-tax/06-tax-planning.md)).

**Owner:** Whitfields · **Cost:** $1,900/mo from surplus · **Due:** 60 days

### Priority 7 — Reduce employer stock concentration (90 days)

**Recommendation.** Reduce employer stock in Marcus's 401(k) from 31% to no more
than **10%**, redeploying to the broad allocation. Execute inside the 401(k),
where rebalancing triggers **no current tax**.

**Rationale.** Uncompensated unsystematic risk, compounded by the fact that
Marcus's salary and job security already depend on the same company
([6.2](../module-06-investments/02-risk.md)).

**One caution.** If the position holds substantial **net unrealized
appreciation**, NUA treatment may be available at separation and could be
forfeited by a later rollover
([7.4](../module-07-retirement/04-planning-for-retirees.md)). Confirm cost basis
with the plan administrator before acting, and consider staging the reduction.

**Owner:** Marcus, with planner support · **Due:** 90 days

### Priority 8 — Investment policy statement and allocation (90 days)

**Recommendation.** Draft an IPS covering objectives, required return, target
allocation with ranges, rebalancing policy, and monitoring. Set **differentiated
allocations**: a growth-oriented allocation in Marcus's accounts, a moderate
allocation in Dana's.

**Rationale.** The retirement surplus means the **required return is well below
7%**, so a moderate allocation for Dana costs the plan little while making the
strategy one she will actually hold through a decline
([6.4](../module-06-investments/04-investment-decisions.md)). Agreeing the
rebalancing rule in advance is the pre-commitment device that prevents the panic
selling her history suggests is a genuine risk.

**Owner:** Planner drafts; both approve · **Due:** 90 days

### Priority 9 — Disability review (120 days)

**Recommendation.** Confirm whether either employer permits paying group LTD
premiums with **after-tax** dollars, which would make benefits **tax-free**.
Consider a small individual supplemental policy for Marcus.

**Rationale.** Employer-paid benefits are taxable, so a stated 60% replaces
materially less ([5.3](../module-05-life-health-disability/03-disability.md)).
Where the plan permits, paying premiums after-tax costs little now and produces
substantially more spendable benefit during a claim.

**Owner:** Whitfields, via HR · **Due:** 120 days

### Priority 10 — Asset location and tax efficiency (ongoing)

**Recommendation.** Place bonds and any tax-inefficient holdings in tax-deferred
accounts; hold broad index funds and ETFs in the taxable brokerage; direct
highest-expected-growth assets to the Roth accounts. Evaluate a **donor-advised
fund** for bunching charitable gifts.

**Rationale.** Location is value available without forecasting
([8.6](../module-08-tax/06-tax-planning.md)). Bunching creates a deduction benefit
they currently forgo entirely as standard-deduction filers.

**Owner:** Planner · **Due:** Next annual review

### Deferred items

- **Long-term care insurance** — appropriate to revisit in their mid-fifties, when
  underwriting and pricing are still favorable but premiums aren't paid for
  decades unnecessarily ([5.4](../module-05-life-health-disability/04-long-term-care.md))
- **Support for Dana's mother** — no action now, but the reserve should stay at
  the upper end of guideline, and Dana should discuss her mother's own documents,
  long-term care exposure, and wishes. That conversation is easier now than later
- **Mortgage prepayment** — at 6.1% this is defensible, but it ranks below
  education funding and insurance. Revisit once education funding is settled
- **Family trip** — fund from the remaining surplus; it's a legitimate goal and
  the plan supports it

## Cash Flow Reallocation

| Use | Monthly |
|-----|---------|
| Additional 529 contributions | $1,900 |
| Life insurance premiums | $158 |
| Umbrella and increased auto limits | $50 |
| Homeowners increase (estimate) | $25 |
| **Committed** | **$2,133** |
| **Remaining surplus** | **$1,474** |

The remaining $1,474 funds the family trip, builds a modest sinking fund for
irregular expenses ([2.4](../module-02-cash-management-debt/04-budgeting.md)), and
leaves genuine flexibility. Deliberately **not** allocating every dollar is a
design choice — a plan with no slack breaks on the first surprise and then gets
abandoned entirely.

## Presenting the Recommendations

Step 5 is where analysis either lands or doesn't. For this case specifically:

**Lead with the good news.** Retirement is on track with a substantial surplus.
This is true, it's what they were most anxious about, and it earns the credibility
to raise harder items.

**Explain the reordering.** They ranked education first; the recommendations put
estate documents and insurance ahead of it. Frame it in terms of their own goals:
*every* goal — including education — depends on both parents being alive, insured,
and having named who would raise their children. Protection isn't competing with
education; it's protecting it.

**Make the education trade-off theirs.** Present the funding levels and their
monthly costs, and let them choose. Presenting a single recommended figure here
would substitute the planner's values for the clients'.

**Address Dana directly.** Her risk tolerance is a documented input, not an
obstacle to manage around. Showing that the plan doesn't require aggressive
returns is likely to reduce her anxiety more than any reassurance would.

**Expect new information.** Objections at this stage frequently surface facts that
never came up in data gathering — which sends you back to step 1. That's the loop
working ([1.2](../module-01-financial-planning-process/02-seven-step-process.md)).

Every action has an **owner** and a **date**. Unassigned actions are where plans
die ([1.2](../module-01-financial-planning-process/02-seven-step-process.md)).

## Monitoring

**Annual review** covering: net worth and cash flow updates, progress against
education and retirement targets, allocation drift and rebalancing, insurance
adequacy as needs change, and beneficiary confirmation.

**Off-cycle triggers**: job change, either parent's health change, Dana's mother
requiring support, an inheritance, a significant market move, a move to another
state, tax law changes, or Nia beginning to drive.

**Sunset items to track**: the life insurance need declines as the mortgage
amortizes and children become independent — coverage may be reduced later. The
20-year terms expire at Marcus 64 and Dana 62; convertibility should be evaluated
before expiry. And the education funding commitment ends, freeing $2,300/month for
retirement or mortgage prepayment — a future cash flow increase worth planning for
**before** it arrives, so it isn't simply absorbed into spending.

## SWOT Summary

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---------|---------|
| **Internal** | Strong ratios; healthy surplus; retirement over-funded; stable dual incomes; HSA in use | No estate documents; underinsured; employer stock concentration; no IPS; spouse risk-tolerance mismatch |
| **External** | Employer match and nonelective contribution; possible state 529 benefit; umbrella available cheaply; still insurable at good rates | Education inflation above general inflation; Dana's mother's potential care needs; market volatility risk to Dana's behavior; possible tax law changes |

## Common Pitfalls in Recommendation and Implementation

- Ordering recommendations by client priority rather than by consequence
- Presenting analysis instead of specific, actionable recommendations
- Making a values decision, like education funding level, on the client's behalf
- Failing to document alternatives considered and why they were rejected
- Leaving actions unassigned or undated
- Allocating every dollar of surplus, leaving no slack
- Reducing retirement contributions below the full employer match
- Selling employer stock without first checking NUA implications
- Treating the plan as complete at implementation rather than monitoring it

## Self-Check

**1. Why do estate documents outrank education funding despite the clients'
stated priorities?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Consequence and cost-effectiveness. With no wills, a court would choose the
guardian for two minor children — an outcome affecting every other goal, fixable
for a few thousand dollars. Education funding is important but is a gradual
shortfall, not a catastrophic exposure. Every goal, education included, depends
on the protective foundation being in place.
</details>

**2. Why is term rather than permanent insurance recommended here?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The need is temporary — it declines as the mortgage amortizes, education is
funded, and the children become independent. Term provides the required
$850,000 and $600,000 affordably; the same amounts as permanent coverage would be
unaffordable and would crowd out education funding. They also have unused
tax-advantaged capacity better suited to accumulation.
</details>

**3. Why pay the credit card from the reserve rather than the monthly surplus?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The reserve is above guideline at 6.87 months, so $6,000 can be drawn while
staying near 6.0 months. This eliminates the 19.9% cost immediately and preserves
the full monthly surplus for ongoing commitments like insurance premiums and 529
contributions.
</details>

**4. Why leave $1,474 of surplus unallocated?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A plan with no slack breaks on the first unplanned expense and then tends to be
abandoned entirely. The remainder funds the family trip goal, a sinking fund for
irregular costs, and genuine flexibility — which makes the committed portions more
likely to survive.
</details>

**5. What must be checked before selling the employer stock?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Whether the position carries substantial net unrealized appreciation. NUA
treatment can allow appreciation to receive long-term capital gain rather than
ordinary income treatment at separation, and it can be forfeited by a later
rollover. Confirm cost basis with the plan administrator and consider staging the
reduction.
</details>

**6. Why plan now for cash flow that frees up in nine years?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
When the education commitment ends, $2,300/month becomes available. Deciding in
advance where it goes — retirement, mortgage prepayment — prevents it from being
silently absorbed into lifestyle spending, which is the default outcome when a
scheduled obligation ends without a plan for the freed cash flow.
</details>
