# 8.4 — Taxation of Employee Benefits and Retirement Income

## Overview

Employee benefits are compensation, and the tax question for each is simply
whether that compensation is currently taxable, excluded, or deferred. Many
benefits are excluded from income entirely, which makes them worth considerably
more than equivalent salary — a point clients routinely miss when evaluating job
offers.

The second half of this chapter covers how retirement income is taxed once it's
being received, including the provisional income mechanics that make Social
Security taxation more complicated than it appears.

> **Note on figures.** Exclusion amounts, limits, and thresholds are indexed
> annually. Verify current figures against IRS publications.

## Group Term Life and Health Insurance

### Group term life insurance

Employer-provided group term life coverage is excluded from the employee's income
**up to $50,000** of coverage.

Coverage **above $50,000** produces **imputed income** — the employee is taxed on
the cost of the excess coverage, determined not by the employer's actual premium
but by an **IRS uniform premium table** (Table I) based on the employee's age. The
imputed amount appears on the W-2 and is subject to income and payroll tax.

Employees may reduce imputed income by paying part of the premium themselves.
Note also that a plan may lose favorable treatment for **key employees** if it
discriminates in their favor.

### Health insurance

**Employer-provided health coverage is excluded from income entirely**, with no
dollar cap. Employer premiums are deductible to the employer and tax-free to the
employee.

This is one of the largest exclusions in the code, and it explains why health
coverage is worth substantially more than equivalent taxable salary. A $15,000
family premium is worth roughly $15,000 tax-free, versus salary that would be
reduced by income and payroll taxes.

Employee-paid premiums through a **cafeteria plan** are made with **pre-tax**
dollars, which also reduces wages subject to FICA — a benefit that pure income
tax deductions don't provide.

## Cafeteria Plans

A **Section 125 cafeteria plan** lets employees choose among taxable cash and
qualified non-taxable benefits, electing benefits with **pre-tax** salary
reduction.

Qualified benefits typically include health, dental, and vision premiums, health
FSAs, dependent care FSAs, HSA contributions, and group term life within limits.

Two structural rules:

- **Irrevocable annual elections**, changeable only on a qualifying life event —
  marriage, divorce, birth, adoption, a change in employment status
- Deferred compensation generally cannot be offered through a cafeteria plan,
  with limited exceptions such as 401(k) deferrals

The pre-tax salary reduction reduces income tax **and** FICA. The minor
trade-off is that lower FICA wages slightly reduce future Social Security
benefits — rarely material, but technically true.

## Flexible Spending Accounts

Covered in [5.2](../module-05-life-health-disability/02-health-plans.md); the tax
mechanics:

- Funded by **pre-tax** salary reduction, reducing income tax and FICA
- **Health FSA**: the **full annual election is available from the start of the
  plan year**, regardless of contributions made to date — a genuine advantage for
  a predictable large expense early in the year
- **Use-it-or-lose-it**, softened by either a grace period or a limited carryover
  at the employer's election, but not both
- **Dependent care FSA** is a separate account with its own limit, and unlike the
  health FSA, reimbursement is limited to amounts actually contributed to date

A planning interaction worth knowing: a dependent care FSA and the child and
dependent care **credit** cannot both be used for the same expenses. Which is
better depends on the taxpayer's bracket — the FSA generally wins at higher
incomes, the credit at lower ones.

## Noncash Fringe Benefits

| Benefit | Tax treatment |
|---------|--------------|
| **Health, dental, vision coverage** | Excluded |
| **Group term life to $50,000** | Excluded; excess imputed |
| **Qualified retirement plan contributions** | Deferred |
| **Educational assistance** | Excluded up to an annual limit |
| **Dependent care assistance** | Excluded up to an annual limit |
| **Qualified transportation** | Excluded up to monthly limits |
| **Adoption assistance** | Excluded up to a limit, with phaseouts |
| **De minimis fringe** | Excluded — small, administratively impractical to account for |
| **Working condition fringe** | Excluded — would be deductible if the employee paid |
| **No-additional-cost service** | Excluded — excess capacity, e.g. standby airline seats |
| **Qualified employee discount** | Excluded within limits |
| **Personal use of a company car** | **Taxable** — imputed income |
| **Most cash and cash-equivalent gifts** | **Taxable**, including gift cards |

The last two are the ones employers get wrong. Cash and gift cards are taxable
compensation regardless of how small or how the employer characterizes them — the
de minimis exclusion does not cover cash equivalents.

## Retirement Income

### Distributions from tax-deferred accounts

Distributions from traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, and similar accounts are taxed as
**ordinary income** — not capital gains, regardless of how the growth was
generated inside the account.

This is a genuine trade-off of tax-deferred accounts that deserves acknowledgment:
long-term capital gains that would have received preferential rates in a taxable
account become ordinary income inside a traditional retirement account. The
deferral and deduction usually outweigh it, but not always — particularly for a
client who would face a 0% capital gains rate.

Where **nondeductible contributions** were made, the taxpayer has **basis**, and
distributions are partially tax-free under a **pro rata** rule applied across all
traditional IRAs. Form 8606 tracks this, and failure to file it results in paying
tax twice on the same money.

### Roth distributions

**Qualified** Roth distributions are entirely **tax-free** — both contributions
and earnings. Requirements are the five-year rule plus a qualifying event
([7.2](../module-07-retirement/02-sources-of-income.md)).

Non-qualified Roth IRA distributions follow **ordering rules**: contributions
first (always tax- and penalty-free), then conversions, then earnings. This
ordering is favorable and means a Roth IRA offers more accessibility than clients
generally realize.

### Pensions and annuitized benefits

Fully taxable where the employee made no after-tax contributions. Where after-tax
contributions exist, the **Simplified Method** determines the tax-free portion of
each payment — conceptually the same exclusion-ratio idea covered in
[8.3](03-property-annuities.md).

## Taxation of Social Security Benefits

The mechanics behind the point raised in
[7.3](../module-07-retirement/03-social-security-medicare.md).

### Provisional income

> **Provisional income = AGI + tax-exempt interest + 50% of Social Security benefits**

Note that **municipal bond interest is added back**, so tax-exempt interest can
still increase the taxable portion of Social Security — a genuine surprise for
retirees who bought munis specifically to reduce taxes.

### The tiers

Provisional income is compared against thresholds that vary by filing status,
producing three tiers:

| Provisional income | Portion of benefits taxable |
|--------------------|----------------------------|
| Below the first threshold | **0%** |
| Between thresholds | Up to **50%** |
| Above the second threshold | Up to **85%** |

The thresholds are **not indexed for inflation**, which is why an increasing share
of retirees are affected each year — a slow, structural change worth mentioning
to clients planning decades ahead.

### The effective marginal rate problem

Because additional income raises provisional income, each extra dollar can trigger
tax on itself **and** on a portion of the Social Security benefit. The result is an
effective marginal rate meaningfully above the nominal bracket — sometimes
described as the "tax torpedo," affecting a band of middle-income retirees.

Planning responses:

- **Roth distributions don't count** toward provisional income — the strongest
  argument for tax diversification
- Manage the **timing** of IRA withdrawals and conversions, using low-income years
- Remember that **municipal interest doesn't help** here, despite being
  federally tax-exempt

## State Taxation of Retirement Benefits

State treatment varies substantially and is a real factor in relocation
decisions:

- Several states impose **no individual income tax** at all
- Many states that do tax income **exempt Social Security benefits** entirely
- Some exempt or partially exempt pension and retirement account distributions,
  sometimes only for public pensions or above a certain age
- Some offer a general retirement income exclusion subject to age or income limits

Also relevant to relocation: **property taxes**, **sales taxes**, and whether the
state imposes its own **estate or inheritance tax** — several do, often with
exclusion amounts far below the federal level.

The practical point is that no single state is best; the answer depends on the
client's income mix. A retiree living on Social Security and a public pension may
fare very differently from one living on IRA withdrawals and capital gains.

Note also that a state generally cannot tax the retirement income of a
**non-resident** earned while working there — so income follows residence, not
where it was earned.

## Common Pitfalls

- Overlooking imputed income on group term life above $50,000
- Treating gift cards as de minimis when they're taxable compensation
- Missing that a health FSA's full election is available immediately, unlike a dependent care FSA
- Using both a dependent care FSA and the dependent care credit for the same expenses
- Failing to file Form 8606, resulting in double taxation of nondeductible basis
- Assuming municipal interest keeps Social Security benefits untaxed — it's added back
- Overlooking the effective marginal rate created by the provisional income tiers
- Assuming a no-income-tax state is cheapest without checking property and sales taxes
- Expecting capital gain rates on traditional retirement account distributions

## Self-Check

**1. How is employer-provided group term life taxed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Coverage up to $50,000 is excluded from income. Above that, the employee has
imputed income based on the IRS uniform premium table (Table I) by age — not the
employer's actual cost — reported on the W-2 and subject to income and payroll
tax.
</details>

**2. Why does municipal bond interest fail to protect Social Security benefits
from taxation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Tax-exempt interest is explicitly added back in computing provisional income. So
while it escapes federal income tax directly, it still increases provisional
income and can push more of the Social Security benefit into taxation.
</details>

**3. What is the "tax torpedo"?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The elevated effective marginal rate arising when additional income both is taxed
itself and pushes more Social Security benefit into taxation. A band of
middle-income retirees can face effective rates well above their nominal bracket.
Roth distributions avoid it since they don't count toward provisional income.
</details>

**4. Why is employer-provided health coverage worth more than equivalent salary?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It's excluded from income entirely with no dollar cap, so the full value is
received tax-free. Equivalent salary would be reduced by both income tax and
payroll tax before the employee could purchase the same coverage.
</details>

**5. What's the consequence of not filing Form 8606 for nondeductible IRA
contributions?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Basis goes untracked, so distributions that should be partially tax-free get
taxed in full — the taxpayer pays tax twice on the same money, once when
contributed and again when withdrawn.
</details>

**6. Why isn't a no-income-tax state automatically best for a retiree?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Such states often have higher property or sales taxes, and many income-tax states
exempt Social Security and some retirement income anyway. Some states also impose
estate or inheritance taxes with low exclusions. The right answer depends on the
client's particular income mix and asset picture.
</details>
