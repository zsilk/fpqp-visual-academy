# 8.5 — Keeping the Proper Records

## Overview

Recordkeeping seems like the dullest topic in the module, and it's the one most
likely to cost a client real money. The burden of proof in a tax dispute rests on
the **taxpayer**, not the IRS. A legitimate deduction without substantiation is,
practically speaking, not a deduction.

The other reason it matters: basis records determine tax owed on assets held for
decades. A client who cannot document what they paid for stock bought in 1998 may
end up paying tax on the entire proceeds rather than only on the gain.

## Why Records Matter

- **Burden of proof.** The taxpayer must substantiate income, deductions, and
  credits claimed
- **Basis determination.** Without records, gain may be computed against a basis
  of zero
- **Statute of limitations.** Generally three years from filing for assessment,
  six years for a substantial omission of income, and **unlimited** for fraud or a
  failure to file — which means some records should never be discarded
- **Amended returns.** Claiming a refund on an amended return generally requires
  filing within three years of the original return or two years of payment,
  whichever is later

## Retention Periods

| Record | Retain |
|--------|--------|
| **Tax returns** | **Permanently** — they establish filing history and support later positions |
| Supporting documents (W-2s, 1099s, receipts) | At least until the statute of limitations expires; longer is safer |
| **Property records** (basis) | **Until the property is sold plus the limitations period** |
| Home purchase, improvement, and sale records | Same — often decades |
| **Retirement account basis** (Form 8606) | **Until the account is fully distributed** |
| Investment purchase confirmations and reinvestment records | Until sold plus the limitations period |
| Business records | At least the limitations period; longer for asset records |
| Insurance policies and claims | While in force, plus a reasonable period |
| Estate and gift tax returns | **Permanently** |

The rule of thumb worth giving clients: **returns forever, supporting records
until the statute expires, and basis records until the asset is gone.**

## Tax Returns

Keep copies permanently. They establish carryforward items — capital loss
carryovers, charitable contribution carryovers, suspended passive losses, and
nondeductible IRA basis — that may not be used for many years.

Retaining returns also matters where a return was **never filed**, since the
limitations period never begins to run.

## Property Records

Basis records are the highest-value records a client keeps, and the ones most
commonly lost.

### Investment property

- Purchase confirmations with date, quantity, price, and commissions
- **Reinvested dividend and capital gain distribution records** — each reinvestment
  is an additional purchase increasing basis (see
  [8.3](03-property-annuities.md))
- Records of stock splits, spin-offs, mergers, and return-of-capital
  distributions, all of which adjust basis
- Records of inherited property valuation, establishing the stepped-up basis
- Records of gifted property, establishing the donor's carryover basis and
  holding period

Brokers report basis to the IRS for **covered securities** — generally those
acquired after specified effective dates by asset type. For **non-covered**
securities acquired earlier, the taxpayer remains responsible, which is precisely
where old holdings create difficulty.

### Personal residence

Even though the Section 121 exclusion shelters gain up to a stated amount, records
still matter — for gains exceeding the exclusion, for a client who doesn't meet
the ownership and use tests, and for property later converted to rental use.

Retain the settlement statement from purchase, records of all **capital
improvements** (a new roof, an addition, a renovation — not routine repairs), and
the settlement statement from sale.

The improvement/repair distinction matters: improvements add to basis, ordinary
repairs do not.

### Business and rental property

Purchase documents, improvement records, and — critically — **depreciation
schedules**. Basis is reduced by depreciation **allowable** whether or not
claimed, so the schedule is needed to compute gain and depreciation recapture
correctly at sale.

## Deduction Records

### Charitable contributions

Substantiation requirements scale with the size and type of gift:

| Gift | Required substantiation |
|------|------------------------|
| Any cash gift | Bank record or written acknowledgment from the charity |
| Cash gift of $250 or more | **Contemporaneous written acknowledgment** from the charity |
| Noncash over $500 | Form 8283, Section A |
| Noncash over $5,000 | **Qualified appraisal** and Form 8283, Section B |

"Contemporaneous" means obtained by the earlier of the filing date or the due
date. An acknowledgment obtained after an audit begins does not satisfy the
requirement — a genuinely harsh rule that costs taxpayers deductions they
legitimately earned.

### Medical expenses

Receipts, statements showing amounts actually paid, insurance reimbursement
records, and mileage for medical travel. Only unreimbursed amounts above the AGI
floor are deductible, so records must establish what insurance did **not** cover.

For clients using the HSA receipt-saving strategy from
[5.2](../module-05-life-health-disability/02-health-plans.md), these records must
be kept indefinitely — reimbursement may occur decades after the expense.

### Business mileage and expenses

Vehicle expense deductions require a **contemporaneous log** recording, for each
business trip: date, destination, business purpose, and miles driven. Total annual
mileage and the business/personal split are also needed.

"Contemporaneous" is the operative word. A log reconstructed from memory during an
audit carries little weight, and vehicle deductions are a recognized audit focus.
The two methods — standard mileage rate or actual expenses — each require the
mileage records; actual expenses additionally require receipts for fuel, repairs,
insurance, and depreciation records.

### Other deductions

Mortgage interest (Form 1098), state and local taxes paid, investment interest and
what it financed, and education expenses (Form 1098-T) with records of what was
actually paid.

## Credit Records

Credits generally require documentation of the qualifying expense and of
eligibility:

- **Child and dependent care** — provider name, address, and taxpayer
  identification number, which the provider must furnish
- **Education credits** — Form 1098-T plus records of amounts actually paid and
  the specific expenses
- **Energy credits** — manufacturer certifications and installation records
- **Adoption credit** — documentation of qualified expenses and finalization

## Mutual Fund Records

Mutual fund holdings deserve specific attention because their basis is
cumulative and easily lost.

- Every purchase, including **each reinvested distribution**
- Annual statements showing distributions and whether reinvested
- Records of exchanges between funds within a family — each exchange is a
  **taxable sale and repurchase**, not a neutral transfer, which surprises many
  investors
- The **basis method elected** — average cost, FIFO, or specific identification.
  Specific identification requires identifying shares **at the time of sale** and
  offers the most control, but only with adequate records

## Practical Guidance

- **Digital copies are acceptable**, provided they're legible and complete —
  scanning eliminates the deterioration and loss that afflict paper
- **Back up** digital records in more than one location; a single hard drive is
  not a records system
- **Organize by tax year**, with a separate permanent file for basis records that
  span many years
- **Download brokerage records before closing an account** — access frequently
  ends when the relationship does, and reconstructing decades of history
  afterward may be impossible
- **Note the reasoning** behind unusual positions while it's fresh; explaining a
  decision three years later is much harder
- Review annually and discard what's beyond its retention period, keeping the
  permanent items

## Common Pitfalls

- Discarding returns after a few years, losing carryforward documentation
- Failing to track reinvested distributions, overstating gain at sale
- Losing basis records for non-covered securities where the broker doesn't report
- Reconstructing a mileage log during an audit rather than keeping it contemporaneously
- Missing the contemporaneous acknowledgment deadline for charitable gifts of $250+
- Not filing Form 8606, losing the record of nondeductible IRA basis
- Discarding home improvement records, inflating gain on eventual sale
- Losing depreciation schedules, making recapture impossible to compute correctly
- Closing a brokerage account without downloading historical records
- Treating within-family fund exchanges as nontaxable

## Self-Check

**1. Why should tax returns be kept permanently?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They document carryforward items — capital loss carryovers, suspended passive
losses, charitable carryovers, nondeductible IRA basis — that may not be used for
years. They also establish filing history, which matters because the limitations
period never begins on an unfiled return.
</details>

**2. Why are basis records the highest-value records a client keeps?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Basis determines the taxable gain. Without documentation, the IRS may treat basis
as zero, taxing the entire proceeds rather than only the gain. The exposure grows
with holding period and appreciation.
</details>

**3. What does "contemporaneous" mean for charitable acknowledgments?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Obtained by the earlier of the return's filing date or its due date. An
acknowledgment secured later — including after an audit begins — doesn't satisfy
the requirement, and the deduction is lost even for a genuine gift.
</details>

**4. What must a business mileage log contain, and why is timing important?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Date, destination, business purpose, and miles for each trip, plus total annual
mileage and the business/personal split. It must be kept contemporaneously — a log
reconstructed from memory during an audit carries little weight, and vehicle
deductions are a known audit focus.
</details>

**5. Why do reinvested mutual fund distributions require tracking?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Each reinvestment is an additional purchase that was already taxed when
distributed, so it increases basis. Failing to track them means reporting an
inflated gain at sale and paying tax twice on the same amounts.
</details>

**6. When does the statute of limitations never expire?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
For fraud or failure to file a return. In those cases the assessment period
never begins to run, which is one reason returns should be retained permanently.
</details>
