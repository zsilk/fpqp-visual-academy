# 8.1 — Types of Taxes and Income

## Overview

Tax is not a standalone planning area. It modifies the outcome of nearly every
recommendation in every other module — which account to fund, which security to
hold where, when to realize a gain, how to structure a business, how to transfer
property at death.

The habit worth building early is to ask of any recommendation: **what is the
after-tax result?** A pretax comparison between two options is frequently the
wrong comparison, and the ranking often reverses once taxes are included.

This chapter establishes the vocabulary: what kinds of taxes exist, and how
different kinds of income are treated.

> **Note on figures.** Rates, brackets, thresholds, and limits change annually
> and with legislation. This chapter describes **structure** rather than current
> amounts — verify all figures against current IRS publications.

## Tax Structures

Three ways a tax can relate to the taxpayer's income:

| Structure | Definition | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **Progressive** | Rate **rises** as the base rises | Federal income tax, federal estate tax |
| **Proportional (flat)** | Rate stays **constant** regardless of base | Many state income taxes; the Medicare payroll portion |
| **Regressive** | Effectively takes a **larger share** from lower incomes | Sales tax; the Social Security payroll portion above the wage base |

The Social Security payroll tax is worth understanding as the standard example of
regressivity in practice: it applies only up to an annually indexed wage base, so
a high earner stops paying partway through the year while a lower earner pays on
every dollar. The stated rate is flat; the effective burden is regressive.

## Types of Taxes for Individuals

| Tax | Base | Notes |
|-----|------|-------|
| **Federal income tax** | Taxable income | Progressive, bracketed |
| **State and local income tax** | Varies by jurisdiction | Some states impose none |
| **Payroll (FICA)** | Wages | Social Security portion capped; Medicare portion uncapped |
| **Self-employment tax** | Net self-employment earnings | Both halves of FICA, with a partial offsetting deduction |
| **Capital gains tax** | Gain on sale of a capital asset | Preferential rates if long-term |
| **Property tax** | Assessed value of real property | Local; a major cost in some jurisdictions |
| **Sales and excise tax** | Consumption | Regressive in effect |
| **Estate tax** | Transfers at death above the exclusion | Federal, plus some states |
| **Gift tax** | Lifetime transfers above exclusions | Unified with the estate tax |
| **Generation-skipping transfer tax** | Transfers skipping a generation | Separate from estate and gift tax |
| **Net investment income tax** | Investment income above an income threshold | An additional surtax |

## Marginal vs. Effective Rate

A distinction that matters constantly in planning:

- **Marginal rate** — the rate applied to the **next** dollar of income
- **Effective (average) rate** — total tax ÷ total income

Because the federal system is **progressive with brackets**, moving into a higher
bracket does **not** apply that rate to all income. Only the dollars within that
bracket are taxed at it. This is among the most persistent misconceptions in
personal finance, and it leads people to refuse raises or extra work in the
belief they'll net less.

**Worked example.** Using illustrative brackets of 10% on the first $12,000, 12%
on the next $35,000, and 22% above that, for a taxpayer with $60,000 of taxable
income:

```
10% × $12,000               = $1,200.00
12% × ($47,000 − $12,000)   = $4,200.00
22% × ($60,000 − $47,000)   = $2,860.00
                    Total   = $8,260.00
```

- **Marginal rate: 22%** — the rate on the next dollar earned
- **Effective rate:** $8,260 ÷ $60,000 = **13.77%**

The effective rate is well below the marginal rate, which is the normal result
under a progressive system.

**Which to use when:** the **marginal** rate governs decisions about *additional*
income or deductions — whether to make a deductible contribution, realize a gain,
or take on extra work. The **effective** rate describes the overall burden and is
useful for comparison across years or taxpayers. Using the effective rate to
evaluate a marginal decision is a common analytical error.

## Categories of Income

The tax code sorts income into three categories, and the sorting matters because
**losses are generally confined to their own category**.

### Active (earned) income

Wages, salaries, bonuses, commissions, tips, and net self-employment earnings —
compensation for personal services in which the taxpayer materially participates.

Taxed at ordinary rates and subject to payroll or self-employment tax. It is the
most heavily taxed category, which is why converting active income into other
forms is a recurring planning theme.

### Portfolio income

Interest, dividends, capital gains, and royalties — income from investments.

Not subject to payroll tax, and some components receive preferential rates.

| Item | Treatment |
|------|-----------|
| Taxable interest | Ordinary rates |
| Municipal bond interest | Generally federally exempt |
| **Qualified** dividends | **Preferential** long-term capital gains rates |
| Ordinary (nonqualified) dividends | Ordinary rates |
| Short-term capital gains | Ordinary rates |
| **Long-term** capital gains | **Preferential** rates |

The qualified/ordinary dividend distinction turns on the payer and on a holding
period requirement. The short-term/long-term capital gain distinction turns on
whether the asset was held **more than one year** — a bright line with large
consequences, and one of the simplest planning levers available.

### Passive income

Income from a trade or business in which the taxpayer does **not** materially
participate, plus most rental activity regardless of participation.

**The passive activity loss rules** are the reason this category exists. Passive
losses generally may only offset **passive income** — not wages, not portfolio
income. Disallowed losses are **suspended** and carried forward, becoming
deductible against future passive income or, generally, when the activity is
disposed of in a fully taxable transaction.

Limited exceptions exist, including a special allowance for certain actively
managed rental real estate that phases out at higher income, and different
treatment for qualifying real estate professionals.

**Material participation** — determined by regulatory tests based on hours and
involvement — is what separates active from passive treatment, and it's often the
pivot on which a client's tax result turns.

## Why the Categories Matter

The practical rule: **losses generally stay in their lane.**

- Passive losses generally can't offset wages or portfolio income
- Capital losses offset capital gains, with only a limited amount deductible
  against ordinary income annually and the remainder carried forward indefinitely
- Ordinary losses have their own rules

A client with a $40,000 passive loss from a rental and $150,000 of wages
generally cannot use that loss against the wages this year. Understanding this
prevents a common and expensive misexpectation.

## Character, Timing, and Location

Three levers organize essentially all tax planning, and they recur throughout
Module 8:

| Lever | Question | Example |
|-------|----------|---------|
| **Character** | What *kind* of income is it? | Holding an asset beyond one year converts short-term to long-term gain |
| **Timing** | *When* is it recognized? | Deferring a gain to a lower-bracket year; accelerating deductions |
| **Location** | *Which account* holds the asset? | Bonds in tax-deferred accounts, tax-efficient equity in taxable |

Every strategy in [8.6](06-tax-planning.md) is an application of one or more of
these.

## Filing and Administration Basics

- **Tax year** — generally the calendar year for individuals
- **Accounting method** — individuals generally use the cash method, recognizing
  income when received and expenses when paid
- **Pay-as-you-go** — tax is paid through withholding or quarterly estimated
  payments; underpayment can trigger penalties even when the return is filed and
  paid on time
- **Statute of limitations** — generally three years from filing for assessment,
  extended to six years for substantial omissions of income, and unlimited for
  fraud or failure to file

## Common Pitfalls

- Believing a higher bracket applies to all income rather than the marginal dollars
- Using the effective rate to evaluate a marginal decision
- Expecting passive losses to offset wages
- Overlooking the one-year holding period that separates short- from long-term gain
- Assuming all dividends receive preferential rates
- Comparing investment options on pretax returns
- Forgetting that the Medicare portion of payroll tax has no wage cap
- Ignoring state tax, which can substantially change the analysis

## Self-Check

**1. Why doesn't entering a higher bracket raise the tax on all income?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The system is progressive with graduated brackets — each bracket's rate applies
only to income within that bracket. Additional income is taxed at the higher rate
only on the portion above the threshold, so total tax always rises less than
proportionally.
</details>

**2. Distinguish marginal from effective rate and state when each is used.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The marginal rate applies to the next dollar of income; the effective rate is
total tax divided by total income. Marginal governs decisions about additional
income or deductions; effective describes overall burden. Using effective for a
marginal decision understates the impact.
</details>

**3. Why can't a passive loss offset wage income?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The passive activity loss rules confine passive losses to offsetting passive
income. Excess losses are suspended and carried forward, becoming usable against
future passive income or generally on a fully taxable disposition of the
activity. Limited exceptions exist for certain rental real estate.
</details>

**4. What separates a long-term from a short-term capital gain, and why does it matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Holding the asset more than one year. Long-term gains receive preferential rates
while short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income, so the holding period can
substantially change the after-tax result — making it one of the simplest
planning levers available.
</details>

**5. Why is the Social Security payroll tax described as regressive?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It applies only up to an annually indexed wage base. A high earner pays on a
smaller fraction of total income than a lower earner who pays on every dollar, so
the effective burden falls more heavily on lower incomes despite a flat stated
rate.
</details>

**6. Name the three levers of tax planning.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Character (what kind of income), timing (when it's recognized), and location
(which account holds the asset). Essentially every planning strategy applies one
or more of these.
</details>
