# 9.4 — Incapacity Planning

## Overview

Estate planning addresses death. Incapacity planning addresses something more
likely and, in some respects, harder: the period when a client is alive but unable
to manage their own affairs.

The defining constraint is timing. **Every tool in this chapter requires legal
capacity to execute.** Once capacity is genuinely in question, the client can no
longer sign a power of attorney or a health care proxy — and the family is left
with court proceedings as the only remaining option. Incapacity documents must be
in place *before* they're needed, which means they belong in every plan
regardless of the client's age or health.

Two distinct domains require attention: **management of property**, and **personal
and medical decision-making**. They involve different documents and often
different people.

## Management of Property

### Conservator (guardian of the property)

When someone becomes incapacitated without advance documents, a court appoints a
**conservator** — sometimes called a guardian of the property or guardian of the
estate — to manage their finances.

Why this is the outcome to avoid:

| Drawback | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| **Cost** | Court proceedings, attorney fees, ongoing bond and accounting costs |
| **Delay** | Petitions take time, often at an urgent moment |
| **Publicity** | Proceedings and inventories are public records |
| **Loss of choice** | The court selects the conservator, which may not be who the client would have chosen |
| **Ongoing supervision** | Periodic accountings and court approval for many actions |
| **Family conflict** | Contested proceedings can be adversarial and lasting |

The proceeding is also inherently difficult for the family, since it requires
establishing in a public forum that a loved one is incapable of managing their own
affairs.

### Durable power of attorney

The primary tool for avoiding conservatorship. A **power of attorney** authorizes
an **agent** (attorney-in-fact) to act on the **principal's** behalf in financial
matters.

The word **durable** is essential: an ordinary power of attorney **terminates**
upon the principal's incapacity — exactly when it's needed. A durable power
contains language causing it to survive incapacity.

| Type | When effective |
|------|----------------|
| **Immediate (standing) durable POA** | Effective on execution and continues through incapacity |
| **Springing durable POA** | Effective only upon a defined triggering event, typically certified incapacity |

Springing powers appeal to clients who dislike granting authority before it's
needed. In practice they create friction: determining that the trigger occurred
requires physician certification, which takes time and which financial
institutions may dispute. Many practitioners favor an immediate durable power
with a highly trusted agent for this reason.

Practical considerations:

- **Scope** — powers may be broad or limited to specified transactions. Certain
  powers (making gifts, changing beneficiaries, creating or amending trusts) must
  generally be **explicitly granted**, and their absence blocks important planning
  later
- **Institutional acceptance** — banks and brokerages sometimes resist older or
  non-standard forms. Using institution-specific forms **in addition to** a general
  durable POA reduces friction
- **Successor agents** — always name them, in case the first is unable or
  unwilling to serve
- **Termination** — a power of attorney ends at the principal's **death**, at
  which point the executor takes over. Agents sometimes mistakenly continue acting
- **Revocation** — the principal may revoke while they retain capacity

The agent is a **fiduciary**, owing duties of loyalty and care. The choice of
agent matters more than the document's wording, since the authority granted is
substantial and oversight is limited.

### Revocable trusts for incapacity

A **funded revocable living trust** provides incapacity management directly: the
**successor trustee** assumes management of trust assets upon the grantor's
incapacity, per the trust's own terms, with no court involvement and no third
party needing to accept a power of attorney form.

This is one of the strongest arguments for a living trust, and it's often
underemphasized relative to probate avoidance. But it governs **only assets titled
in the trust** — which is why a trust and a durable power of attorney are
complements rather than alternatives. The power of attorney covers everything
outside the trust, including the act of transferring assets into it.

## Personal and Medical Care Decision-Making

### Durable power of attorney for health care

Also called a **health care proxy** or medical power of attorney. It appoints an
agent to make **medical decisions** when the principal cannot.

Its advantage over a written instruction document is flexibility: no document can
anticipate every medical circumstance, but a trusted agent can apply the client's
values to whatever actually arises.

Choosing the agent deserves real thought. The right person is someone who
understands the client's wishes, can remain composed under pressure, and is
willing to advocate against medical staff or family members if necessary. That is
not always the closest relative, and naming someone out of obligation is a common
error.

The client should **discuss their wishes explicitly** with the agent. A proxy who
has never had the conversation is left guessing at the hardest moment of their
life.

### Living will (advance directive)

A written statement of the client's wishes regarding **life-sustaining
treatment** — typically addressing artificial nutrition and hydration, mechanical
ventilation, resuscitation, and comfort care in terminal or persistent vegetative
conditions.

It **guides** decision-making rather than appointing a decision-maker. The two
documents work together: the living will states the wishes, the health care proxy
appoints someone to interpret and apply them.

Related documents include **DNR** (do not resuscitate) and **POLST/MOLST** orders,
which are physician orders rather than legal directives and carry immediate
clinical effect. **HIPAA authorizations** are also necessary — without one,
providers may refuse to release medical information even to a named agent, which
can obstruct decision-making entirely.

Documents are useless if nobody can find them. Copies should go to the agent,
physicians, and the hospital record, not into a safe deposit box.

## Medicaid Planning

For clients facing extended long-term care costs, Medicaid becomes relevant — with
the qualifications set out in
[5.4](../module-05-life-health-disability/04-long-term-care.md): it is a
**means-tested welfare program**, requiring assets and income to be spent down to
state-set limits.

Key features affecting planning:

- **Five-year look-back.** Asset transfers within roughly five years of applying
  are scrutinized, and uncompensated transfers create a **penalty period** of
  ineligibility. Gifting late is worse than not gifting, because the penalty
  begins when the applicant would otherwise qualify — precisely when care is
  needed and resources are gone
- **Exempt assets** typically include a primary residence up to an equity limit
  (with protections where a spouse remains), one vehicle, personal effects,
  certain burial funds, and some life insurance
- **Spousal impoverishment rules** let a community spouse retain a limited
  resource allowance and income allowance
- **Estate recovery.** States must attempt to recover paid costs from the
  recipient's estate after death, which can reach the home

Medicaid planning sits squarely in **elder law** and requires an attorney. Rules
vary substantially by state, penalties for missteps are severe, and the interaction
with the client's other goals is intricate. The paraplanner's role is to identify
the issue, gather relevant facts, and refer — attempting the planning directly
would be both outside competence and outside scope
([1.3](../module-01-financial-planning-process/03-knowledge-domains.md)).

## ABLE Accounts

**Achieving a Better Life Experience** accounts are tax-advantaged savings
accounts for individuals whose qualifying disability began before a statutory age
threshold (raised by recent legislation).

| Feature | Treatment |
|---------|-----------|
| Contributions | Not federally deductible; some states offer a benefit |
| Growth | Tax-deferred |
| Qualified withdrawals | **Tax-free** |
| Qualified expenses | Broad — education, housing, transportation, health, assistive technology, basic living |
| Annual contribution | Limited to the gift tax annual exclusion amount, with an additional allowance for employed beneficiaries |
| **Means-tested benefits** | Balances up to a threshold are **disregarded** for SSI; Medicaid eligibility is generally unaffected |
| Ownership | The **beneficiary** owns the account |

The significance is that ABLE accounts let a disabled individual hold meaningful
savings **without** losing SSI and Medicaid eligibility — which the ordinary asset
limits would otherwise prevent. Before ABLE, saving anything meaningful in the
beneficiary's own name jeopardized benefits.

**ABLE and special needs trusts are complements**, not alternatives. ABLE accounts
are simple, inexpensive, and give the beneficiary direct control over modest
amounts. **Special needs trusts** ([9.2](02-transfer-tools.md)) handle larger sums,
provide professional management, and have no contribution ceiling. Families with
substantial resources typically use both.

Note that Medicaid may claim against remaining ABLE balances at the beneficiary's
death in some circumstances — a distinction from a properly drafted third-party
special needs trust, which generally avoids this.

## SWOT Analysis Applied to Incapacity Planning

Using the framework from
[6.4](../module-06-investments/04-investment-decisions.md) to assess a client's
readiness:

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---------|---------|
| **Internal** | **Strengths** — durable POA and health care proxy in place; funded revocable trust; willing and capable agents; documents discussed with family | **Weaknesses** — no documents; springing POA with unclear trigger; named agent who is aging or distant; unfunded trust; wishes never communicated |
| **External** | **Opportunities** — ABLE account eligibility; long-term care insurance while insurable; state-specific tools; institution-specific POA forms | **Threats** — cognitive decline reducing capacity to execute documents; long-term care costs; Medicaid look-back constraints; family conflict; institutional refusal of an old POA |

The external column is where the analysis earns its keep. The most serious threat
— **declining capacity** — is also the one that eliminates the ability to respond,
which makes incapacity planning uniquely time-sensitive. Documents that are merely
"on the list" become impossible rather than merely overdue.

## The Core Document Set

Every client should have:

1. **Durable power of attorney** for financial matters
2. **Durable power of attorney for health care** (health care proxy)
3. **Living will / advance directive**
4. **HIPAA authorization**
5. **Will**, naming an executor and a guardian for minor children
6. **Beneficiary designations**, reviewed and current
7. **Revocable living trust**, where circumstances warrant — and **funded**

Items 1 through 4 address incapacity; 5 and 6 address death; 7 addresses both.

Review triggers mirror those in [9.1](01-estate-terminology.md), with particular
attention to whether named agents remain willing, capable, and available. An agent
named twenty years ago may now be elderly, estranged, or deceased.

## Common Pitfalls

- Deferring documents until capacity is already questionable, when execution is no longer possible
- Using a non-durable power of attorney, which terminates exactly when needed
- Choosing a springing POA without appreciating the certification friction
- Failing to grant specific powers (gifting, beneficiary changes, trust amendments) that later prove necessary
- Naming no successor agents
- Creating a revocable trust for incapacity and never funding it
- Omitting a HIPAA authorization, blocking the health care agent's access to information
- Never discussing wishes with the named health care agent
- Storing documents where nobody can access them
- Making uncompensated transfers within the Medicaid look-back period
- Attempting Medicaid planning without elder law counsel
- Overlooking ABLE account eligibility for a disabled client
- Assuming an agent's authority continues after the principal's death

## Self-Check

**1. Why must incapacity documents be executed well before they're needed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Executing them requires legal capacity. Once capacity is genuinely in question,
the client can no longer sign a power of attorney or health care proxy, leaving
court-appointed conservatorship as the only option — costly, public, delayed, and
with the court rather than the client choosing who serves.
</details>

**2. What does "durable" mean, and why is it essential?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A durable power of attorney survives the principal's incapacity. An ordinary
power terminates upon incapacity — precisely the moment it becomes necessary — so
without durability the document fails at its intended purpose.
</details>

**3. How do a living will and a health care proxy differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A living will states the client's wishes about life-sustaining treatment; a
health care proxy appoints an agent to make medical decisions. The living will
provides guidance, the proxy provides a decision-maker who can apply the client's
values to circumstances no document anticipated. Both are needed.
</details>

**4. Why are a funded revocable trust and a durable POA complements rather than
alternatives?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The successor trustee manages only assets titled in the trust. The durable power
of attorney covers everything outside it — including the act of transferring
assets into the trust. Neither alone covers the full picture.
</details>

**5. What makes an ABLE account valuable for a disabled beneficiary?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Balances up to a threshold are disregarded for SSI, and Medicaid eligibility is
generally unaffected, so the beneficiary can hold meaningful savings in their own
name without losing means-tested benefits — which ordinary asset limits would
otherwise prevent. Growth and qualified withdrawals are also tax-free.
</details>

**6. Why is gifting shortly before a Medicaid application worse than not gifting?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Uncompensated transfers within the roughly five-year look-back create a penalty
period of ineligibility that begins when the applicant would otherwise have
qualified. The applicant is then both without the transferred assets and without
coverage, at the moment care is actually needed.
</details>
