# 9.3 — Estate Transfer Expenses

## Overview

Transferring an estate costs money in three ways: **administrative expenses**
(probate, executor, attorney, appraisal), **transfer taxes** (estate, gift, and
generation-skipping), and **income tax** on income in respect of a decedent.

For most families the administrative costs and the **liquidity problem** matter
far more than transfer taxes, because current federal exclusion levels place the
estate tax beyond the reach of the overwhelming majority of estates. Planners who
focus exclusively on estate tax often miss the problems their clients actually
have.

> **Note on figures.** Exclusion amounts, annual exclusions, rates, and thresholds
> are indexed annually and subject to legislation — the federal exclusion is
> scheduled to change. This chapter describes **structure** only. Verify all
> current figures before relying on them.

## The Unified Transfer Tax System

Federal gift and estate taxes are **unified**: a single lifetime exemption covers
both, so lifetime gifts and transfers at death draw on the same pool.

The purpose of the unification is to prevent avoidance by simply giving
everything away before death. Without it, the estate tax would apply only to
people who died unexpectedly.

Three related taxes:

| Tax | Applies to |
|-----|-----------|
| **Gift tax** | Lifetime transfers above the annual exclusion |
| **Estate tax** | Transfers at death above the remaining exclusion |
| **Generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax** | Transfers that skip a generation |

## Gift Tax

### Annual exclusion

Each donor may give up to an indexed amount **per recipient per year** with no
gift tax and **no use of the lifetime exemption**. There's no limit on the number
of recipients.

**Gift splitting** lets a married couple treat gifts as made half by each,
effectively doubling the per-recipient amount — but it requires **filing a gift
tax return** to elect, even when no tax is owed.

The exclusion applies only to gifts of a **present interest** — the recipient must
have an immediate right to use or enjoy the property. Gifts to a trust where the
beneficiary's enjoyment is delayed are **future interests** and don't qualify,
unless the trust includes withdrawal rights (Crummey powers) creating a present
interest.

Annual exclusions **do not accumulate** — an unused year is gone permanently,
which is why systematic annual gifting is a meaningful long-run strategy for
larger estates.

### Unlimited exclusions

Two transfers are entirely excluded, regardless of amount:

- **Qualified tuition** paid **directly to the educational institution**
- **Qualified medical expenses** paid **directly to the provider**

The direct-payment requirement is strict — reimbursing the individual converts it
into an ordinary gift. Properly used, these are among the most efficient wealth
transfer tools available, and they don't consume the annual exclusion, which
remains available for other gifts to the same person.

### Marital and charitable deductions

- **Unlimited marital deduction** for transfers to a **U.S. citizen** spouse.
  Transfers to a non-citizen spouse are limited to an indexed annual amount, with
  a **QDOT** (qualified domestic trust) available for larger amounts
- **Unlimited charitable deduction** for transfers to qualifying organizations

### Lifetime exemption and filing

Gifts exceeding the annual exclusion consume the lifetime exemption. Tax is owed
only after it's exhausted — rare in practice.

A **gift tax return (Form 709)** is required when gifts to any one recipient
exceed the annual exclusion, when splitting gifts, or when making certain
transfers of future interests, **even if no tax is due**. Filing establishes the
record of exemption used, which matters at death.

Note also: the **donor** pays gift tax, not the recipient. Gifts received are not
income to the recipient.

## Estate Tax

### Computing the taxable estate

```
     Gross estate
   − Debts, funeral, and administration expenses
   − Marital deduction
   − Charitable deduction
   = Taxable estate
   + Adjusted taxable gifts (post-1976)
   = Tentative tax base
   × Rates
   − Gift taxes payable and unified credit
   = Estate tax due
```

### The gross estate

Broader than probate property, and this catches people out. It includes:

- Property owned outright at death
- The decedent's share of **jointly held** property (generally all of it for
  non-spouse joint tenants, except to the extent the survivor contributed; half
  for spousal joint tenancy)
- **Life insurance** where the decedent held **incidents of ownership**, or where
  proceeds are payable to the estate
- **Retirement accounts** and annuities
- **Revocable trust** assets — retained control means retained inclusion
- Certain lifetime transfers with **retained interests** or retained control
- Property over which the decedent held a **general power of appointment**
- Gifts of life insurance within **three years** of death

The unifying principle: **retained control means inclusion.** This is why
revocable trusts provide no estate tax benefit and irrevocable ones can.

### Valuation

Property is valued at **fair market value** on the date of death. Alternatively,
the executor may elect the **alternate valuation date** — six months after death —
but only if it reduces **both** the gross estate and the estate tax. The election
applies to the entire estate, not selected assets.

**Valuation discounts** may apply to closely held business interests and
fractional interests, for **lack of marketability** and **lack of control**. These
require qualified appraisals and are an audit focus.

### Marital deduction and portability

The **unlimited marital deduction** defers rather than eliminates tax — assets
pass to the surviving spouse untaxed and are included in the survivor's estate at
their death.

**Portability** allows the surviving spouse to use the deceased spouse's unused
exclusion (the DSUE amount). It requires a **timely-filed estate tax return** to
elect, even when no tax is otherwise owed.

This is one of the most commonly missed steps in estate administration. A family
whose first-to-die estate owes nothing often skips the return entirely — and
forfeits an exclusion amount that could matter enormously at the survivor's death,
particularly if exclusion levels fall.

**Credit shelter (bypass) trusts** are the traditional alternative: funding a
trust with the first spouse's exclusion at the first death, so the assets **and
their future appreciation** bypass the survivor's estate. Portability made these
less essential for pure exclusion-preservation purposes, but they still offer
advantages — sheltering appreciation, controlling the ultimate remainder
(important in blended families), and providing creditor protection. Note that
assets in a bypass trust do **not** receive a second step-up at the survivor's
death, which is the trade-off against portability.

## Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax

A separate tax on transfers to a **skip person** — someone two or more generations
below the transferor, such as a grandchild, or an unrelated person more than
37.5 years younger.

Its purpose is to prevent avoidance of a full generation of estate tax by
transferring directly to grandchildren. It applies **in addition to** gift or
estate tax, at a flat rate equal to the highest estate tax rate.

Each transferor has a **GST exemption**, generally equal to the estate tax
exclusion. A **predeceased ancestor exception** applies where the intervening
generation has already died.

GST is genuinely complex and warrants specialist counsel whenever
multigenerational transfers or dynasty trusts are contemplated.

## Administrative Expenses

Frequently larger than transfer taxes for ordinary estates:

| Expense | Notes |
|---------|-------|
| **Court and probate fees** | Vary widely by state; some are percentage-based |
| **Executor commissions** | Statutory or reasonable; a family executor may waive |
| **Attorney fees** | Hourly, flat, or percentage depending on jurisdiction |
| **Appraisal fees** | Real property, business interests, collectibles |
| **Accounting and tax preparation** | Final 1040, Form 706, fiduciary returns |
| **Bond premiums** | Unless waived by the will |
| **Property maintenance** | Insurance, taxes, upkeep during administration |

Notably, a family executor waiving commissions isn't always optimal: commissions
are **taxable income** to the executor but **deductible** to the estate, so where
the executor is also the primary beneficiary, the analysis depends on relative tax
rates.

## Income in Respect of a Decedent

**IRD** is income the decedent had earned but not yet received at death —
traditional IRA and retirement account balances, accrued but unpaid wages,
deferred compensation, accrued interest, and installment sale payments.

Two consequences:

- IRD is included in the **gross estate** for estate tax purposes, **and**
- The recipient owes **income tax** on it — there is **no step-up in basis**

This produces potential double taxation. A partial remedy exists: an **IRD income
tax deduction** for the estate tax attributable to the IRD item.

The practical planning implication: **IRD assets are the least efficient to leave
to individual heirs**, and the most efficient to leave to **charity**, since a
charity pays no income tax on them. A client with both an IRA and appreciated
taxable securities who intends a charitable bequest should generally direct the
**IRA to charity** and the appreciated securities to family — the securities
receive a step-up, and the IRA's embedded income tax disappears.

## The Liquidity Problem

Often the most pressing real issue, and the one most likely to affect ordinary
clients.

Estate obligations — debts, funeral and administration expenses, taxes — come due
within months, while estate assets may be **illiquid**: a closely held business, a
farm, real estate, collectibles.

Without liquidity, the executor may be forced into a **distress sale** at an
unfavorable price, which is precisely how family businesses and farms get lost.

Solutions:

| Source | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| **Life insurance** | The classic answer — immediate, income-tax-free liquidity. Owned by an **ILIT** so proceeds stay outside the estate |
| **Funded buy-sell agreement** | Establishes a buyer and price for a business interest ([2.5](../module-02-cash-management-debt/05-business-forms.md)) |
| Retaining liquid assets | Simple, at the cost of lower expected returns |
| Installment payment of estate tax | Relief for estates consisting largely of a closely held business, subject to strict requirements |
| Borrowing | Available but adds cost and requires creditworthy collateral |

Life insurance in an ILIT is the standard recommendation because it solves the
liquidity problem **without** enlarging the estate that created it.

## Common Pitfalls

- Focusing on estate tax when administrative costs and liquidity are the real issues
- Missing the portability election because no tax was owed
- Assuming a revocable trust reduces estate tax — it doesn't
- Overlooking that life insurance is included when the insured holds incidents of ownership
- Reimbursing tuition or medical costs rather than paying institutions directly
- Failing to file Form 709 when required, losing the record of exemption used
- Leaving IRD assets to individuals and appreciated assets to charity — exactly backwards
- Ignoring state estate and inheritance taxes with much lower exclusions
- Failing to plan liquidity for an illiquid estate, forcing a distress sale
- Electing alternate valuation without confirming it reduces both estate and tax

## Self-Check

**1. What does it mean that the gift and estate taxes are unified?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A single lifetime exemption covers both, so lifetime gifts and transfers at death
draw on the same pool. This prevents avoiding estate tax by giving everything
away before death.
</details>

**2. Why should an IRA go to charity and appreciated stock to family?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The IRA is income in respect of a decedent — no step-up, and heirs owe ordinary
income tax on distributions. A charity pays no income tax, so that liability
disappears. Appreciated securities receive a step-up at death, so family receives
them free of the built-in gain. Reversing the two wastes both advantages.
</details>

**3. What is portability and why is it commonly lost?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It lets a surviving spouse use the deceased spouse's unused exclusion. It
requires a timely-filed federal estate tax return to elect — which families
frequently skip when the first estate owes no tax, forfeiting exclusion that
could matter greatly at the survivor's death.
</details>

**4. Why does a revocable trust provide no estate tax benefit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The grantor retains the power to amend or revoke, so the assets remain within
their control and are included in the gross estate. Retained control means
inclusion — which is the general principle governing the gross estate.
</details>

**5. What is the liquidity problem and how is it usually solved?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Estate debts, expenses, and taxes come due within months while assets may be
illiquid — a business, farm, or real estate — forcing a distress sale. Life
insurance owned by an ILIT is the standard solution, providing immediate
income-tax-free liquidity without adding to the taxable estate.
</details>

**6. When may the alternate valuation date be elected?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Six months after death, and only if the election reduces **both** the gross
estate and the estate tax. It applies to the entire estate rather than selected
assets.
</details>
