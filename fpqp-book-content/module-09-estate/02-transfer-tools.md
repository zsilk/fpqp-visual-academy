# 9.2 — Estate Transfer Tools

## Overview

This chapter covers the mechanisms that actually move property: wills, property
titling, contracts, trusts, and lifetime gifts. Each has distinct strengths, and a
real plan combines several — the will handling what nothing else reaches, titling
and beneficiary designations handling the bulk, and trusts handling what requires
ongoing control.

The recurring theme from [9.1](01-estate-terminology.md) applies throughout:
**coordination matters more than any individual document.** Tools that
individually make sense can defeat each other when they aren't reviewed together.

## Wills

### Strengths

- **Names a guardian** for minor children — often the single most important
  function
- **Names an executor** of the testator's choosing
- Directs property that has no other transfer mechanism
- Can create a **testamentary trust** for minors or beneficiaries needing
  management
- Can specify how debts, expenses, and taxes are apportioned
- Revocable and amendable during life
- Relatively inexpensive to prepare

### Weaknesses

- **Governs only probate property** — no effect on beneficiary designations,
  survivorship property, or trust assets
- **Subject to probate**, with its cost, delay, and publicity
- **Becomes a public record**
- Provides **no incapacity planning** — a will operates only at death
- Can be **contested** by disappointed heirs
- Requires **ancillary probate** for out-of-state real property

That fourth point is worth stating plainly to clients: a will does nothing if the
client becomes incapacitated. Incapacity requires a **durable power of attorney**
for financial matters and a **health care proxy** with an advance directive.
Without them, a court-supervised guardianship or conservatorship may be the only
option — expensive, public, and slow, at the worst possible time.

### Types and formalities

**Formal (attested)** wills are typed and executed with witnesses per state
requirements — the standard and by far the most reliable. **Holographic**
(handwritten) wills are recognized in some states with specific requirements.
**Nuncupative** (oral) wills are rarely valid and severely limited.

A **codicil** amends a will; substantial changes are usually better handled by a
new will to avoid ambiguity.

A **self-proving affidavit** — a notarized statement by testator and witnesses —
lets the will be admitted without locating witnesses years later, and is
inexpensive insurance.

Common drafting failures: no residuary clause, no contingent beneficiaries,
naming a minor to receive property outright, failing to update after major life
events, and storing the original where nobody can find it. An unlocatable original
may be presumed revoked.

## Intestate Succession

Without a valid will, state statute controls. Typical patterns distribute among
spouse and descendants in defined shares, then to parents, siblings, and more
remote relatives, with property **escheating** to the state only when no heirs
exist.

What intestacy **cannot** do:

- Provide for an **unmarried partner** — who typically receives nothing
- Provide for **stepchildren** not legally adopted
- Make **charitable** gifts
- Distinguish among children by need or circumstance
- Name a **guardian** for minor children — a court decides
- Name an **executor** of the family's choosing
- Avoid outright distribution to a young adult at majority

The mismatch between statutory defaults and actual wishes is the practical
argument for a will, and it's most acute for blended families and unmarried
couples.

## Property Ownership

Titling determines transfer, and it silently overrides the will.

| Form | Ownership | At death |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Fee simple (sole)** | One owner, complete interest | Passes by will or intestacy — **probate** |
| **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JTWROS)** | Equal undivided shares | Passes **automatically** to survivor(s) — no probate |
| **Tenancy by the entirety** | Spouses only, in states recognizing it | Passes automatically to surviving spouse; adds creditor protection |
| **Tenancy in common** | Separate, possibly unequal shares | The decedent's share passes by will — **probate** |
| **Community property** | Spouses in community property states; each owns half | The decedent's half passes by will |

### JTWROS — convenient and frequently misused

Joint tenancy avoids probate, which is why clients like it. The problems are
substantial:

- **Overrides the will entirely.** Adding one child as joint owner "for
  convenience" disinherits the others as to that asset
- **Exposes the asset to the joint owner's creditors**, divorce, and lawsuits
- **Gift tax consequences** may arise when a non-spouse is added
- **Loss of control** — the joint owner can often withdraw or encumber
- **Basis consequences**: only the decedent's share generally receives a step-up
  with a non-spouse joint owner, forfeiting part of the step-up available if the
  asset had passed at death

Adding an adult child to a bank account or deed for convenience is among the most
common well-intentioned estate mistakes. A **durable power of attorney** or a
**revocable trust** achieves the intended convenience without these consequences.

### Community property

In community property states, most property acquired during marriage is owned
equally regardless of title. **Separate property** — owned before marriage or
received by gift or inheritance — remains individual unless commingled.

Each spouse controls the disposition of their half at death. The major advantage,
noted in [9.1](01-estate-terminology.md): at the first death, the **entire**
community interest generally receives a **step-up in basis**, not just the
decedent's half — a substantial benefit unavailable in common law states.

## Transfers by Contract

Property passing by contractual beneficiary designation, outside both will and
probate:

- **Life insurance** proceeds
- **Retirement accounts** — IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s
- **Annuities**
- **POD** (payable on death) bank accounts and **TOD** (transfer on death)
  securities registrations
- **TOD deeds** for real property, in states permitting them

These are efficient — immediate, private, free — and dangerous precisely because
they're easy to set and then forget. Reviewing them is the highest
value-per-minute activity in estate planning.

Specific cautions:

- Always name **contingent** beneficiaries
- Never name a **minor** directly; use a trust or custodial arrangement
- Avoid naming the **estate**, which forces probate and can eliminate favorable
  retirement account distribution treatment
- Coordinate designations with the overall plan so shares work out as intended
- **ERISA plans** generally require spousal consent to name a non-spouse
  beneficiary

### Worked example — will substitutes defeating a plan

A widowed client's will leaves "everything equally to my three children." Assets:

| Asset | Value | Titling / beneficiary | Passes to |
|-------|-------|----------------------|-----------|
| Home | $400,000 | JTWROS with daughter Ann | **Ann alone** |
| IRA | $500,000 | Beneficiary: son Ben | **Ben alone** |
| Life insurance | $250,000 | Beneficiary: deceased spouse, no contingent | **The estate** — probate |
| Brokerage | $150,000 | Sole name | Estate — split three ways |
| Checking | $30,000 | POD to daughter Cara | **Cara alone** |

Intended: roughly $443,000 each. Actual result: Ann receives about $533,000, Ben
about $633,000, and Cara about $163,000 — because only the brokerage account and
the lapsed insurance proceeds pass under the will.

Nothing here was drafted badly. The will is fine. The plan fails entirely on
coordination, and a single beneficiary review would have caught all of it.

## Trusts

A **trust** is a legal arrangement in which a **trustee** holds property for
**beneficiaries** under terms set by the **grantor** (settlor, trustor).

### Core distinctions

**Revocable vs. irrevocable:**

| | Revocable | Irrevocable |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| Grantor can amend or revoke | **Yes** | Generally no |
| Included in grantor's gross estate | **Yes** | Generally no |
| Creditor protection from grantor's creditors | **No** | Generally yes |
| Income taxed to | Grantor | Trust or beneficiaries, depending on structure |
| Primary purpose | Probate avoidance, incapacity management | Estate tax reduction, asset protection, control |

The trade-off is direct: **control versus benefit.** Retaining control means
retaining estate inclusion. Giving up control is what purchases the tax and
protection benefits.

**Living (inter vivos) vs. testamentary:** created during life versus created by
the will at death. A testamentary trust necessarily goes through probate, since
the will creates it.

### Revocable living trusts

The workhorse of ordinary estate planning.

**Advantages**: avoids probate for assets titled in the trust, including
**ancillary probate** for out-of-state real property; provides **incapacity
management** through a successor trustee without court involvement; remains
**private**; and is fully amendable during life.

**Limitations**: no estate tax benefit (assets remain in the gross estate); no
creditor protection during the grantor's life; costs more than a will to
establish; and — critically — **requires funding**.

An **unfunded** revocable trust accomplishes nothing. Assets must actually be
retitled into the trust. This is the most common failure with living trusts:
clients pay for the document and never complete the retitling, leaving assets to
pass through probate anyway.

A **pour-over will** is the companion document, directing any assets not titled in
the trust into it at death. It's a safety net, not a substitute for funding — the
poured-over assets still pass through probate.

### Common irrevocable trusts

| Trust | Purpose |
|-------|---------|
| **ILIT** (irrevocable life insurance trust) | Owns life insurance so proceeds are excluded from the gross estate |
| **Credit shelter / bypass trust** | Uses the first spouse's exclusion, sheltering assets and their appreciation from the survivor's estate |
| **QTIP** (qualified terminable interest property) | Provides income to the surviving spouse while the grantor controls the ultimate remainder — common in blended families |
| **Charitable remainder trust** | Income to non-charitable beneficiaries for a period, remainder to charity |
| **Charitable lead trust** | The reverse — income to charity, remainder to family |
| **Special needs trust** | Supplements a disabled beneficiary without disqualifying them from means-tested benefits |
| **Spendthrift trust** | Protects assets from a beneficiary's creditors and their own imprudence |
| **GRAT** (grantor retained annuity trust) | Transfers appreciation above a statutory rate with minimal gift tax |

The **ILIT** is worth understanding specifically, since it addresses the estate
inclusion problem from
[5.1](../module-05-life-health-disability/01-life-insurance.md). Life insurance
proceeds are included in the gross estate when the insured held **incidents of
ownership**. If an ILIT owns the policy from inception, the insured holds none and
the proceeds are excluded. Note the **three-year rule**: transferring an existing
policy to an ILIT and dying within three years pulls the proceeds back into the
estate — which argues for having the trust purchase the policy originally.

**Special needs trusts** deserve emphasis for a practical reason: leaving assets
outright to a disabled beneficiary can disqualify them from means-tested benefits
like Medicaid and SSI. A properly drafted special needs trust supplements those
benefits without replacing them. Families sometimes attempt to solve this by
disinheriting the disabled child and relying on a sibling — an arrangement with no
legal protection if the sibling divorces, is sued, or simply declines.

### Trusts and titling

A trust only governs assets **actually transferred into it**. Retitling means
changing deeds, account registrations, and business interests into the trust's
name.

Retirement accounts are generally **not** retitled into a revocable trust — doing
so would be a taxable distribution. Instead the trust may be named as beneficiary,
though this requires care: trust beneficiary designations interact with the
retirement distribution rules in
[7.2](../module-07-retirement/02-sources-of-income.md), and a poorly drafted trust
can accelerate taxation. This is specialist drafting work.

## Gifts

Lifetime transfers, which remove assets and their **future appreciation** from the
estate.

### Gift tax framework

Developed further in [9.3](03-transfer-expenses.md):

- The **annual exclusion** permits gifts up to a set amount per recipient per year
  with no gift tax and no use of the lifetime exemption. **Gift splitting** lets
  married couples combine their exclusions
- Gifts above the annual exclusion use the **lifetime exemption**, which is
  unified with the estate tax exclusion
- **Unlimited exclusions** apply for direct payments of **tuition** to an
  educational institution and **medical expenses** to a provider — these must be
  paid **directly** to the institution, not reimbursed to the individual
- Transfers to a **U.S. citizen spouse** are unlimited under the marital deduction

The direct-payment exclusions are underused. A grandparent can pay a
grandchild's full tuition directly, in any amount, with no gift tax consequence
and no reduction of the annual exclusion available for other gifts.

### UGMA and UTMA

Custodial accounts, covered in
[8.6](../module-08-tax/06-tax-planning.md). The estate planning points:

- The gift is **irrevocable** and the property is the **minor's**
- The child gains **unrestricted control at majority**
- The **kiddie tax** limits income shifting
- If the **donor serves as custodian and dies** while serving, the account is
  included in the donor's estate — easily avoided by naming a different custodian

### Advantages and disadvantages of gifting

**Advantages**: removes future appreciation from the estate; uses annual
exclusions that otherwise expire each year; shifts income to lower-bracket
recipients within kiddie tax limits; provides support when it's actually needed;
and remains private.

**Disadvantages**: **carryover basis forfeits the step-up**; the transfer is
irrevocable; the donor loses control and the asset; recipients may be unprepared
to manage it; and it can affect financial aid or means-tested benefit eligibility.

The **gift-versus-bequest** decision is the recurring tension, and the rule of
thumb bears repeating: **gift cash and high-basis assets, bequeath low-basis
appreciated assets.** Gifting appreciated property trades a step-up that would
have eliminated the gain for an estate tax benefit the client may not even need,
given current exclusion levels.

## Common Pitfalls

- Relying on a will while beneficiary designations and titling control the assets
- Adding a child as joint owner for convenience, disinheriting siblings and exposing the asset
- Creating a revocable trust and never funding it
- Assuming a pour-over will substitutes for funding
- Transferring an existing policy to an ILIT and dying within three years
- Leaving assets outright to a disabled beneficiary, disqualifying them from benefits
- Naming a minor or the estate as beneficiary
- Omitting contingent beneficiaries
- Serving as custodian of a UTMA the donor funded, causing estate inclusion
- Reimbursing tuition rather than paying the institution directly, wasting the exclusion
- Gifting appreciated assets that would have received a step-up
- Having no durable power of attorney or health care directive

## Self-Check

**1. Why is an unfunded revocable living trust worthless?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A trust governs only assets actually titled in its name. Without retitling,
assets remain in the grantor's individual name and pass through probate — the
exact outcome the trust was created to avoid. A pour-over will catches them, but
they still go through probate.
</details>

**2. What's wrong with adding an adult child as joint owner of a bank account for
convenience?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It's a completed transfer with survivorship — the account passes to that child
alone at death, disinheriting other children as to that asset regardless of the
will. It also exposes the funds to the child's creditors and divorce, may have
gift tax consequences, and forfeits part of the step-up. A durable power of
attorney achieves the intended convenience without these effects.
</details>

**3. How does an ILIT keep life insurance out of the gross estate, and what's the
three-year rule?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Proceeds are included when the insured holds incidents of ownership. If the ILIT
owns the policy from inception, the insured holds none, so proceeds are excluded.
The three-year rule pulls proceeds back into the estate if an existing policy is
transferred to the trust and the insured dies within three years — which is why
the trust should purchase the policy originally.
</details>

**4. Why is leaving assets outright to a disabled beneficiary a problem?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It can disqualify them from means-tested benefits such as Medicaid and SSI. A
properly drafted special needs trust supplements those benefits without replacing
them. Disinheriting the beneficiary and relying on a sibling's informal promise
offers no legal protection.
</details>

**5. What's distinctive about the tuition and medical gift exclusions?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They're unlimited in amount and don't consume the annual exclusion or lifetime
exemption — but payment must go **directly** to the educational institution or
medical provider. Reimbursing the individual instead makes it an ordinary gift.
</details>

**6. State the general rule for deciding whether to gift or bequeath an asset.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Gift cash and high-basis assets; bequeath low-basis appreciated assets. Gifting
carries over the donor's basis, so the recipient inherits the built-in gain, while
bequeathing delivers a step-up that eliminates it.
</details>
