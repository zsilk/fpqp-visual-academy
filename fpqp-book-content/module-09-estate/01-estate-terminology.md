# 9.1 — Estate Planning Terminology

## Overview

Estate planning determines how property transfers at death or incapacity — to
whom, at what cost, under whose control, and with how much delay and publicity.

The most important early insight is that **a will controls less than clients
assume.** A large share of the typical estate passes outside the will entirely,
through beneficiary designations and property titling. A client with a
meticulously drafted will and a stale 401(k) beneficiary form has a plan that
doesn't work — and the beneficiary form wins.

> **Note on figures.** Exclusion amounts, exemption levels, and rates are indexed
> and subject to legislation, with the federal estate tax exclusion scheduled to
> change. Verify all current figures before relying on them.

## Classes of Property

How property is classified determines how it transfers:

| Class | Description | Transfers by |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| **Probate property** | Solely owned property with no beneficiary designation or survivorship feature | Will, or intestacy law |
| **Non-probate property** | Property with a survivorship feature, beneficiary designation, or held in trust | Operation of law or contract |

**Real property** is generally governed by the law of the state where it's
located — which is why owning property in multiple states can trigger **ancillary
probate** in each. **Personal property** is generally governed by the law of the
decedent's domicile.

## Administering a Will

### Who is involved

| Party | Role |
|-------|------|
| **Testator** | The person who makes the will |
| **Executor** (personal representative) | Named in the will to administer the estate |
| **Administrator** | Appointed by the court when there's no will or no named executor able to serve |
| **Beneficiary / legatee / devisee** | Receives property under the will |
| **Heir** | Someone entitled to inherit under intestacy law |
| **Guardian** | Named for minor children — often the will's most important provision |
| **Witnesses** | Required to attest execution; number and qualifications vary by state |

For parents of minor children, **naming a guardian** is frequently the single
strongest reason to have a will. Without it, a court decides, without knowing the
family's preferences.

### Testate and intestate

- **Testate** — dying with a valid will; property passes as directed
- **Intestate** — dying without a valid will; property passes under the state's
  **intestate succession statute**

Intestacy statutes distribute to a defined hierarchy of relatives. They reflect
legislative assumptions about what an average person would want, and they
frequently diverge from what a particular client actually wants — they ignore
unmarried partners, stepchildren not legally adopted, charities, and any
distinction between family members.

**Partial intestacy** occurs when a will fails to dispose of all property. A
**residuary clause** — directing everything not otherwise given — prevents it, and
its absence is a common drafting failure.

### Probate

The court-supervised process of validating a will, settling debts and taxes, and
distributing remaining property.

**What probate does**: proves the will's validity, appoints the executor, provides
a forum for creditor claims and will contests, and produces clear title for
distributed assets.

**Drawbacks**:

| Issue | Effect |
|-------|--------|
| **Cost** | Court fees, executor commissions, attorney fees — varying widely by state |
| **Delay** | Months to years before beneficiaries receive property |
| **Publicity** | Probate is a **public** record; the will and often an inventory become viewable |
| **Ancillary probate** | Separate proceedings in each state where real property is located |
| **Court supervision** | Reduced flexibility |

Many states offer **simplified or small estate procedures** for modest estates,
which substantially reduce cost and delay.

Probate avoidance is a legitimate goal but shouldn't be pursued blindly. Probate
provides a structured creditor claim process and a clear mechanism for resolving
disputes — genuine benefits in some situations.

### Executor and administrator duties

Locating and safeguarding assets; obtaining date-of-death valuations; notifying
creditors and beneficiaries; paying valid debts, expenses, and taxes; filing the
decedent's final income tax return and any estate tax and fiduciary income tax
returns; and distributing property per the will or intestacy law.

The executor is a **fiduciary**, personally liable for mismanagement. Clients
naming an executor should consider organizational capability and impartiality
among family members, not just closeness — and should ask the person first.

## Settling an Estate

The practical sequence: locate the will and key documents, secure property, obtain
death certificates, open probate if required, inventory and value assets, notify
creditors, pay debts and expenses in the statutory priority order, file required
tax returns, and distribute the remainder.

### Digital assets

An increasingly consequential category — email, cloud storage, photographs, social
media, cryptocurrency, domain names, online financial accounts, and loyalty
programs.

The difficulties are structural: terms of service may prohibit transfer, access
credentials may be unknown, and computer fraud statutes create genuine uncertainty
about unauthorized access even by a legitimate fiduciary. Most states have adopted
a version of the **Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act**
(RUFADAA), which gives effect to the user's own directions — through a
provider's online tool first, then the will or trust, and only then the terms of
service.

Practical recommendations: use providers' legacy contact tools where offered,
grant explicit authority over digital assets in estate documents, and maintain a
secure inventory of accounts. **Cryptocurrency** deserves specific attention —
without the private keys, the assets are permanently unrecoverable, and no court
order can retrieve them.

## Will Substitutes

Property passing **outside** the will, and typically outside probate:

| Substitute | Mechanism |
|------------|-----------|
| **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship** | Passes automatically to the surviving joint owner |
| **Tenancy by the entirety** | Spousal form with survivorship, in states recognizing it |
| **Beneficiary designations** | Life insurance, retirement accounts, annuities |
| **POD / TOD accounts** | Payable-on-death, transfer-on-death registrations |
| **TOD deeds** | Real property, in states permitting them |
| **Living trusts** | Trust assets pass under the trust's terms |

**These override the will.** A will directing "all my property equally to my three
children" does not touch an IRA naming only one of them.

That single fact makes **beneficiary review** among the highest-value, lowest-cost
recommendations available. Common failures: a former spouse still named after
divorce, a deceased primary beneficiary with no contingent, a minor named
directly (forcing guardianship proceedings), or an estate named as beneficiary
(needlessly subjecting assets to probate and losing favorable retirement account
stretch treatment).

## Estate Taxes

A brief orientation; developed in [9.3](03-transfer-expenses.md).

The **federal estate tax** applies to the taxable estate above an exclusion
amount, which is currently high enough that relatively few estates owe it — though
the exclusion is scheduled to change, and the number of affected estates would
rise accordingly.

The **gross estate** includes property owned at death, life insurance proceeds
where the decedent held incidents of ownership, retirement accounts, the
decedent's share of jointly held property, and certain lifetime transfers with
retained interests.

Two provisions do most of the work in practice:

- The **unlimited marital deduction** — transfers to a U.S. citizen spouse are
  fully deductible, deferring tax until the survivor's death
- **Portability** — a surviving spouse may use the deceased spouse's unused
  exclusion, but only if an estate tax return is **timely filed** to elect it.
  This election is missed with some regularity in estates that owe no tax, and it
  can be very costly later

Several **states** impose their own estate or inheritance taxes, frequently with
exclusions far below the federal level. An estate owing nothing federally may owe
substantial state tax — which makes state of domicile a live planning
consideration.

## Step-Up in Basis

Reprising [8.3](../module-08-tax/03-property-annuities.md) because it drives so
many estate decisions.

Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to **fair market value at
the date of death** (or the alternate valuation date, if elected). Appreciation
during the decedent's lifetime **escapes income tax entirely**.

The consequences:

- **Bequeath** low-basis appreciated assets — the gain disappears
- **Gift** cash or high-basis assets during life — no step-up is being sacrificed
- Assets that **don't** receive a step-up: traditional IRAs and retirement
  accounts, annuities, and other income in respect of a decedent (IRD)

In **community property** states, a valuable feature: when one spouse dies, the
**entire** community property interest — both halves — generally receives a
step-up, not just the decedent's half. In common law states, only the decedent's
share steps up.

Note also that basis can step **down** if property has declined below basis, which
argues for realizing such losses during life rather than holding them.

## When to Review an Estate Plan

Estate documents go stale silently. Review triggers:

- **Marriage, divorce, or remarriage** — divorce may revoke some provisions by
  statute but rarely all, and almost never beneficiary designations
- **Birth or adoption** of a child or grandchild
- **Death** of a spouse, beneficiary, executor, trustee, or named guardian
- **Substantial change in wealth** — inheritance, business sale, large gain or loss
- **Relocation to another state**, since laws differ materially on titling,
  community property, and state death taxes
- **Changes in tax law**, particularly to exclusion amounts
- **Acquisition or sale of a business**
- **Health changes** affecting the client or a beneficiary
- Periodic review every few years absent any trigger

Divorce deserves emphasis. Statutes in many states revoke will provisions in favor
of a former spouse, but they generally do **not** reach beneficiary designations on
retirement accounts governed by federal law. Failing to update those forms after
divorce is one of the most common and most consequential estate planning errors.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming a will governs assets that pass by beneficiary designation or titling
- Failing to update beneficiary forms after divorce, death, or a birth
- Naming a minor directly as beneficiary, forcing guardianship proceedings
- Naming the estate as beneficiary, adding probate and losing favorable treatment
- Omitting a residuary clause, producing partial intestacy
- Not naming a guardian for minor children
- Owning out-of-state real property without planning for ancillary probate
- Gifting appreciated assets that would have received a step-up
- Missing the portability election because no tax was owed
- Ignoring state estate and inheritance taxes with low exclusions
- Leaving digital assets and cryptocurrency inaccessible

## Self-Check

**1. Why can a carefully drafted will still fail to control an estate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Will substitutes — beneficiary designations, joint tenancy with survivorship, POD
and TOD registrations, and trusts — pass outside the will and override it. Much
of a typical estate transfers this way, so stale designations defeat the will's
intent regardless of drafting quality.
</details>

**2. State the four main drawbacks of probate.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Cost (court fees, executor commissions, attorney fees), delay (months to years),
publicity (probate records are public), and ancillary probate in each state where
real property is located. Court supervision also reduces flexibility.
</details>

**3. Explain the step-up and its gifting implication.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Property from a decedent takes a basis equal to date-of-death fair market value,
so lifetime appreciation escapes income tax. The implication is to bequeath
low-basis appreciated assets and gift cash or high-basis assets, since gifting
appreciated property carries over the low basis and wastes the step-up.
</details>

**4. What is portability and how is it lost?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It lets a surviving spouse use the deceased spouse's unused federal estate tax
exclusion. It requires a timely-filed estate tax return to elect it — commonly
missed in estates owing no tax, which can prove costly at the survivor's death.
</details>

**5. Why is divorce a particularly dangerous point for beneficiary designations?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Many state statutes revoke will provisions favoring a former spouse, which
creates a false sense that everything updated automatically. Those statutes
generally don't reach retirement account beneficiary designations governed by
federal law, so a former spouse can remain entitled to the account.
</details>

**6. What's distinctive about community property at the first spouse's death?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The entire community property interest — both spouses' halves — generally
receives a step-up in basis, not merely the decedent's share. In common law
states only the decedent's share steps up.
</details>
