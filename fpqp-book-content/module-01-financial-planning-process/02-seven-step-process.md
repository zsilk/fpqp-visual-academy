# 1.2 — Steps in the Personal Financial Planning Process

## Overview

The seven-step process is the profession's standard procedural framework, set out
in the CFP Board's *Practice Standards for the Financial Planning Process*. Its
purpose is to make planning repeatable and defensible: the same disciplined
sequence applied to every client, so nothing important gets skipped because it
was uncomfortable or inconvenient.

Two things about the sequence are worth fixing in mind early. First, the order is
not arbitrary — each step supplies the raw material for the next, and skipping
ahead produces recommendations resting on assumptions nobody verified. Second,
the process is a **loop, not a line**. Step 7 feeds back into Step 1, because
clients' lives change and a plan that isn't revisited quietly goes stale.

## The Seven Steps

### Step 1 — Understanding the client's personal and financial circumstances

Gathering both the quantitative and the qualitative picture.

- **Quantitative**: income, expenses, assets, liabilities, insurance policies,
  tax returns, employee benefits, estate documents, account statements.
- **Qualitative**: health, family relationships, career stability, values, risk
  attitude, expectations about supporting parents or children, what money is
  *for* in this client's mind.

The qualitative half is routinely underweighted and routinely decisive. Two
clients with identical balance sheets may need entirely different plans because
one has a disabled child and the other intends to sell a business in four years.

This step also involves assessing whether the information is complete and
internally consistent, and going back to the client when it isn't. A data-gathering
questionnaire is the usual instrument, supplemented by an interview — the
questionnaire captures facts efficiently, the conversation surfaces the context
that facts alone don't carry.

**Other advisers.** Clients usually already have an attorney, a CPA, an insurance
agent. Identifying them early matters: it establishes who owns which piece of the
plan, prevents contradictory advice, and defines where the paraplanner's role
stops and licensed practice begins.

### Step 2 — Identifying and selecting goals

Goals are elicited, quantified, and — critically — **prioritized**. The planner's
contribution here is not just recording what the client says, but helping them
articulate what they haven't. Clients often present a goal that is really a proxy
for something else ("I want to pay off the mortgage" may mean "I want to feel
secure").

The planner also has to apply reasonable-assumption discipline: if a client's
goals are mutually unachievable given their resources, that must be surfaced now,
not discovered at step 5. Selecting goals is the step where trade-offs become
explicit.

### Step 3 — Analyzing the client's current course of action and potential alternatives

The analytical core. It has two halves:

1. **Evaluate the current course** — if the client changes nothing, do they reach
   their goals? This establishes the baseline and quantifies the gap.
2. **Evaluate alternatives** — identify and compare other courses of action that
   could close it.

Analysis here means running the numbers: retirement needs projections, insurance
needs analysis, debt paydown comparisons, allocation review, tax projections. It
also means assessing the *material advantages and disadvantages* of each option
against the client's goals, risk tolerance, and time horizons — including the
option of doing nothing.

### Step 4 — Developing the financial planning recommendations

Selecting from the analyzed alternatives and forming specific recommendations.
Each one should be traceable: this recommendation, addressing this goal, chosen
over these alternatives, for these reasons. That traceability is what
distinguishes a recommendation from a suggestion, and it's what makes the
recommendation defensible if later questioned.

Recommendations should be prioritized and sequenced. Some are prerequisites for
others — establishing an emergency fund typically precedes aggressive investing,
and disability coverage often matters more than portfolio optimization for a
young earner whose main asset is future income.

### Step 5 — Presenting the recommendations

Communication, not computation. The analysis is worthless if the client doesn't
understand and accept it. This step covers presenting each recommendation, the
reasoning behind it, and the assumptions and risks it depends on — in language
the client can act on.

Expect and invite pushback. Client objections at this stage frequently reveal
information that never surfaced during data gathering, which may send you back to
step 1. That's the loop working correctly, not a failure.

### Step 6 — Implementing the recommendations

A plan that isn't implemented produces no outcome. This step establishes:

- **What** actions are to be taken
- **Who** performs each one — planner, client, attorney, CPA, insurance agent
- **When** each is due

Assigning ownership and deadlines explicitly is what makes implementation
actually happen. Unassigned actions are the most common place plans die.

### Step 7 — Monitoring progress and updating

The plan is reviewed on a defined schedule and revised when circumstances change.
Triggers for an off-cycle review include marriage, divorce, birth, death, job
change, inheritance, serious illness, business sale, relocation to another state,
and significant tax law changes.

Monitoring closes the loop back to step 1. The responsibilities of each party
during monitoring — how often, who initiates, what gets reviewed — should be
agreed in advance rather than assumed.

## Quick Reference

| Step | Name | Output |
|------|------|--------|
| 1 | Understanding circumstances | Complete quantitative and qualitative data set |
| 2 | Identifying and selecting goals | Quantified, prioritized goals |
| 3 | Analyzing current and alternative courses | Gap analysis and viable alternatives |
| 4 | Developing recommendations | Specific, sequenced, justified recommendations |
| 5 | Presenting recommendations | Client understanding and buy-in |
| 6 | Implementing recommendations | Assigned actions with owners and deadlines |
| 7 | Monitoring and updating | Scheduled review; loop back to step 1 |

## Planner Compensation

Compensation model shapes incentives, which is why it belongs in this chapter
rather than being treated as a business detail.

| Model | How paid | Conflict to be aware of |
|-------|----------|-------------------------|
| Fee-only | Client pays directly — hourly, flat, retainer, or % of AUM | AUM fees can discourage recommending debt paydown or annuitization, which reduce assets under management |
| Commission | Paid by product provider on sale | Incentive toward products that pay, and toward transacting |
| Fee-based / fee-and-commission | Combination of both | Client may not understand which hat the adviser is wearing at a given moment |
| Salary | Employer pays | Employer's sales targets may still flow through |

No model is conflict-free. What matters professionally is that conflicts are
disclosed clearly enough that the client can weigh them, and that the client's
interest governs when the two diverge. This connects directly to the fiduciary
material in [1.5](05-ethics-regulation.md).

## Common Pitfalls

- Beginning analysis before data gathering is complete, so conclusions rest on guesses
- Recording goals without prioritizing them, leaving trade-offs unresolved
- Skipping the "current course of action" baseline, which removes the standard of comparison
- Presenting analysis rather than recommendations — the client can't act on a projection
- Leaving implementation steps unassigned
- Treating the plan as a deliverable that ends at step 6

## Self-Check

**1. Why does the process loop rather than terminate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Client circumstances, goals, markets, and tax law all change. Step 7 monitoring
detects those changes and returns to step 1 with updated information, so the plan
stays aligned with reality rather than with conditions that existed on the day it
was written.
</details>

**2. At which step does prioritization of goals occur, and why does it matter there?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Step 2. Prioritization must precede analysis, because when resources can't fund
every goal, the analysis in step 3 needs to know which goals give way.
</details>

**3. A client objects to a recommendation during step 5, revealing they intend to
support an aging parent — something never mentioned. What should happen?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Return to step 1. This is new material information about the client's
circumstances; it changes the data set, likely changes goals and priorities in
step 2, and requires re-analysis. Proceeding to implementation would build on a
picture now known to be incomplete.
</details>

**4. Why is it useful to identify the client's other advisers in step 1?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It clarifies who is responsible for which part of the plan, avoids contradictory
or duplicated advice, enables coordination on items requiring legal or tax
expertise, and marks the boundary of what the paraplanner may appropriately
handle.
</details>
