# 1.4 — Client Behaviors, Communication, and Counseling Principles

## Overview

Plans fail for human reasons far more often than technical ones. The arithmetic
of retirement funding is not hard; getting a client to save consistently, hold an
allocation through a bear market, or confront the possibility of their own
disability is very hard. This chapter covers the psychology behind that gap and
the communication skills used to close it.

The practical framing: technical analysis tells you what the client *should* do,
behavioral understanding tells you what they *will* do, and counseling skill is
how you narrow the distance between the two.

## Cognitive and Emotional Biases

Biases are systematic deviations from rational decision-making. They're
predictable, they affect everyone including the planner, and they can be
partially managed once recognized.

| Bias | What it looks like | Planning consequence |
|------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Overconfidence | Overestimating one's own knowledge or forecasting ability | Excessive trading, concentrated positions, underestimating risk |
| Anchoring | Fixating on a reference number — a purchase price, a market peak | Refusing to sell below what they paid; unrealistic target values |
| Confirmation | Seeking information that supports an existing belief | Dismissing analysis that contradicts a favored holding |
| Loss aversion | Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please | Holding losers too long, selling winners early, excessive conservatism |
| Recency | Overweighting recent events when forecasting | Chasing performance after rallies, abandoning equities after crashes |
| Mental accounting | Treating money differently by arbitrary category | Carrying 22% card debt while holding a low-yield "vacation fund" |
| Herding | Following the crowd | Buying at tops, selling at bottoms |
| Familiarity / home bias | Preferring the known | Overconcentration in employer stock or domestic assets |
| Status quo | Preferring inaction | Never rebalancing; leaving stale beneficiary designations in place |
| Framing | Being swayed by presentation rather than substance | Different decisions on identical options described differently |
| Hindsight | Believing past events were predictable | Unrealistic expectations of the planner's foresight |
| Availability | Judging likelihood by ease of recall | Overinsuring vivid risks, underinsuring statistically likelier ones |

### Working with biases rather than against them

Confronting a bias head-on usually triggers defensiveness. More effective
approaches:

- **Reframe toward the goal.** "Selling at a loss" reframes as "redeploying to
  the allocation that funds your retirement date."
- **Use pre-commitment.** Agreed rebalancing rules and automatic contributions
  bind the calm client against the panicked one.
- **Make the abstract concrete.** Loss aversion softens when the client sees the
  historical cost of missing a recovery.
- **Exploit mental accounting constructively.** Separate labeled accounts for
  distinct goals is technically arbitrary but genuinely improves follow-through.

Note the last one: the goal isn't to eliminate every bias but to prevent the
damaging ones and occasionally harness the harmless ones.

## Attitudes and Values

Beneath biases sit attitudes and values — durable, largely formed before the
client ever met you, and often traceable to family and early experience.

- **Values** are what the client believes matters: security, independence,
  generosity, status, family obligation, freedom.
- **Attitudes** are dispositions toward specific things: debt, risk, insurance,
  inheritance, professional advice.

A client raised through financial instability may hold cash far beyond what
analysis supports. That's not innumeracy — it's a value about security, and
attacking it with a spreadsheet fails. Acknowledging it and planning around it
works better: honor the oversized reserve, and invest the remainder appropriately.

Money scripts — unspoken beliefs like "discussing money is impolite" or "wealth
corrupts" — operate the same way and account for a lot of otherwise puzzling
client resistance.

## Principles of Communication

Communication is a two-way exchange with real potential for distortion at every
step. What the planner intends, what they say, what the client hears, and what
the client understands can all differ.

Practical principles:

- **Match language to the listener.** Jargon signals expertise to peers and
  creates distance with clients. "Sequence-of-returns risk" becomes "a bad market
  in your first few retirement years does more damage than the same market later."
- **Attend to nonverbal signals** — posture, eye contact, tone, hesitation. When
  words and body language disagree, the body language is usually more accurate.
- **Confirm understanding rather than assume it.** "Does that make sense?" reliably
  produces a yes. "Can you tell me how you'd explain this to your spouse?" produces
  information.
- **Watch for barriers**: distraction, emotional load, defensiveness, information
  overload, and the client's reluctance to admit confusion.
- **Respect the couple as two clients.** Partners frequently hold different
  attitudes and goals. Directing communication to only one is a common and
  damaging error.

## Counseling Skills

### Nondirective skills

The planner draws information out without steering it. Dominant in step 1 (data
gathering) and step 2 (goal identification), where the objective is to learn what
the client actually thinks.

| Skill | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Attending | Nonverbal presence — eye contact, open posture, undivided attention | Closing the laptop while the client speaks |
| Active listening | Full attention to content and feeling, without composing a reply | Pausing before responding |
| Open-ended questioning | Questions that can't be answered yes/no | "What would a good retirement look like to you?" |
| Clarifying | Resolving ambiguity | "When you say secure, what amount would feel secure?" |
| Paraphrasing / restating | Reflecting content back in your own words | "So the priority is staying in this house." |
| Reflecting feeling | Naming the emotion beneath the content | "It sounds like the market drop shook your confidence." |
| Summarizing | Consolidating a long exchange into its essentials | "Three things stood out — let me check I've got them." |
| Silence | Allowing a pause so the client fills it | Waiting after a difficult question |

Silence is the most underused of these. Planners rush to fill pauses; the client
was often about to say the most important thing in the meeting.

### Directive skills

The planner actively guides. Dominant in step 5 (presenting recommendations),
where the client needs guidance rather than exploration.

- **Leading** — steering toward a specific topic
- **Advising / recommending** — stating the recommended course and the reasoning
- **Confronting** — respectfully naming a contradiction between stated goals and
  actual behavior ("You've said retiring at 62 is the top priority, and savings
  are running below what that requires — can we look at that?")
- **Informing / educating** — supplying knowledge the client lacks
- **Interpreting** — offering a framework for what the client has described

### Choosing between them

Nondirective early, directive late. Being directive during data gathering
contaminates the information — the client tells you what they think you want to
hear. Being nondirective during the presentation abdicates the planner's role;
the client came for a recommendation, not a survey of possibilities.

## Common Pitfalls

- Presenting analysis to a client who hasn't been heard yet
- Treating resistance as ignorance rather than as information
- Using technical vocabulary as a credibility display
- Directing communication to one member of a couple
- Confusing agreement with understanding
- Filling every silence

## Self-Check

**1. Why use nondirective skills in step 1 and directive skills in step 5?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Step 1 aims to gather accurate, complete information; directing the conversation
biases what the client reveals. Step 5 aims to secure understanding and action on
a specific recommendation, which requires the planner to guide rather than
explore.
</details>

**2. A client refuses to sell a stock that's down 40%, saying they'll sell "when
it gets back to what I paid." Which biases are operating?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Anchoring on the purchase price, which has no bearing on the stock's future
prospects, and loss aversion — realizing the loss feels worse than continuing to
hold. Possibly disposition effect as the combined behavior pattern.
</details>

**3. How does mental accounting explain someone carrying credit card debt while
holding a labeled savings account?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The client treats the two as separate mental buckets rather than as one balance
sheet. Economically, using the savings to retire debt at a much higher rate is
clearly better, but the "vacation fund" is mentally categorized as untouchable.
</details>

**4. Why is a client's oversized cash position not necessarily a knowledge problem?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It may reflect a deeply held value about security, often formed through
experience of instability. Presenting more data won't shift a values-based
position; acknowledging it and planning around it is more effective.
</details>
