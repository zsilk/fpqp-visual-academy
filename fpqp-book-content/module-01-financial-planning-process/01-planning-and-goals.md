# 1.1 — Personal Financial Planning and Financial Goals

## Overview

Personal financial planning is the practice of coordinating a person's whole
financial life toward the outcomes they actually want, rather than managing each
piece in isolation. The distinguishing word is *coordinating*. Anyone can buy a
mutual fund or a term policy; planning is the discipline of making sure the fund,
the policy, the mortgage, the 401(k), and the will all point the same direction
and don't quietly undermine one another.

That coordination is the whole reason the profession exists. A tax move that
wrecks a financial aid application, a life insurance purchase that crowds out the
emergency fund, a Roth conversion that pushes a retiree into a higher Medicare
premium bracket — none of these are visible if you look at one product at a time.
They only show up when someone holds the entire picture at once.

## Key Concepts

### Planning is integrative, not product-driven

A useful mental test: if a recommendation could have been made without knowing
anything else about the client, it probably wasn't planning. Real
recommendations depend on the rest of the picture — the client's tax bracket,
time horizon, dependents, risk tolerance, and existing coverage.

### The core planning areas

The field is conventionally divided into a handful of domains. They overlap
constantly, which is precisely the point.

| Area | Central question |
|------|------------------|
| Cash flow and debt management | Does more come in than goes out, and is borrowing working for or against the client? |
| Risk management and insurance | What events could derail everything, and who bears that cost? |
| Investment planning | How should assets be allocated to fund future goals at tolerable risk? |
| Tax planning | How can the timing, character, and location of income reduce lifetime tax? |
| Retirement planning | Will assets support the client once earned income stops? |
| Estate planning | How does property transfer, to whom, at what cost, and under whose control? |

Education funding and business planning are often treated as their own areas,
though both are really cash flow, tax, and investment questions wearing a
specific hat.

### Goals give the plan its shape

Without goals there is no way to evaluate a recommendation, because there is no
standard to evaluate it against. Goals convert vague wishes ("retire
comfortably") into targets that can be measured, funded, and monitored.

A workable goal specifies four things:

1. **What** — the outcome in concrete terms
2. **How much** — a dollar amount, in today's dollars or inflated
3. **When** — a target date, which sets the time horizon
4. **Priority** — where it ranks when resources are scarce

That last one carries more weight than people expect. Nearly every client has
goals that compete for the same dollars, and the planner's job often isn't to
find more money but to help the client decide what matters most.

### Goals are usually sorted by horizon

- **Short-term** (under ~1 year) — emergency reserve, a vacation, paying off a card
- **Intermediate** (~1–5 years) — a home down payment, a car, a wedding
- **Long-term** (5+ years) — retirement, education funding, legacy goals

Horizon drives strategy directly. Short-horizon money belongs somewhere stable
and liquid because there's no time to recover from a loss; long-horizon money can
accept volatility in exchange for expected return. Mismatching the two — parking
a down payment in equities, or retirement savings in cash — is one of the most
common and costly errors in personal finance.

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Personal financial planning | Coordinating a client's financial resources across all planning areas to achieve stated goals |
| Financial goal | A specific, quantified, time-bound outcome the client wants to fund |
| Time horizon | The interval between now and when a goal must be funded |
| Risk tolerance | The level of volatility or potential loss a client is emotionally willing to accept |
| Risk capacity | The level of loss a client can absorb financially without derailing goals — distinct from tolerance |
| Liquidity | How quickly an asset converts to cash without meaningful loss of value |

Tolerance and capacity are worth keeping separate in your head. A young high
earner may have enormous capacity for risk but panic in a drawdown; a retiree may
be temperamentally fearless but unable to afford a bad sequence of returns. Good
planning respects the lower of the two.

## Worked Example

A client, 34, wants to retire at 65 and buy a home in three years.

- The home down payment has a **three-year** horizon → high-grade short-duration
  instruments, money market, or CDs. Expected return is secondary; certainty of
  principal is the requirement, because a 20% drawdown in year two has no time to
  recover before the money is needed.
- Retirement has a **31-year** horizon → an equity-weighted allocation is
  appropriate. Volatility along the way is tolerable and, given three decades of
  compounding, the return differential dominates.

Same client, same risk tolerance, opposite recommendations — because the horizons
differ. This is the clearest illustration of why goals must be defined before
strategy is chosen.

## Why People Don't Plan

Understanding the resistance matters, because most of a planner's early work is
overcoming it. The recurring reasons:

- **Perceived complexity** — the subject feels like it requires expertise they lack
- **Procrastination** — the cost of delay is invisible and deferred; the effort is immediate
- **Belief that planning is for the wealthy** — precisely backwards, since those with less margin have less room for error
- **Avoidance** — money is entangled with fear, shame, and family history
- **Assumption that a plan already exists** — owning a 401(k) is mistaken for having a plan

Naming these plainly with a client is often more productive than presenting
another projection.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating a product sale as a plan
- Setting goals with no dollar amount or date, which makes progress unmeasurable
- Ignoring inflation when stating a long-horizon goal in today's dollars
- Letting stated risk tolerance override actual risk capacity
- Optimizing one area at the expense of another

## Self-Check

**1. Why is a goal without a date effectively unusable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The date establishes the time horizon, and the horizon drives asset allocation,
required savings rate, and the appropriate level of risk. Without it there's no
way to compute how much must be saved or determine what the money should be
invested in.
</details>

**2. A client says they're comfortable with aggressive investing, but a 25% loss
would force them to delay retirement by a decade. What's the conflict?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Risk tolerance is high but risk capacity is low. Planning should follow capacity
here — the client cannot financially absorb the loss regardless of how they feel
about volatility.
</details>

**3. Give an example of two planning areas producing contradictory advice in isolation.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Many valid answers. One: maximizing pretax retirement contributions is optimal
tax planning, but if it leaves no emergency reserve, a job loss forces an early
withdrawal with penalty and tax — a worse outcome than the deduction was worth.
</details>
