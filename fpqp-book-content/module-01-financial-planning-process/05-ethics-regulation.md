# 1.5 — Ethical and Regulatory Issues for the Financial Planning Professional

## Overview

Financial planning runs on trust, and trust exists because of an enforceable
structure behind it: professional codes of ethics, standards of care, and
securities and insurance regulation. This chapter covers what that structure
requires and, in particular, the difference between the two standards of care a
client might receive — which is the single most consequential distinction in the
chapter.

## Professional Ethics

Codes of ethics across the financial planning profession converge on a common set
of principles. The FPQP® designation carries its own code; the CFP Board's is the
most widely cited. The shared substance:

| Principle | Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|
| Integrity | Honesty in all professional dealings; no subordination to personal gain |
| Objectivity | Intellectual honesty and impartiality in judgment |
| Competence | Maintaining the knowledge and skill to do the work; recognizing limits |
| Fairness | Disclosing conflicts; balancing interests fairly |
| Confidentiality | Protecting client information; disclosing only with consent or legal compulsion |
| Professionalism | Conduct that reflects credit on the profession |
| Diligence | Timely, thorough service and appropriate supervision |

Competence carries an obligation people often miss: it requires *declining* work
you aren't qualified for, and referring it out. Attempting a matter outside your
competence violates the principle even if the outcome happens to be fine.

## Standards of Care

### Fiduciary standard

A fiduciary must act in the client's best interest, placing the client's interests
above their own. Concretely:

- **Duty of loyalty** — the client's interest governs; conflicts must be avoided
  or fully disclosed and managed
- **Duty of care** — act with the care, skill, prudence, and diligence a prudent
  professional would use in the circumstances
- **Duty to follow client instructions** — comply with reasonable and lawful
  directions

Who is held to it: registered investment advisers under the Investment Advisers
Act of 1940, CFP® professionals when providing financial advice under the CFP
Board's standards, and ERISA fiduciaries with respect to retirement plans.

### Suitability standard

A weaker requirement: the recommendation must be *suitable* given the client's
circumstances — appropriate to their objectives, financial situation, and risk
tolerance — but it need not be the best available option for them.

Historically the standard applied to broker-dealers. Regulation Best Interest
(Reg BI), effective 2020, raised the bar for broker-dealer recommendations to
retail customers — requiring them to act in the retail customer's best interest
and not place their own interests ahead — though it remains distinct from the
full fiduciary duty under the Advisers Act.

### Why the distinction matters

Consider two nearly identical funds, one paying the adviser a commission and one
not.

- Under a **suitability** standard, recommending the commission-paying fund can be
  defensible if the fund suits the client.
- Under a **fiduciary** standard, recommending the more expensive fund because it
  pays the adviser is a breach.

The client typically cannot tell which standard they're receiving, which is
exactly why disclosure obligations exist and why it's a fair question for a client
to ask directly.

## Who Regulates What

| Regulator / body | Scope |
|------------------|-------|
| SEC | Investment advisers with roughly $100M+ in AUM; securities markets generally |
| State securities regulators | Investment advisers below the SEC threshold; state-level enforcement |
| FINRA | Self-regulatory organization overseeing broker-dealers and registered representatives |
| State insurance departments | Insurance producers, products, and company solvency |
| CFP Board | Enforcement of its Code and Standards against CFP® certificants |
| DOL | Fiduciary conduct with respect to ERISA-covered retirement plans |

### Key statutes

- **Securities Act of 1933** — registration and disclosure for new issues
- **Securities Exchange Act of 1934** — secondary markets; created the SEC
- **Investment Company Act of 1940** — regulation of investment companies
- **Investment Advisers Act of 1940** — registration and fiduciary duty for advisers
- **Sarbanes-Oxley (2002)**, **Dodd-Frank (2010)** — corporate governance and
  post-crisis reform
- **Gramm-Leach-Bliley (1999)** — privacy notices and safeguarding client information

### Investment adviser registration

The Advisers Act reaches anyone who, for compensation, is in the business of
advising others about securities. Registration requires Form ADV, which is
delivered to clients and functions as the primary disclosure document — covering
services, fees, conflicts, disciplinary history, and business practices.

## Practical Obligations

- **Disclose conflicts of interest** clearly enough that the client can actually
  weigh them. Buried disclosure is not disclosure.
- **Protect confidentiality**, including recordkeeping and data security under
  Gramm-Leach-Bliley.
- **Avoid misrepresenting credentials**, services, or performance. Designation
  misuse is a recurring enforcement issue.
- **Stay within scope.** Practicing law, preparing tax returns, or selling
  securities without the applicable license carries regulatory and, in some cases,
  criminal exposure.
- **Document.** Contemporaneous records of recommendations and their rationale are
  the practical defense in any later dispute.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming all financial professionals owe the same standard of care
- Treating disclosure as satisfied by fine print the client cannot reasonably parse
- Confusing suitability with best interest
- Implying a credential confers authority it doesn't
- Answering legal or tax questions rather than referring them
- Failing to document why a recommendation was made

## Self-Check

**1. State the practical difference between fiduciary and suitability standards.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
A fiduciary must act in the client's best interest, putting the client's interest
above their own. Suitability requires only that the recommendation be appropriate
for the client's circumstances — it may be suitable without being the best
available option, so a higher-cost product that compensates the adviser can
satisfy suitability while breaching fiduciary duty.
</details>

**2. Which statute imposes fiduciary duty on registered investment advisers?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The Investment Advisers Act of 1940.
</details>

**3. Why does the competence principle require declining certain engagements?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Competence obligates the professional to have the knowledge and skill the work
requires, and to recognize where that ends. Taking on work outside one's
competence breaches the principle regardless of how the outcome turns out;
the correct response is referral.
</details>

**4. A planner recommends a fund paying a commission when a cheaper, nearly
identical fund exists. Analyze under both standards.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Under suitability, it may be defensible if the fund fits the client's objectives
and risk profile. Under a fiduciary standard it is a breach of the duty of
loyalty — the adviser's compensation interest was placed ahead of the client's
interest in lower cost. Reg BI would also implicate this for a broker-dealer's
retail recommendation.
</details>

**5. What is Form ADV for?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It's the investment adviser's registration and primary client disclosure
document, covering services, fee structure, conflicts of interest, disciplinary
history, and business practices. It's delivered to clients so they can evaluate
the adviser and its conflicts.
</details>
