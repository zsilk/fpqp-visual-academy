# 1.3 — Knowledge Required for Appropriate Analysis and Plan Creation

## Overview

This chapter functions as a map of the rest of the program. Step 3 of the
planning process — analysis — is only as good as the technical knowledge behind
it, and that knowledge spans every remaining module. The point isn't to master
all of it at once, but to understand what each domain contributes and, just as
importantly, where domains collide.

The recurring theme: a recommendation that is optimal within one domain is
frequently suboptimal once another domain is considered. Competence means seeing
the interaction, and recognizing when a question has crossed into territory
requiring a licensed professional.

## The Domains and What Each Contributes

### Cash management and the use of debt (Module 2)

The foundation, because everything else is funded from cash flow. Requires
reading a statement of financial position and a cash flow statement, evaluating
liquidity and emergency reserves, understanding credit costs and terms, and
knowing when debt is constructive versus corrosive.

Without positive cash flow and adequate reserves, no other recommendation is
sustainable. A client cannot fund retirement, insurance premiums, or education
from a deficit.

### Time value of money (Module 3)

The computational engine underneath nearly every other domain. Future value,
present value, solving for rate, payment, or number of periods. Retirement needs
analysis, education funding, loan comparisons, lease-versus-buy decisions, and
required savings rates are all TVM problems in different clothing.

This is the module where mechanical fluency matters most — the concepts recur
constantly, and slow or error-prone calculation becomes a bottleneck everywhere
else.

### Risk management and property insurance (Modules 4 and 5)

Identifying exposures that could destroy a plan, then deciding how each is
handled — avoided, retained, reduced, or transferred. Covers property, liability,
life, health, disability, and long-term care, plus annuities as a longevity-risk
tool.

Risk management usually precedes wealth accumulation in priority. An uninsured
catastrophic loss undoes years of saving, so a client without disability coverage
or adequate liability limits generally shouldn't be optimizing an equity
allocation yet.

### Investments (Module 6)

Asset classes and their characteristics, the risk/return relationship,
diversification, asset allocation, and the vehicles used to implement it.
Supports every goal that must be funded from invested assets.

Investment decisions can't be made without inputs from other domains: horizon
comes from goals, capacity comes from cash flow and reserves, and after-tax
return depends on tax treatment and account location.

### Retirement planning (Module 7)

Projecting the resources needed to sustain a client once earned income stops, and
the vehicles for getting there — employer plans, IRAs, Social Security, Medicare.
Includes distribution-phase issues: sequence-of-returns risk, longevity risk, and
withdrawal strategy.

Sits at the intersection of TVM, investments, and tax. Contribution decisions are
tax decisions; withdrawal decisions are tax decisions; both are investment
decisions.

### Tax implications (Module 8)

How income is taxed, what reduces it, and how the timing, character, and location
of income can be influenced. Tax is not a standalone area — it modifies the
result of nearly every recommendation in every other domain.

The useful habit is to ask of any recommendation: what is the after-tax outcome?
A pretax comparison between two options is frequently the wrong comparison.

### Estate planning (Module 9)

How property transfers at death or incapacity, to whom, at what cost, and under
whose control. Wills, probate, titling, beneficiary designations, trusts, gifts,
and transfer taxes.

Deserves attention earlier than clients expect. Beneficiary designations and
property titling override a will, cost nothing to review, and are among the most
frequently neglected items in an otherwise sound plan.

## How the Domains Interact

Some of the collisions worth internalizing early:

| Decision | Domains in tension |
|----------|--------------------|
| Maximize pretax retirement contributions | Tax (favorable) vs. cash flow and liquidity (reserve may be inadequate) |
| Pay off a low-rate mortgage early | Cash flow certainty vs. investment opportunity cost vs. lost deduction |
| Buy permanent rather than term life insurance | Risk transfer and tax-deferred cash value vs. cost per dollar of coverage and cash flow |
| Roth conversion in a low-income year | Tax (long-term benefit) vs. retirement (current-year bracket, Medicare premium surcharges) |
| Gift appreciated assets to children | Estate (removes future appreciation) vs. tax (no step-up in basis at death) |
| Hold bonds in a taxable account | Investment (allocation intact) vs. tax (asset location inefficiency) |

None of these have universal answers. They resolve only against a specific
client's goals, bracket, horizon, and priorities — which is exactly why steps 1
and 2 come before step 3.

## Scope of Practice

Knowing the boundary is itself part of the required knowledge. Several areas
demand a licensed professional:

- **Drafting legal documents** — wills, trusts, powers of attorney are the
  attorney's work. A paraplanner may identify the need and gather relevant facts,
  not draft or advise on execution.
- **Preparing or signing tax returns and rendering tax opinions** — the CPA's or
  enrolled agent's work.
- **Selling securities or insurance products** — requires the applicable
  registration or license.
- **Providing individualized investment advice for compensation** — implicates
  investment adviser registration.

The professional response to an out-of-scope question is referral and
coordination, not an approximate answer. Recognizing the boundary early also
supports step 1's identification of the client's other advisers.

## Common Pitfalls

- Optimizing a single domain in isolation and calling it a recommendation
- Comparing options on a pretax basis when the client experiences after-tax results
- Deferring risk management until after accumulation, leaving the plan exposed
- Treating estate planning as a late-life concern and skipping beneficiary review
- Answering a legal or tax question that should have been referred

## Self-Check

**1. Why is cash management treated as foundational relative to the other domains?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Every other recommendation is funded from cash flow. Without positive cash flow
and an adequate emergency reserve, premiums lapse, contributions stop, and
invested assets get liquidated at bad times — so recommendations in other domains
aren't sustainable.
</details>

**2. A client wants to gift appreciated stock to an adult child. Which two domains
conflict, and how?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Estate and tax. Gifting removes the asset and its future appreciation from the
estate, which helps for transfer-tax purposes. But a lifetime gift carries over
the donor's basis, whereas an asset held until death generally receives a
step-up. The child may face a large capital gain that would have been eliminated
by holding.
</details>

**3. Why does time value of money get described as the engine beneath other modules?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Retirement needs, education funding, required savings rates, loan comparisons,
and rate-of-return calculations are all present value / future value problems.
The same handful of operations recurs throughout the curriculum with different
labels.
</details>

**4. A client asks you to draft a simple will since their situation is
straightforward. What's the correct response?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Decline and refer to an attorney. Drafting legal documents is the unauthorized
practice of law regardless of apparent simplicity. The appropriate role is
identifying the need, gathering relevant facts, and coordinating with the
client's attorney.
</details>
