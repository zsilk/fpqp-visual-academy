# 2.2 — Financial Situation Analysis

## Overview

The statements in 2.1 produce raw numbers. Ratios turn them into judgments — they
let you say whether $18,000 in cash is a lot or a little, which depends entirely
on what the client spends each month.

Two habits make ratio work reliable. First, know exactly which inputs each ratio
takes; most errors are input errors, not arithmetic. Second, treat the guideline
figures as starting points to be adjusted for the client's circumstances, never
as pass/fail thresholds.

## Liquidity Ratios

### Basic liquidity ratio

> **Liquid assets ÷ monthly non-discretionary outflows**

Answers: how many months could this client cover expenses if income stopped?

- **Liquid assets** = cash and cash equivalents — checking, savings, money market
  funds, short CDs. Not retirement accounts (penalties and taxes), not invested
  assets subject to market risk, not use assets.
- **Non-discretionary outflows** = what must be paid regardless — housing, food,
  utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, transportation. Not travel,
  dining out, or subscriptions, which would be cut immediately in a real
  emergency.

**Guideline: 3–6 months.**

Adjust upward for: single-income households, commissioned or self-employment
income, specialized occupations with long job searches, poor health, unstable
employers. Adjust downward for: dual stable incomes, substantial accessible
credit, disability coverage already in place.

Using total rather than non-discretionary outflows is the most common error here
and inflates the target.

### Current ratio

> **Current assets ÷ current liabilities**

A quick solvency check on near-term obligations. Below 1.0 means the client can't
cover what's due within a year from what's available within a year.

## Savings Ratio

> **(Savings + employer contributions) ÷ gross income**

Measures whether the client is actually accumulating. Employer match counts — it's
real money going toward the goal.

**Guideline: 10–20%+**, rising with a later start. A client beginning at 25 can
reach retirement on 10–12%; a client beginning at 45 needs substantially more,
which is a direct consequence of the time value of money in Module 3.

Savings for this ratio means money set aside for future goals — retirement
contributions, education funding, taxable investment. Paying down mortgage
principal builds net worth but is usually tracked separately.

## Debt Ratios

### Front-end (housing) ratio

> **Monthly housing costs ÷ monthly gross income**

Housing costs mean PITI — **P**rincipal, **I**nterest, **T**axes, **I**nsurance —
plus HOA dues where applicable. For renters, rent plus renters insurance.

**Guideline: ≤ 28%.**

### Back-end (total debt) ratio

> **Total monthly debt service ÷ monthly gross income**

Total debt service is PITI plus every other recurring debt payment: auto loans,
student loans, credit card minimums, personal loans.

**Guideline: ≤ 36%.**

These two are the ratios mortgage underwriters apply, which is why they use gross
income — lenders standardize on it. A client failing the back-end ratio will have
difficulty qualifying regardless of how comfortable they feel.

### Consumer debt ratio

> **Monthly non-mortgage debt payments ÷ monthly net (take-home) income**

**Guideline: ≤ 20%**, with under 10% preferable.

Note the denominator switch: **net** income, not gross. This ratio asks what
share of spendable income is committed to consumer debt, so it uses the money the
client actually receives. Mixing this up is a frequent error.

### Debt to total assets

> **Total liabilities ÷ total assets**

A leverage measure. Naturally high for young clients with a new mortgage and
should decline steadily with age. A rising ratio in a client's fifties is a
warning sign.

## Gross vs. Net Income

Which one a ratio takes is not arbitrary:

| Uses gross income | Uses net income |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Front-end (housing) ratio | Consumer debt ratio |
| Back-end (total debt) ratio | Budget construction |
| Savings ratio | Discretionary spending analysis |

Gross income appears in the lending ratios because that's the underwriting
convention. Net income appears where the question concerns what the client can
actually spend. Reporting a ratio computed on the wrong base produces a number
that looks plausible and is wrong.

## Worked Example

Continuing the client from 2.1:

- Liquid assets: $18,000 · Non-discretionary monthly outflows: $5,200
- Gross monthly income: $9,500 · Net monthly income: $7,100
- Housing (PITI): $2,400 · Auto loan: $520 · Student loans: $290 · Card minimums: $90
- Monthly savings incl. employer match: $760
- Total liabilities: $251,000 · Total assets: $473,000

| Ratio | Calculation | Result | Guideline | Reading |
|-------|-------------|--------|-----------|---------|
| Basic liquidity | $18,000 ÷ $5,200 | 3.5 months | 3–6 | Low end of range |
| Savings | $760 ÷ $9,500 | 8.0% | 10–20% | Below target |
| Front-end | $2,400 ÷ $9,500 | 25.3% | ≤ 28% | Acceptable |
| Back-end | $3,300 ÷ $9,500 | 34.7% | ≤ 36% | Acceptable, little room |
| Consumer debt | $900 ÷ $7,100 | 12.7% | ≤ 20% | Acceptable |
| Debt to assets | $251,000 ÷ $473,000 | 53.1% | Context | Reasonable mid-career |

**Analysis.** No crisis, but two findings drive the plan: the savings rate is
below target, and the reserve is thin. These compete for the same $350 monthly
surplus identified in 2.1, so prioritization is required — and reserve generally
precedes accumulation, since a client without a cushion ends up funding
emergencies with credit or early withdrawals.

The back-end ratio at 34.7% also means new debt is effectively off the table
until something is retired. The auto loan is the natural first target.

## Common Pitfalls

- Counting retirement accounts as liquid assets
- Using total instead of non-discretionary outflows in the liquidity ratio
- Applying gross income to the consumer debt ratio, which takes net
- Omitting taxes and insurance from housing costs — the ratio takes full PITI
- Excluding employer match from the savings ratio
- Reporting ratios without interpreting them against the client's circumstances

## Self-Check

**1. Why does the liquidity ratio exclude retirement accounts?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
They aren't truly liquid — accessing them before age 59½ generally triggers
income tax plus a 10% penalty, and doing so undermines a separate goal. The ratio
measures resources available for an emergency without damaging the plan.
</details>

**2. Which ratios take gross income, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Front-end (housing), back-end (total debt), and savings ratios. The first two are
lender underwriting conventions standardized on gross income. The consumer debt
ratio is the exception, using net income because it asks what share of spendable
income is committed.
</details>

**3. A client shows a back-end ratio of 41%. What follows?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It exceeds the 36% guideline, so debt service is consuming too much income. The
client will likely struggle to qualify for new credit, and cash flow available
for savings is constrained. The plan should focus on retiring debt before adding
obligations.
</details>

**4. Same 3.5-month reserve for two clients — a salaried government employee and
a self-employed contractor. Same assessment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
No. The government employee has stable income and could reasonably sit at the low
end. The contractor faces variable income and irregular payment timing, arguing
for six months or more. The ratio is identical; the recommendation is not.
</details>
