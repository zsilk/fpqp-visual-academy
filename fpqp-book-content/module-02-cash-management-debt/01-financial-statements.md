# 2.1 — Financial Statements

## Overview

Two statements describe a client's financial condition, and they answer different
questions:

- The **statement of financial position** (personal balance sheet) is a snapshot:
  what the client owns and owes at a single moment.
- The **cash flow statement** is a video: what came in and went out over a period.

A client can look wealthy on one and be in trouble on the other. Someone with a
large home and retirement balance may have negative monthly cash flow and be
serving the gap with credit cards. The reverse also happens — strong income,
nothing accumulated. Both statements are needed because neither alone tells you
whether the client is actually okay.

These are the primary output of step 1 of the planning process and the input to
essentially all analysis in step 3.

## Statement of Financial Position

Structured around one identity:

> **Assets − Liabilities = Net Worth**

Always dated, because it's valid only for that moment. Assets are carried at
**fair market value** — what the asset would sell for today, not what was paid
for it. Liabilities are the **outstanding principal balance** today, not the sum
of remaining payments (which would include unaccrued interest).

### Asset categories

| Category | Contents | Note |
|----------|----------|------|
| Cash and cash equivalents | Checking, savings, money market, CDs under a year, T-bills | The liquidity layer |
| Invested assets | Brokerage holdings, retirement accounts, business interests, investment real estate | Assets held to produce return |
| Use assets (personal use) | Residence, vehicles, furnishings, jewelry | Consumed, not held for return |

The invested/use distinction matters. Use assets generally don't fund goals — a
client isn't going to sell the couch to pay for retirement — so net worth
including a home and cars overstates the resources actually available. Some
analysis is better done on invested assets alone.

### Liability categories

- **Current** — due within a year: card balances, the current portion of loans,
  unpaid taxes
- **Long-term** — mortgages, student loans, remaining auto loan balance

### Ownership and titling

Note how each asset is titled — sole, joint tenancy with right of survivorship,
tenancy in common, community property, trust. Titling doesn't change the net
worth arithmetic, but it determines how the asset transfers at death, which makes
this the same data set estate planning needs. Capturing it once, here, saves
rework in Module 9.

## Cash Flow Statement

Covers a period — usually a month or a year.

> **Inflows − Outflows = Net Cash Flow (surplus or deficit)**

**Inflows**: salary and wages, self-employment income, bonuses, investment income,
rental income, pension and Social Security benefits, alimony.

**Outflows**:

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|------|------------|----------|
| Fixed | Amount doesn't vary period to period | Mortgage or rent, auto loan, insurance premiums |
| Variable | Amount fluctuates with usage or choice | Groceries, utilities, dining, entertainment, clothing |
| Discretionary | Could be reduced or eliminated | Travel, subscriptions, gifts |

Taxes and savings both belong in outflows. Savings is the classic omission —
leaving it out overstates available surplus and hides that the client has no
savings plan at all.

A **surplus** is what funds every recommendation. A **deficit** is the finding
that supersedes almost everything else in a plan, because it means net worth is
eroding.

### Fixed vs. variable and the leverage of each

Only variable and discretionary outflows respond quickly to a budget. Fixed
outflows require a structural change — refinancing, moving, selling a vehicle.
When a client needs to free up cash flow fast, the variable side is where it
happens; when the fixed side is too large a share of income, the problem is
structural and the recommendation is different in kind.

## Supporting Detail

### Footnotes

The statements carry footnotes for anything the numbers alone don't convey:
how an asset is titled, the interest rate and maturity on a liability, the
valuation basis for an illiquid holding, and contingent items. A balance sheet
without footnotes loses most of the information that makes it actionable in
later modules.

### Where the data comes from

Practical sources for step 1 data gathering: recent account and brokerage
statements, mortgage and loan statements, pay stubs, the prior year's tax
return, employee benefits summaries, insurance policy declarations pages, and
credit reports. The tax return is especially efficient — it surfaces income
sources, deductions, and investment activity in one document, often including
items the client forgot to mention.

## Worked Example

Client data:

- Cash and equivalents: $18,000
- Invested assets: $145,000 · Use assets: $310,000
- Current liabilities: $9,000 · Long-term liabilities: $242,000
- Monthly inflows: $9,500 · Monthly outflows: $9,150

Calculations:

- **Total assets** = $18,000 + $145,000 + $310,000 = $473,000
- **Total liabilities** = $9,000 + $242,000 = $251,000
- **Net worth** = $473,000 − $251,000 = **$222,000**
- **Net cash flow** = $9,500 − $9,150 = **+$350/month surplus**

Reading: solvent with a positive but thin surplus — $350/month is what's
available to fund every recommendation in the plan, which is not much. Note also
that use assets are 65% of total assets, so net worth substantially overstates
goal-funding capacity. The invested asset base is $145,000, and that's the figure
that matters for funding future goals.

Whether this client's position is adequate is a question the ratios answer —
see [2.2](02-financial-situation-analysis.md).

## Common Pitfalls

- Recording assets at purchase price or sentimental value instead of fair market value
- Listing the total of remaining loan payments as the liability rather than the principal balance
- Omitting savings from outflows, overstating true surplus
- Reading net worth as available resources when most of it is use assets
- Preparing a balance sheet without a date, making it uninterpretable later
- Dropping footnotes, losing titling and interest rate detail needed downstream

## Self-Check

**1. Why is a liability recorded at outstanding principal rather than the sum of remaining payments?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The remaining payments include interest that hasn't accrued yet. The client's
obligation today is the principal balance — that's what it would take to
extinguish the debt now.
</details>

**2. A client has net worth of $600,000 and a monthly cash flow deficit of $700.
Which finding drives the plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The deficit. Net worth is a snapshot, and a persistent deficit means it's
shrinking — the gap is being funded by drawing down assets or adding debt.
Correcting the deficit precedes almost every other recommendation.
</details>

**3. Why separate invested assets from use assets?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Use assets are consumed rather than held to produce return and generally won't be
liquidated to fund goals. Including them in net worth overstates the resources
actually available for retirement, education, and similar objectives.
</details>

**4. Two clients each hold a 3-month reserve. One is a tenured professor, the
other a commissioned salesperson. Same recommendation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
No. The guideline flexes with income stability and replacement difficulty. The
professor's income is highly stable, so three months may be adequate. The
commissioned earner faces variable income and a potentially longer gap, arguing
for six months or more.
</details>
