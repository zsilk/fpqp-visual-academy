# 2.4 — Budgeting

## Overview

A cash flow statement records what happened. A budget states what *should* happen
going forward. The difference is that a budget is a plan, and so it can be
compared against actual results — which is the entire source of its value.

Budgets fail far more often for behavioral reasons than analytical ones. The
arithmetic is trivial; sustaining the discipline is not. So the design goal isn't
the most precise budget, it's the most sustainable one. A rough budget the client
follows for years beats a meticulous one abandoned in six weeks.

## Creating a Budget

### Step 1 — Establish actual current spending

Start from reality, not from what the client believes they spend. Three months of
bank and card statements is the usual evidence base, and it reliably surprises
people — variable spending is systematically underestimated.

Categorize every outflow as fixed, variable, or discretionary (see
[2.1](01-financial-statements.md)). This is what identifies where change is
actually possible.

### Step 2 — Set the target

Work backward from goals. If the plan requires $700/month toward retirement and
reserve, that's the surplus the budget has to produce. The budget is the
mechanism connecting goals to daily behavior.

### Step 3 — Allocate

Assign a target amount per category. Two useful principles:

- **Pay yourself first.** Treat savings as a fixed obligation funded before
  discretionary spending, not as whatever remains at month end. Nothing ever
  remains at month end. This single reframing does more for savings rates than
  any other budgeting technique.
- **Automate.** Automatic transfers on payday remove the monthly decision
  entirely and neutralize the status quo and present bias that defeat
  intention-based saving.

### Step 4 — Reconcile

If targets exceed income, something gives — reduce spending, increase income, or
lower or defer a goal. Forcing that decision explicitly is the point. Clients who
skip reconciliation end up with a budget that never balanced and a deficit they
didn't choose.

### Common budget frameworks

| Framework | Method | Fits |
|-----------|--------|------|
| Line-item | Target per spending category | Clients wanting detail and control |
| 50/30/20 | 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings and debt paydown | Clients who need simplicity to sustain it |
| Zero-based | Every dollar assigned a job; income − allocations = 0 | Clients with leakage they can't locate |
| Envelope | Physical or digital limits per category | Clients who overspend on variable categories |

Match the framework to the client's temperament, not to elegance. A detail-averse
client handed a 30-category spreadsheet will abandon it.

## The Emergency Fund

Usually the first budget priority, ahead of accelerated debt paydown beyond
minimums and beyond investing past an employer match.

The logic is sequencing: without a reserve, the next unexpected expense goes on a
credit card or forces an early retirement withdrawal, undoing prior progress and
adding cost. The reserve is what makes every other part of the plan durable.

- **Target**: 3–6 months of non-discretionary outflows, adjusted for income
  stability (see [2.2](02-financial-situation-analysis.md))
- **Location**: liquid and stable — high-yield savings or money market. Not
  equities; the reserve is needed precisely when markets are down
- **Interim target**: a starter reserve of $1,000–$2,000 while high-rate debt is
  attacked is a reasonable compromise, since it covers most small shocks without
  prolonging expensive debt

## Implementation

A budget only exists once it changes behavior. What makes that happen:

- **Automate the savings transfer** on payday
- **Separate accounts by purpose** — a distinct account for reserve, another for
  a near-term goal. Technically arbitrary mental accounting, genuinely effective
- **Reduce friction on the desired behavior, add it to the undesired one** —
  remove stored card numbers from shopping sites; keep the reserve at a different
  institution
- **Start with two or three categories**, not twenty. Precision can be added once
  the habit holds
- **Include an allowance for unbudgeted spending.** A budget with no slack breaks
  on the first unplanned dinner and then gets abandoned entirely
- **Involve both partners.** A budget one member of a couple imposes on the other
  reliably fails

## Monitoring

Compare actual against budgeted, on a regular cycle — monthly is typical.

Where variances appear, diagnose before correcting:

| Variance | Likely cause | Response |
|----------|--------------|----------|
| One category consistently over | Target was unrealistic | Adjust the target, offset elsewhere |
| Overage in a single month only | Irregular or one-time expense | Add a sinking fund for periodic costs |
| Overspending across all categories | Income is genuinely insufficient, or the budget was aspirational | Structural change — income, fixed costs, or goals |
| Consistently under | Target set too loosely | Redirect the difference to goals |

**Irregular expenses** are the most common cause of budget failure: insurance
premiums, property taxes, holidays, car maintenance, annual subscriptions. They
aren't monthly, so they're omitted, and then they blow up the month they arrive.
The fix is a **sinking fund** — divide the annual cost by twelve and set it aside
monthly, so the expense is already funded when it lands.

Budgets also need periodic revision. A raise, a new child, a move, or a paid-off
loan all change the underlying picture. This mirrors step 7 of the planning
process: monitor, and update when circumstances change.

## Worked Example

Client from 2.1/2.2 — $9,500 gross, $7,100 net monthly, $350 surplus, 8% savings
rate, 3.5-month reserve.

Goal: reach a 10% savings rate and a 6-month reserve.

Current state:

- Savings including match: $760/month (8.0%)
- To reach 10% of gross: $950/month → **$190/month additional**
- Reserve gap: 6 × $5,200 = $31,200 target, less $18,000 held = **$13,200 needed**

Available surplus is $350. Allocating $190 to savings leaves $160/month toward
the reserve, which closes a $13,200 gap in about 82 months — nearly seven years.
Too slow.

Options to surface for the client:

1. **Reduce variable spending.** A $300/month reduction roughly doubles the pace.
2. **Retire the auto loan.** The $520 payment ends in 14 months, and redirecting
   it closes the reserve gap in about two years thereafter.
3. **Sequence rather than parallel.** Fund the reserve first at the full $350,
   reaching six months in ~38 months, then redirect everything to savings.
4. **Revise the target.** Given dual stable income, four months' reserve may be
   defensible, cutting the gap to $2,800.

Option 2 is the most realistic without lifestyle change, and it illustrates a
general point: a scheduled debt payoff is a future cash flow increase, and
planning for where that money goes *before* it frees up is what prevents it from
being absorbed into spending.

## Common Pitfalls

- Building a budget from what the client believes they spend rather than statements
- Treating savings as the residual instead of a fixed obligation
- Omitting irregular annual expenses, guaranteeing periodic failure
- Too many categories at the start
- No allowance for unplanned spending, making the budget brittle
- One partner constructing the budget unilaterally
- Never revisiting it after a material change in circumstances

## Self-Check

**1. Why does "pay yourself first" outperform saving what's left over?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Spending expands to consume available income, so nothing reliably remains. Making
savings a fixed obligation funded first — ideally automatically — removes the
monthly decision and the behavioral biases that defeat it.
</details>

**2. What's a sinking fund and which failure does it prevent?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Setting aside a monthly fraction of a known irregular expense — insurance
premiums, property taxes, holidays — so it's funded when due. It prevents the
common failure where non-monthly expenses are omitted from the budget and then
break it when they arrive.
</details>

**3. Why does the emergency fund generally precede accelerated debt paydown?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Without a reserve, the next unexpected expense goes on credit or forces an early
retirement withdrawal — reversing the paydown progress and adding cost. The
reserve is what keeps the rest of the plan from unwinding. A small starter
reserve alongside aggressive paydown of high-rate debt is a common compromise.
</details>

**4. A client is over budget in every category every month. Diagnosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Not a discipline problem in one area — either income is genuinely insufficient
for the client's obligations, or the budget was aspirational rather than built
from actual spending. Either way the response is structural: raise income, reduce
fixed costs, or revise goals. Tightening individual category targets won't work.
</details>
