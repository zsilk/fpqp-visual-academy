# 2.5 — Closely Held Business Forms

## Overview

Many planning clients own a business, and the entity form drives consequences
across nearly every other module: how income is taxed, whether personal assets
are exposed to business creditors, what retirement plans are available, how the
interest transfers at death, and what it's worth.

Two questions separate the forms from one another:

1. **Is there liability protection** — can business creditors reach the owner's
   personal assets?
2. **How is income taxed** — does it pass through to the owner's return, or is it
   taxed at the entity level first?

Almost everything else is detail hanging off those two.

## Sole Proprietorship

An unincorporated business with one owner. The default form — no filing is needed
to create one; operating a business without choosing an entity makes you a sole
proprietor.

| Aspect | Treatment |
|--------|-----------|
| Formation | None required beyond local licensing |
| Liability | **Unlimited personal liability** — no separation between owner and business |
| Taxation | Pass-through; reported on Schedule C of the owner's Form 1040 |
| Self-employment tax | Owner pays both halves of Social Security and Medicare |
| Continuity | Terminates at the owner's death |
| Capital | Limited to the owner's resources and personal borrowing capacity |

Simple and cheap, and the liability exposure is the reason planners rarely leave
a client here once the business has meaningful assets or risk. A single lawsuit
reaches the owner's home and savings.

## Partnerships

Two or more owners carrying on a business together.

### General partnership

- Every partner has **unlimited personal liability**, including for the acts of
  other partners within the scope of the business — a significant and
  underappreciated exposure
- Pass-through taxation; the partnership files an information return (Form 1065)
  and issues Schedule K-1s
- Each partner is generally an agent of the partnership and can bind it
- Dissolves on a partner's death or withdrawal absent an agreement providing
  otherwise

### Limited partnership (LP)

- At least one **general partner** with unlimited liability and management
  authority
- One or more **limited partners** whose liability is capped at their investment,
  provided they don't participate in management — participation can forfeit the
  protection
- Pass-through taxation

### Limited liability partnership (LLP)

Common among professional practices. Partners are shielded from liability arising
from other partners' malpractice, while remaining liable for their own.

A written partnership agreement matters enormously — covering profit and loss
allocation, management authority, admission of new partners, and buy-sell terms
on death, disability, or withdrawal. Its absence is where partnership disputes
originate.

## Corporations

A separate legal entity owned by shareholders.

### C corporation

| Aspect | Treatment |
|--------|-----------|
| Liability | Limited to shareholders' investment |
| Taxation | Entity taxed on its income; dividends taxed again to shareholders — **double taxation** |
| Continuity | Perpetual; unaffected by shareholder death |
| Ownership | Unlimited shareholders, multiple classes of stock |
| Capital | Best access — can issue stock publicly |
| Fringe benefits | Broadest range deductible to the corporation |

Double taxation is the defining drawback for closely held businesses, though it's
often mitigated by paying out earnings as reasonable salary, which the
corporation deducts.

### S corporation

A corporation making an election for pass-through treatment.

- Liability protection identical to a C corporation
- **Pass-through taxation** — income flows to shareholders' returns, avoiding
  entity-level tax
- Eligibility restrictions: limited number of shareholders, generally individuals
  who are U.S. citizens or residents, and **only one class of stock**
- A potential self-employment tax advantage: an owner-employee takes reasonable
  salary subject to payroll tax, with remaining distributions not subject to it.
  "Reasonable" is the operative constraint and an audit focus

## Limited Liability Company (LLC)

The form most closely held businesses now choose.

- **Limited liability** for all members
- **Flexible taxation** — by default a single-member LLC is disregarded and taxed
  like a sole proprietorship, and a multi-member LLC like a partnership; either
  can elect corporate or S corporation treatment
- Flexible management — member-managed or manager-managed
- Fewer formalities than a corporation
- Flexible allocation of profits and losses, not strictly tied to ownership
  percentage

An operating agreement serves the same essential role as a partnership agreement.

## Specialized Forms

- **Professional corporation / PC or PLLC** — for licensed professionals, where
  state law restricts ownership and malpractice liability remains personal
- **Family limited partnership (FLP)** — used in estate planning to transfer
  interests to family members, often at valuation discounts for lack of control
  and marketability, while senior members retain management
- **Nonprofit corporation** — organized for exempt purposes; no owners

## Comparison

| | Sole prop | General partnership | LP | C corp | S corp | LLC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Liability protection | No | No | GP: no / LP: yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Entity-level tax | No | No | No | **Yes** | No | Elective |
| Ownership limits | 1 owner | None | None | None | Restricted | None |
| Formation complexity | Lowest | Low | Moderate | Highest | High | Moderate |
| Continuity | Ends at death | Varies | Varies | Perpetual | Perpetual | Varies by agreement |
| Self-employment tax | All net income | On distributive share | GP only | Salary only | Salary only | Depends on election |

## Operating the Closely Held Business

### Financing

| Source | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| Owner capital | No repayment obligation; concentrates the owner's net worth in one asset |
| Retained earnings | Cheapest external-free source; limited by profitability |
| Bank debt | Requires collateral and typically a **personal guarantee**, which reverses the entity's liability protection for that debt |
| SBA-backed loans | Better terms for qualifying small businesses |
| Trade credit | Supplier terms; short-term working capital |
| Equity investors | Capital without repayment, at the cost of control and dilution |

The personal guarantee point is worth emphasizing to clients: forming an LLC does
not protect personal assets against a loan the owner personally guaranteed, which
is most small business bank debt.

### Recurring planning issues

- **Concentration risk.** The business is frequently the client's largest asset
  *and* their income source, so a single event threatens both simultaneously.
- **Liquidity.** Closely held interests are illiquid, which creates problems at
  death when estate taxes or equalization among heirs come due.
- **Succession and buy-sell agreements.** A funded buy-sell — commonly with life
  insurance — establishes who buys the interest, at what price, on death,
  disability, or departure. Absent one, the family may end up in business with
  the surviving owners, or unable to sell at all.
- **Retirement plan selection.** Entity form and employee count drive which plans
  are available — SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, solo 401(k), or a full qualified plan.
- **Commingling.** Mixing personal and business funds jeopardizes liability
  protection and is one of the most common small business errors.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming an entity choice protects against personally guaranteed debt
- Treating an LLC as a tax classification rather than a legal form with elective tax treatment
- Overlooking that a limited partner who participates in management can lose the liability shield
- Operating a partnership without a written agreement
- Commingling personal and business finances
- Ignoring succession planning until the owner's death forces it
- Advising on entity selection rather than coordinating with the client's attorney and CPA

## Self-Check

**1. Two questions distinguish most business forms. What are they?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Whether the form provides limited liability protecting the owner's personal
assets, and whether income is taxed at the entity level or passes through to the
owners' returns.
</details>

**2. What is double taxation and which form has it?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The C corporation. Corporate income is taxed at the entity level, and dividends
distributed to shareholders are taxed again on their personal returns — the same
earnings taxed twice.
</details>

**3. What's the S corporation's potential self-employment tax advantage, and its limit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
An owner-employee pays payroll tax only on reasonable salary; remaining
distributions aren't subject to self-employment tax. The limit is the
reasonableness requirement — setting an artificially low salary to shift income
into distributions is a recognized audit target.
</details>

**4. Why is a funded buy-sell agreement important for a closely held business?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It establishes in advance who purchases a departing or deceased owner's interest
and at what price, and the funding — often life insurance — ensures the money
exists when needed. Without one, heirs may be stuck with an illiquid,
unmarketable interest, or forced into business with the surviving owners.
</details>

**5. A client forms an LLC, then personally guarantees the bank loan. What protection remains?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The LLC still shields personal assets from other business liabilities — tort
claims, trade creditors, other contracts. But the personal guarantee waives that
protection for that specific debt, so the lender can pursue personal assets if
the business defaults.
</details>
