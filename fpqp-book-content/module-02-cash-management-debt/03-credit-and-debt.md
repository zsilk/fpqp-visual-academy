# 2.3 — Credit and Debt

## Overview

Debt is a tool, and like most tools it's neither good nor bad in itself. The
useful distinction is between borrowing that acquires an appreciating or
income-producing asset and borrowing that funds consumption. A mortgage on a
suitable home and a balance carried on restaurant meals are both debt; they do
opposite things to a client's financial position.

This chapter covers what credit is used for, how lenders decide, the major
consumer credit types, the legal protections borrowers have, and what happens
when debt becomes unmanageable.

## Uses and Concerns

### Legitimate uses of credit

- Acquiring assets too large to fund from current cash flow — a home, a vehicle
- Investing in earning capacity — education
- Bridging genuine timing mismatches between income and expense
- Emergencies where liquid reserves are insufficient
- Convenience and transaction record-keeping, when balances are paid in full
- Building a credit history, which affects future borrowing costs

### Concerns

- **Cost.** Interest converts a purchase price into a larger total. Revolving
  credit at 20%+ is among the most expensive money a household can use.
- **Fixed obligation.** Debt service is a fixed outflow that persists after
  income falls. It reduces flexibility precisely when flexibility is needed.
- **Overextension.** Available credit invites spending beyond income, and minimum
  payments make the trajectory hard to perceive.
- **Opportunity cost.** Dollars servicing debt aren't funding goals.
- **Compounding against the borrower.** The same mathematics that builds wealth
  through investing works in reverse on a carried balance.

A concrete illustration: a $5,000 balance at 20% APR, paid at a 2% minimum,
takes decades to retire and costs more in interest than the original purchase.
This is worth computing for clients rather than asserting — the number is
persuasive in a way the warning isn't.

## Qualifying for Credit

### The five Cs

| C | What the lender is assessing |
|---|------------------------------|
| Character | Willingness to repay — credit history, stability, references |
| Capacity | Ability to repay from income — the debt-to-income ratios |
| Capital | Net worth and assets available as a fallback |
| Collateral | The specific asset securing the loan |
| Conditions | Economic environment, loan purpose, and terms |

### Credit reports and scores

Three nationwide bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — compile consumer
credit reports. Reports may differ, since not every creditor reports to all
three.

Credit scores condense the report into a number. The FICO score is the most
widely used, generally ranging 300–850, built from roughly these components:

| Factor | Approximate weight | Practical meaning |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Payment history | ~35% | Late payments do the most damage |
| Amounts owed / utilization | ~30% | Balance relative to available credit; lower is better |
| Length of credit history | ~15% | Older accounts help — closing them can hurt |
| Credit mix | ~10% | Variety of installment and revolving accounts |
| New credit / inquiries | ~10% | Many recent applications suggest distress |

Two practical implications clients often get backwards: closing an old, unused
card can *lower* a score by shortening average history and reducing available
credit, and carrying a small balance is not necessary to build score — paying in
full is fine.

Consumers are entitled to free credit reports from each bureau under federal law.
Reviewing them is a legitimate, no-cost planning recommendation and frequently
surfaces errors or accounts the client didn't know about.

## Types of Consumer Credit

### Mortgages

The largest debt most clients carry.

| Type | Structure | Consideration |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| Fixed-rate | Rate and payment constant for the term | Predictable; higher initial rate |
| Adjustable-rate (ARM) | Rate resets periodically against an index plus margin | Lower initial rate; borrower carries rate risk. Check caps — periodic and lifetime |
| Interest-only | Interest only for an initial period | No principal reduction; payment jumps sharply at recast |
| Balloon | Small payments, large principal due at maturity | Requires refinancing or sale |
| Reverse mortgage | Converts home equity to income for older homeowners | Balance grows; reduces heirs' inheritance |

Related terms worth knowing:

- **Points** — prepaid interest paid at closing to reduce the rate. Worth it only
  if the client holds the loan past the breakeven period.
- **PMI** — private mortgage insurance, generally required below 20% equity.
  Protects the lender, not the borrower, and can typically be removed once
  sufficient equity is reached.
- **Escrow** — lender-held account funding taxes and insurance.
- **Refinancing** — replacing a loan with a new one. Compare the closing costs
  against monthly savings to find the breakeven, and note that resetting to a new
  30-year term can raise total interest even at a lower rate.
- **Home equity loans and HELOCs** — borrowing against equity. Lower rates than
  unsecured debt, but the house is collateral, which converts unsecured risk into
  the risk of foreclosure.

### Credit and debit cards

Credit cards are revolving unsecured credit — a limit, a minimum payment, and
typically a grace period on new purchases if the prior balance was paid in full.
Cash advances usually have no grace period and a higher rate.

Debit cards draw directly on a deposit account. They are not credit, build no
credit history, and carry different fraud liability protections than credit cards
— a distinction worth flagging to clients who use them interchangeably.

### Auto loans

Secured installment debt against a rapidly depreciating asset. The core risk is
being **upside down** — owing more than the vehicle is worth — which is likelier
with long terms and small down payments. Gap insurance addresses the exposure
where a total loss occurs while upside down.

### Student loans

- **Federal** loans carry fixed rates, income-driven repayment options, deferment
  and forbearance, and potential forgiveness programs.
- **Private** loans are underwritten on credit, often variable-rate, with far
  fewer protections.

Student debt is generally not dischargeable in bankruptcy absent an undue
hardship showing, which makes it structurally different from other consumer debt
and argues against refinancing federal loans into private ones without carefully
weighing the protections given up.

## Buying vs. Leasing

### Autos

| | Buying | Leasing |
|---|--------|---------|
| Monthly payment | Higher | Lower |
| Ownership at end | Yes — asset retained | No, unless purchase option exercised |
| Mileage | Unrestricted | Capped, with per-mile overage charges |
| Long-run cost | Lower if held past payoff | Higher if leasing continuously |
| Best fit | Drivers who keep vehicles for many years | Drivers wanting a new vehicle every few years, predictable low mileage |

The general rule: buying wins for clients who hold vehicles well past the loan
term, because the years with no payment are where the savings accumulate.
Perpetual leasing means a permanent car payment.

### Housing

Renting offers mobility, no maintenance obligation, and lower transaction costs.
Owning offers equity accumulation, payment stability under a fixed-rate mortgage,
potential appreciation, and possible tax benefits from mortgage interest and
property tax deductions — though these only help clients who itemize, which is
fewer households since the standard deduction increased.

Ownership carries costs renters don't see: maintenance, property taxes,
insurance, and high transaction costs on both ends. Short expected tenure argues
strongly for renting, since transaction costs alone can exceed several years of
appreciation.

## Consumer Credit Protection

| Law | Protection |
|-----|-----------|
| Truth in Lending Act (TILA) | Uniform disclosure of APR and finance charges; right to rescind certain home-secured loans |
| Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) | Access to your report, right to dispute errors, limits on how long negative items stay |
| Fair Credit Billing Act (FCBA) | Dispute procedures for billing errors; limits liability for unauthorized card charges |
| Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) | Prohibits discrimination in lending on protected bases |
| Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) | Restricts third-party collector conduct — timing, harassment, false statements |
| Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act (FACTA) | Free annual reports; identity theft provisions |
| CARD Act | Limits on rate increases, fees, and marketing to young consumers |

The practical takeaway for a paraplanner: clients in collections frequently don't
know the FDCPA restricts what collectors may do, and clients with report errors
frequently don't know disputes must be investigated under the FCRA.

## Bankruptcy

A legal process for resolving debts that cannot be repaid. It's the last option,
with lasting credit consequences, but it exists for genuine reasons.

| | Chapter 7 | Chapter 13 |
|---|-----------|------------|
| Nature | Liquidation | Reorganization |
| Mechanism | Non-exempt assets sold, eligible debts discharged | Debts repaid under a court-approved 3–5 year plan |
| Eligibility | Means test based on income | Requires regular income; debt limits apply |
| Assets | Non-exempt assets may be lost | Debtor generally retains assets |
| Credit report | Up to 10 years | Up to 7 years |

**Generally not dischargeable**: most student loans, recent tax obligations,
child support and alimony, court fines, and debts from fraud.

Counseling requirements apply before filing. Clients considering bankruptcy
should be referred to a bankruptcy attorney — this is squarely legal practice.

## How Much Debt Is Acceptable?

There's no universal number, but a workable framework:

1. **Ratios first** — is the client within the front-end, back-end, and consumer
   debt guidelines from 2.2?
2. **Purpose** — is the borrowing acquiring something durable, or funding
   consumption?
3. **Rate relative to alternatives** — debt at a rate above expected investment
   return should generally be retired before investing beyond an employer match.
4. **Stability** — can the client service this debt through a period of reduced
   income?
5. **Trajectory** — is total debt rising or falling year over year?

A client can pass every ratio and still be overextended if all the debt is
consumption debt at high rates and the balance is growing.

### Paydown strategies

- **Avalanche** — highest interest rate first. Mathematically optimal; minimizes
  total interest.
- **Snowball** — smallest balance first. Suboptimal arithmetically, but the early
  wins sustain motivation, and a strategy the client actually completes beats one
  they abandon.

Choosing between them is a behavioral judgment, not a mathematical one — see
[1.4](../module-01-financial-planning-process/04-client-behaviors.md).

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating all debt as equivalent regardless of purpose or rate
- Advising a client to close old cards, shortening history and cutting available credit
- Refinancing federal student loans privately without weighing the protections lost
- Comparing a lease payment to a loan payment without accounting for the retained asset
- Assuming mortgage interest produces a tax benefit for a client who takes the standard deduction
- Consolidating unsecured debt into a home equity loan without naming the foreclosure risk
- Advising on bankruptcy rather than referring to counsel

## Self-Check

**1. Why can closing an old unused credit card lower a score?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
It reduces total available credit, raising the utilization ratio (~30% of the
score), and can shorten average account age (~15%). Both move the score down.
</details>

**2. Two clients each carry $30,000 of debt. One is a mortgage at 4%, the other
credit cards at 22%. Same recommendation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
No. The mortgage acquired an appreciating asset at a low rate and may not warrant
accelerated payoff. The card balance is consumption debt at a rate exceeding any
reasonable expected return, so retiring it takes priority over most investing
beyond an employer match.
</details>

**3. What's the risk in consolidating credit card debt into a HELOC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
The rate falls, but unsecured debt becomes secured by the home — nonpayment now
risks foreclosure rather than collection. It also frees up card capacity, and
without a behavior change the client may re-accumulate card balances on top of
the HELOC.
</details>

**4. When does buying an auto clearly beat leasing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
When the client keeps vehicles well beyond the loan term. The savings come from
the payment-free years after payoff. A client who replaces a vehicle every three
years never reaches those years and may find leasing competitive.
</details>

**5. Distinguish Chapter 7 from Chapter 13.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
Chapter 7 is liquidation — non-exempt assets are sold, eligible debts discharged,
subject to a means test, and it remains on the report up to 10 years. Chapter 13
is reorganization — the debtor keeps assets and repays under a court-approved
3–5 year plan, requires regular income, and stays on the report up to 7 years.
</details>
