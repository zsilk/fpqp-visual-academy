Chapter 4.1 — Principles of Risk Management

Before insurance comes a bigger question: what could go wrong, how badly, and who should bear the cost? Insurance is only one of four answers — and it isn't always the right one.

🎮 The 4 ways to handle any risk

🧠
Memory hook: “ARRT” — Avoid, Reduce, Retain, Transfer. Insurance turns risk into ART. 🎨 Three rules resolve most coverage decisions: don't risk more than you can afford to lose; consider the odds; and don't risk a lot for a little (never skip liability coverage to save a small premium).

Low frequencyRetainTransfer — this is what insurance is for

Insurance is economically sensible precisely in the low-frequency, high-severity quadrant — rare enough to keep premiums affordable, severe enough the client can't absorb it. That's also why extended warranties on small electronics are poor value (high frequency, low severity) while liability coverage is essential.

😈 Peril vs. hazard: physical, moral & morale

⚡ Peril

The cause of loss — fire, windstorm, theft, collision.

🧨 Hazard

A condition that increases the likelihood or severity of a peril.

Sort each example into physical, moral, or morale hazard:

⚠️
The classic exam trap: moral vs. morale. Moral = dishonesty (intentional — arson for proceeds, inflating a claim). Morale = carelessness because coverage exists (leaving a car unlocked since it's insured).

✅ What makes a risk insurable

An insurable risk must generally be measurable and determinable, accidental and unintentional, part of a large homogeneous pool, not catastrophic to the insurer, and economically feasible to cover. Insurers pool many similar exposures — individual losses are unpredictable, but aggregate losses across a large pool are quite predictable (the law of large numbers), which is what lets the insurer price coverage at all.

🧠
Insurance covers pure risk (only loss or no-loss outcomes), never speculative risk (where gain is also possible) — which is why no one insures investment losses. Adverse selection — those most likely to suffer a loss are most likely to seek coverage — is why insurers use underwriting, exclusions, and waiting periods.

⚖️ The principle of indemnity

Insurance restores the insured to their pre-loss financial position — no better. The insured should never profit from a loss. Several provisions enforce this:

✅ Replacement cost

Pays what it costs to rebuild/replace new — no depreciation subtracted. Higher payout, higher premium.

📉 Actual cash value (ACV)

Replacement cost minus depreciation. Old roof = smaller check. This is where clients get unpleasantly surprised.

Check yourself — 4.1

Distinguish peril, physical hazard, moral hazard, and morale hazard.
A peril is the cause of loss (fire, theft). A physical hazard is a tangible condition increasing risk (frayed wiring). A moral hazard is dishonesty — intent to cause or exaggerate a loss (arson for proceeds). A morale hazard is carelessness because coverage exists (leaving a car unlocked).
Which quadrant of frequency and severity is insurance designed for, and why?
Low frequency, high severity. Low frequency keeps premiums affordable; high severity means the client couldn't absorb the loss. High-frequency, low-severity risks are cheaper to retain than to insure, since the premium must cover the expected claims plus the insurer's costs.
Why can't speculative risk be insured?
Speculative risk carries the possibility of gain as well as loss. Insurance covers pure risk, where the only outcomes are loss or no loss. Insuring a chance of gain would violate indemnity and create obvious moral hazard.
What is subrogation and which principle does it serve?
After paying a claim, the insurer assumes the insured's right to recover from the at-fault party. It serves the principle of indemnity by preventing the insured from collecting twice for the same loss.
Under an open perils policy, who bears the burden of proof, and why does that make it more valuable?
The insurer, which must demonstrate that an exclusion applies. Under named perils the insured must prove the loss came from a listed peril. Shifting that burden, plus covering everything not excluded, makes open perils substantially broader.

🏠 The 80% rule (coinsurance)

To get FULL replacement-cost payment on a partial loss, insure your home for at least 80% of its replacement cost. Insure less, and claims get scaled down proportionally — an exam-favorite calculation.

Payment = Loss × (Amount carried ÷ (0.80 × Replacement cost))

✅ Case 1 — requirement met

$400,000 replacement cost, required minimum $320,000. Insured for $320,000, partial loss of $60,000.

Paid in full: $60,000

⚠️ Case 2 — under-insured

Same home, insured for only $240,000, same $60,000 loss.

$60,000 × ($240,000 ÷ $320,000) = $45,000

Client absorbs $15,000 plus the deductible — despite carrying $240,000 of coverage on a $60,000 loss.

Match the coverage letters to what they insure.
A — dwelling and attached structures. B — other, detached structures. C — personal property/contents. D — loss of use / additional living expenses. E — personal liability. F — medical payments to others.
A home has a $500,000 replacement cost and is insured for $300,000. A $100,000 partial loss occurs. What does the insurer pay, ignoring the deductible?
Required minimum is 0.80 × $500,000 = $400,000, so the requirement isn't met. Payment = $100,000 × ($300,000 ÷ $400,000) = $75,000. The client absorbs $25,000.
Why is Coverage A set to replacement cost rather than market value?
Market value includes land, which can't be destroyed, and reflects location and market conditions unrelated to rebuilding cost. Insurance is meant to rebuild the structure, so the correct basis is what construction would cost — which may be well above or below market value.
A client owns a $15,000 painting and has $300,000 of Coverage C. Are they covered?
Not fully. Fine art and similar categories carry internal sublimits well below the Coverage C limit, and open perils coverage may not apply. The remedy is a scheduled personal property endorsement listing the appraised item, which also typically broadens perils and may waive the deductible.
Distinguish Coverage E from Coverage F.
Coverage E is personal liability — it responds when the insured is legally liable for injury or damage to others, and includes defense costs generally paid above the limit. Coverage F is medical payments to others — a small no-fault benefit paying a guest's medical expenses regardless of fault, designed to settle minor incidents before they become liability claims.

Chapter 4.3 — Automobile and Umbrella Liability Insurance

Auto insurance is where most households encounter their largest liability exposure. The principle from 4.1 applies directly: don't risk a lot for a little. The premium gap between minimum and substantial liability limits is usually modest — the protection gap is enormous.

⚖️ Tort vs. no-fault & negligence rules

Two liability systems, varying by state. In tort states, the at-fault driver's insurer pays and the injured party may sue. In no-fault states, each driver's own insurer pays their economic losses regardless of fault, with limits on the right to sue.

☔ The umbrella policy

Worked example. A client carries auto liability of 250/500/100 and a $1 million umbrella. They cause an accident resulting in a $900,000 judgment for one injured person.

Auto policy pays its per-person limit: $250,000. Umbrella pays the remainder: $650,000. Client out of pocket: $0. Without the umbrella, the client owes $650,000 personally — from savings, home equity, and garnished future wages.

Umbrella premiums are low relative to the coverage — often a few hundred dollars a year for $1 million — because underlying policies absorb the frequent claims and the umbrella only responds to rare, severe ones. It also often covers personal injury offenses like libel, slander, and defamation that homeowners liability may exclude.

Check yourself — 4.3

Decode split limits of 100/300/50.
$100,000 bodily injury per person, $300,000 bodily injury per accident across all injured people, and $50,000 property damage per accident.
Why should UM/UIM limits match liability limits?
Liability coverage protects other people from the insured's negligence. UM/UIM protects the insured when the at-fault driver has no or inadequate coverage. Since a substantial share of drivers are uninsured or carry minimums, matching the limits means the client is as well protected as the strangers they might injure.
A client owes $28,000 on a car with an ACV of $21,000, and it's totaled. Result?
The insurer pays ACV of $21,000 less the deductible, and the client still owes roughly $7,000 plus the deductible on a car they no longer have. Gap insurance covers this difference.
A client with 250/500/100 auto limits and a $1M umbrella faces a $900,000 judgment to one person. Who pays what?
The auto policy pays its $250,000 per-person limit; the umbrella pays the remaining $650,000. The client pays nothing out of pocket. Without the umbrella they would personally owe $650,000.
Why does a young professional with few assets still benefit from an umbrella?
A judgment reaches future income, not just current assets. Wage garnishment can follow someone for years, so high future earning capacity is itself the asset being protected — and umbrella premiums are low precisely because severe claims are rare.
