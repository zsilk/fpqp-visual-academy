## Introduction (p. 174)
- For most clients, determining the correct levels of **life and health insurance** is one of the most important financial decisions they can make.
- The primary reason for buying **life insurance** is to financially protect a family when a family member dies. Life insurance can also **protect a business**, **transfer wealth** from one generation to another, **provide retirement income**, and serve as a **source of financing**. It is perhaps the most versatile form of insurance.
- **Health, disability, and long-term care** insurance are designed to protect against the risks associated with illness and injury. Many sources of coverage exist, typically including the **government**, **group coverage offered by employers**, and **individual plans**. This module reviews the features and provisions of the various types of insurance and provides guidelines on how much coverage is appropriate.

## Chapter 5.1 - Life Insurance (pp. 175-188) [LO 5-1 through 5-3]

### Why life insurance (p. 175)
- Life insurance helps relieve the financial strain a person's death may cause for surviving family members. The risk of death makes survivors financially vulnerable: the deceased's income ends at death, and for a one-income family the source of financial support ends. In a two-income family, losing one earner's wage can make it impossible to keep up the current standard of living.
- **Survivors** can be a spouse and children, or other loved ones who depend on the income earner - which could include aging parents or a disabled sibling.
- The death of a family member who does NOT have earned income can also create a burden worth insuring against - e.g., the death of a stay-at-home parent who provided day care or homemaking services can strain the widowed working parent's or the children's finances.
- The life insurance **death benefit** can replace a deceased individual's economic contribution to relieve or eliminate a surviving family's financial distress. While still alive, the insured has the peace of mind of knowing survivors are protected. [LO 5-1]

### Group Life Insurance (p. 175)
- **Group life insurance** = a single contract covering an entire group of people. Typically the **policyowner is an employer** and the policy covers the employees or members of the group. It is often provided as part of a complete employee benefit package.
- **Term** life insurance is the most common type of group life policy. An employer can provide up to **$50,000** (face amount) of group term life coverage to an employee **without income tax consequences** to the employee. The **imputed value** of the premium paid for employer-provided coverage in EXCESS of the term premium for a $50,000 face amount IS taxable to the employee.
  - **Example - Victor (p. 175):** Victor's employer, Tahoe Corporation, provides $100,000 of term life insurance for each employee, at an annual premium cost of $350 for Victor. Because the employer can provide up to $50,000 of coverage tax-free, only **half of the $350 premium ($175)** is taxable. Victor pays taxes on $175 even though he did not receive the money directly.
- Some employers offer group policies providing **permanent coverage** (insurance with economic value for more than one year). Group permanent coverage increases in value over the years and **becomes an individual policy at retirement or upon termination of employment**. Permanent coverage may be provided by a traditional **group whole life (ordinary)** policy or **group universal life**.

### Types of Life Insurance and Features (p. 176)
- Life insurance policies have been designed and refined over three centuries. Two basic classes: **term** and **permanent** policies. Policies sold today have the same fundamental structure as those created years ago, but insurers continue to refine their products.

### Term Life Insurance (p. 176)
- The **simplest** form. In its most fundamental form, term life insurance is protection against financial loss due to death occurring during a **specified period (or "term")**. The policy pays the **face amount** to the designated beneficiary if the insured dies during the coverage term. Term insurance offers **pure protection** and has **NO cash value** - all premium dollars go toward the insurance protection, and when the policy period ends, no cash value is paid.
- **Annual renewable term** (also called **one-year term**) is the most elementary form. It covers the insured against death for one year; typically at the end of the period the owner can renew for another year or let it terminate. The **premium increases each year** because the probability of death increases each year (life expectancy decreases with each passing year).
- **Level premium plans:** owners who want coverage for a set period (e.g., five or ten years) can budget a **level premium**, which most insurers offer. Early-year premiums are set ABOVE the amount required for those years' cost of insurance (per actuarial formulas), and the higher cost of insurance in later years is supplemented by the excess premium paid earlier. At the end of the prescribed period, many policies may be renewed at a new, higher level premium for the same number of years. With both annual renewable term and set-period term, the **face amount** (e.g., $100,000) stays the SAME; premiums increase at each renewal, but coverage generally does not change.
- **Decreasing term insurance:** some situations (e.g., covering a mortgage repayment schedule) don't need a level face amount. With decreasing term, the **premium stays the same while coverage decreases**. Because it's most commonly used to cover loan repayment (especially mortgages), the decreasing schedule frequently follows a loan amortization schedule closely - as the loan is repaid, the coverage decreases so it approximately equals the outstanding loan balance. Some planners do NOT recommend decreasing term because, even if the loan balance could be repaid, enough other needs would remain to warrant level coverage. With some companies the premium difference from level term is too small to justify decreasing term; with others it can be more significant.

### Life Insurance Premium (p. 177)
- How does an insurer determine what to charge as a premium? Regardless of type, the factors are fairly consistent - **three main elements** are calculated and bundled:
  - **Mortality** - the relative frequency of deaths in a specific population; the death rate.
  - **Expenses** - insurer operating costs (administrative fees and other costs of running the firm).
  - **Interest** - earnings on premium dollars between the time they are collected and paid out as claims (return on the firm's investment of premiums).
- Mortality charges reflect the odds of dying at a given age. Because the insurer is more likely to pay a death claim as the insured ages, the cost of coverage (especially term) increases each year - a problem for those wanting long-term or lifetime coverage.
- Term premiums can be **prohibitive at older ages**, to the point the cumulative cost approaches the benefit. Term policies aren't offered for 100-year-olds, but if they were, the cost for a $100,000 policy would probably be about $100,000 because life expectancy is so short. If your $50,000 policy's premium bill came due at $10,000, most people did the obvious - terminated the contract right when they really needed coverage. Insurers responded by developing programs allowing people to keep coverage for their **whole life**.

### Whole Life Insurance (pp. 177-178)
- **Whole life** = a permanent policy guaranteed to remain in force for the insured's **entire lifetime** ("whole life"), provided required premiums are paid, or to the policy maturity date. Unlike term (which becomes unaffordable/unobtainable at older ages), whole life is designed to remain in force for life, and **premiums will never increase**.
- The primary difference between term and whole life: whole life has a **cash value** (sometimes called a savings element) built in, resulting in a higher initial premium than term. Actuaries use the three pricing factors to set a higher initial premium at each age, allowing a **level premium** payment.
- A key feature: taxes are **deferred** on the cash value accumulation. In most cases the contract owner pays no income tax on the cash value unless the money is withdrawn (with exceptions), and even then taxes are due only on amounts above and beyond premiums paid. This tax-deferred status makes permanent life popular as **"forced savings"** that can ultimately fund retirement or college expenses.
- Whole life, also known as **straight life** (or **ordinary life**), has a premium that remains **level** throughout the contract, and premiums must be paid for life. It's the most basic, traditional, "plain vanilla" permanent life. It works well for people who have trouble saving, because premiums must be paid to keep the policy in force; over time the individual builds cash value they can either **borrow against** (while keeping the policy in place) or **receive outright** if they terminate the policy.
- On a $100,000 policy, the individual receives $100,000 if there are no loans outstanding. An outstanding $15,000 loan reduces the $100,000 to $85,000 - and the same reduction applies if the insured dies while the policy is in force: the death benefit is reduced by any outstanding loans. The amount available to beneficiaries is reduced if the insured has taken loans. When the insured dies, beneficiaries receive the **face amount less any loans**, NOT the face value PLUS the cash value. (The cash value is what enables the insurance to stay in place for a lifetime.)
  - **Example - Bob (p. 178):** Bob took out a $250,000 whole life policy 20 years ago so his wife and two children would have income replaced by the death benefit. He's paid the same premium every year, and the cash balance grows by at least a minimum **3%/year**. Last year Bob borrowed **$40,000** of the cash value to buy a boat. He pays **8%** interest on the loan; when he repays, 6% of the interest goes back to him and the insurer keeps the other 2% for administrative costs - in effect Bob has a **2% loan, not an 8% loan**. Ironically, Bob dies in a boating accident with **$36,000** of the loan still outstanding. Bob's wife (beneficiary) receives a check for **$214,000** - the $250,000 face amount less the $36,000 loan outstanding.
- A variation of whole life is **limited payment life**. Premiums are typically paid only up until age 65, then the insured has a **paid-up policy** for the rest of their life - attractive because many don't want premium payments in retirement. Limited payment life adjusts the total annual premiums UPWARD from traditional whole life, so the insured has coverage for life but payments are made only for a specified number of years (e.g., 15, 20, or 30) or until a preset time (e.g., age 65, as in a **Life Paid at 65** policy).
- The cash value in most traditional whole life policies is invested **conservatively** in the insurer's **general account** - typically high-grade bonds, high-grade stocks, and real estate. Why so conservative? Primarily for **safety** - policyowners want to be certain the company will exist when it's time to pay benefits. The conservative strategy means lower returns (higher premiums and/or lower cash value increases), but security is a top priority for whole life policyowners.

### Universal Life Insurance (p. 179)
- Universal life (UL), also called **flexible premium adjustable life**, was designed for people who want **flexible premiums and flexible coverage** over their lifetime. UL premiums are flexible, not fixed like whole life. Premiums paid into a UL policy accumulate as interest in the policy's cash value. Monthly interest credited to the cash value is either the guaranteed rate or the current rate, **whichever is higher**. Every month, a term cost of insurance and a monthly administrative fee are taken from the cash value by the insurer. As long as there's enough cash value to cover these monthly expenses, the policy stays in force. Within limits, premiums can be increased or decreased by the policyowner (or even occasionally skipped) - but adequate premiums need to be paid on a regular basis.
- Most UL policies provide a return based on **short-term interest rates**, so they react faster in a rising-rate economy than ordinary life. The same speed applies in a falling market: when rates fall, UL's rate of return falls faster than for ordinary life.
- If the cash value return is better than expected, the owner may be able to delay or skip a scheduled premium payment. UL contracts refer to a **target premium** - the estimated amount needed to keep the policy in force, based on assumed expense and investment return rates. If the target premium isn't sufficient, the owner (wanting to keep the policy in force) must continue regular payments, perhaps with more frequency or a higher amount than the original target. Whenever there's not enough money to pay the cost of insurance and additional premiums aren't made, the policy will **lapse**.
- UL has other pertinent features besides flexible premiums: within limits, a policyowner can increase or decrease the policy **face amount** to match changing needs (increasing coverage may require **evidence of insurability**). Because of UL's **unbundled structure**, the policyowner can see what's happening inside the contract - UL statements identify each area of interest and expense. The policyowner receives an annual report on policy activity, including cash value accumulations, loans, partial withdrawals, and premium payments.

### Equity-Indexed Universal Life Insurance (p. 179)
- Equity-indexed UL = a permanent life policy that allows policyholders to tie accumulation values to a stock market index such as the **Standard & Poor's 500 Index**. Indexed UL typically contains a minimum guaranteed interest rate component along with the indexed account option, giving policyholders the security of fixed UL with the growth potential of the underlying market index.
- Unlike the interest-bearing investments that support most life policies, stocks have no stated rate of return and may lose value. However, the downside protection of equity-indexed UL guarantees that the interest credited to the cash value will **never be below zero**.

### Variable Life Insurance (p. 180)
- Variable life combines the traditional protection and savings functions with the growth potential of investment securities (especially common stocks). The number and type of investment choices vary by company and policy; most companies offer suggested model portfolios to simplify selection.
- A variable life policy includes a guarantee that the death benefit in any given year will **never be less than the initial face amount** of the original policy, while also guaranteeing **fixed premiums** (much like traditional whole life). As long as the required premium is paid, the insurance remains in force regardless of the performance of the insured's selected investments. In other words, the insurance is there as long as premiums are paid, but the **cash value moves up and down with the market**.
- The investment choices are made by the **insured**, not the insurer. They are provided by an open-end investment company, usually identified as a **separate account**, which operates much like a mutual fund. A **prospectus** for this product includes everything found in any mutual fund prospectus, including fund management and administration charges.
  - **Variable Life Separate Accounts (p. 180):** some separate accounts have names similar to popular mutual funds. Understand that most of the time the funds used in variable life policies are NOT the same funds as the outside funds, even if they bear identical names and fund management. Frequently the funds in the variable life separate accounts don't mirror the structure, components, investment instruments, or performance of the similarly named outside mutual funds. Policy expenses account for some of the difference in results; the rest is because they are simply not identical.

### Variable Universal Life Insurance (VUL) (p. 180)
- VUL combines the characteristics of universal life with variable life; sometimes known as **flexible premium variable life**. It combines the **unbundled flexibility** of UL with the **"insured makes the investment selection"** aspect of variable life. However, a VUL policy can **lapse** if the cash value falls below the amount needed to cover the monthly insurance premiums - meaning it does NOT have a guaranteed minimum death benefit.

| | Level Term | Whole Life | Universal Life | Variable Life | Variable Universal Life |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### Term or Permanent Life Insurance? (pp. 181-182)
Factors to consider when deciding between term (temporary) and cash value (permanent) life insurance:
- **The amount of insurance needed:** a client who needs a large amount of coverage would find term provides more coverage for a relatively low premium relative to permanent plans.
- **Duration of need:** if coverage is needed only for a specific period (e.g., until the children graduate college), term may be appropriate. If the need is long-term or until beyond age 65, a cash value policy may be better.
- **Risk tolerance:** variable life is permanent life with an investment feature - the cash value is invested in subaccounts, and because they earn fluctuating rates and death benefits may fluctuate, the policy is "variable." A client willing to take on risk would find variable products more appealing. Term policies are not variable (no cash values). A more conservative client would prefer term if the need is temporary, or whole life for permanent needs because of the known death benefit and premium.
- **The amount of the client's disposable income:** initially term is less expensive for the same face amount than permanent. However, as time passes and the term policy renews at increasing premiums, it eventually costs much more than had the client initially purchased permanent. A client with significant disposable income may prefer a permanent policy to lock in a guaranteed premium level; clients with little disposable income likely prefer term. (p. 182)
- Financial planners should help clients ensure adequate protection. The best solution may not be a simple term-vs-permanent choice but a **combination** of the two, based on the client's disposable income and risk tolerance.
- **Rating Life Insurance from a Company's Perspective (p. 182):** underwriters consider many factors in setting the actual cost. The term **rating** describes what type of risk the underwriter believes the insured is - a nonsmoking person in good health might get a **preferred** rating, while someone who recently had a heart attack might be **"rated up"** and charged higher premiums. Generally, the higher the rating (higher risk), the higher the premium.

### Policy Provisions (pp. 182-185) [LO 5-2]
- Provisions (clauses) protect the interests of one or both parties named in a contract. Life insurance is highly regulated, and policies contain many standard provisions explaining how the policy works and defining parties' rights, privileges, and obligations. Most common provisions (p. 182):
  - The life insurance policy and a copy of the original application constitute the **entire contract**.
  - The **insuring clause** (insuring agreement) sets forth the insurer's promise to pay benefits upon the insured's death.
  - **Misstatement of age** shall be cause for an adjustment in the amount of insurance.
- **Ownership/Assignment (pp. 182-183):** the policyowner may or may not be the insured (e.g., a business may buy a policy on the business owner or a key employee). As long as there's a demonstrable **insurable interest**, almost anyone can own a policy on another's life. Insurable interest prohibits buying a life contract on another's life unless you actually have something to lose if they die (e.g., you can insure your spouse, but typically not an acquaintance). Ownership rights belong to the **owner, not the insured**. The owner may assign or transfer the policy, receive the cash values and dividends, name the beneficiary, or borrow against the policy.
  - **Viatical Agreements (p. 183):** **viatication** = the process whereby an insurance policy on the life of a terminally or chronically ill person is sold to one or more people (usually a business firm known as a **viatical settlement company**). The buyer generally pays **60% to 90%** of the death benefit, depending on the insured's life expectancy. The sale proceeds help the terminally ill person cover the often-extreme health care expenses of terminal illness. Following IRS and SEC rulings, today most viatical arrangements are treated as **investments**, with all implications regarding taxation and related regulations.
- **Grace Period (p. 183):** if the insured doesn't pay the premium on the due date, the policy stays in force for a limited time before coverage lapses. This is the grace period, lasting up to **31 days**. Technically the contract lapses if the premium isn't paid on the due date, but it remains in full force during the grace period. Purpose: prevent **unintentional lapsing**. Example: premium due June 1st, but the insurance remains in effect until June 30th - as long as the premium is received on or before June 30th, the policy remains in force.
- **Beneficiary (pp. 183-184):** the entity (a person, persons, trust, organization, or estate) designated to receive all or part of the proceeds at the insured's death. More than one beneficiary is allowed. A **primary beneficiary** receives the proceeds first; if the primary is unable (can't be found or has died) or unwilling (perhaps due to estate tax complications) to receive proceeds, a **contingent (second) beneficiary** may be named. It's possible and often advisable to have several contingent beneficiaries (secondary, tertiary, and so on).
- **Incontestability Provision (pp. 183-184):** protects the insured. After a life policy has been in effect for **two years**, the company cannot claim that a statement in the application was meant to defraud the insurer. Purpose: give the insurer adequate time to discover material misrepresentation and/or fraud; it also helps prevent untimely lawsuits and claim denials. There's value in an established policy past the contestable period - something to consider when replacing one policy with another.
- **Reinstatement (p. 184):** most lapsed policies can be reinstated, but typically conditionally. The reinstatement provision allows reinstatement within a specified period after lapse (e.g., three years). Generally all overdue premiums must be paid and the insured must provide current **evidence of insurability**. If health has deteriorated, the insured may no longer be insurable and coverage won't be reinstated. If reinstatement is approved, there most likely will be a **new contestable and suicide exclusion period**, specific to the information requested on the reinstatement forms (e.g., a change in health).
- **Suicide Clause (p. 184):** under most circumstances, coverage for suicide is excluded during a specified period following policy inception. The suicide exclusion period is normally **two years** (less in some states). Under this clause, if the insured commits suicide within the stated period after policy issue, the insurer need only repay the cumulative premiums collected plus interest. Once the period passes, suicide is treated as any other death. The exclusion exists primarily to protect insurers against those who buy coverage shortly before a suicide attempt.
- **Policy Loan (p. 184):** the policyowner may borrow from the cash value of a permanent life policy. Technically the policyowner borrows from the insurer, using the policy as collateral; the cash value determines the loan amount available (generally up to **90%** of the cash value). Interest is charged, and if the insured dies, any unpaid loan balance is deducted from policy proceeds before distribution to a beneficiary. In some cases, especially with variable and universal life, an unpaid policy loan plus interest can grow large enough to cause the policy to **lapse**. Handled properly, a policy loan can be a valuable source of income for short-term needs.
- **Policy Dividends (pp. 184-185):** policies that pay dividends are **participating** policies; those that don't are **nonparticipating** policies. Dividends are a **refund of a portion of the premium**, based on the difference between the gross premium charged and the insurer's actual experience. Dividends are **NOT guaranteed** and, because they're a return of premium, are **not taxable**. Options for using dividends:
  - **Cash:** the insurer sends a check for the dividend amount.
  - **Accumulate with interest:** the insurer pays interest on dividends like a bank pays interest on deposits. The dividends aren't taxable, but any **interest earned is taxable** in the year received. The insured can request part or all of the balance at any time.
  - **Reduce/pay premium:** using dividends to help pay the premium may let the insured maintain a policy that might otherwise be cancelled due to cash flow problems. Example: premium $1,500, dividend $300 - the insured pays only $1,200.
  - **Paid-up additions (PUAs):** each annual dividend purchases an additional amount of life insurance. Each PUA builds its own cash value and also earns dividends (think compound interest). The cash value buildup is tax-deferred under the life insurance cash value tax rules. Example: if the PUA amount is another $500 of death benefit, that $500 benefit is fully paid-up for life with no additional premiums due.
  - **One-year term insurance (the "fifth dividend option"):** the dividend is used to purchase an amount equal to the **guaranteed cash value** of the policy, with any remaining dividend allocated to another option. This provides term coverage in the amount of the cash value. If the insured dies with this in force, the beneficiary receives the death benefit PLUS an additional death benefit equal to the cash value.

### How much life insurance is needed? (pp. 185-186) [LO 5-3]
- Recall the four risk management techniques (retention, avoidance, reduction, transfer) and the three rules (don't risk more than you can afford to lose; consider the odds; don't risk a lot for a little). Applying these helps determine how much life insurance is needed.
- Some techniques can be discounted immediately: you cannot **avoid** the risk of death, and you cannot **reduce** it either (you can live carefully but cannot reduce the certainty of death). Except in the broadest sense, you cannot reduce the risk of death by **sharing** it - death is individual and its likelihood can't be reduced by incorporation or group membership. No one knows when death will come (p. 186), only that it will; even a careful, healthy lifestyle is no guarantee of delaying death (though it's recommended anyway to improve quality of life).
- That leaves **two** techniques for the financial risk related to death: **retain** the risk or **transfer** it. When you die, who suffers economic loss? Do you have enough liquid assets to cover final expenses, pay debts and taxes, and provide income for dependents? The odds are 100% you'll face death, and age alone isn't a determinant. As for not risking a lot for a little - how much will it cost survivors to continue their lifestyle after you're gone? With sufficient assets the cost may be negligible; without enough liquid assets it can be quite high.
- For many people, life insurance is the best option for **transferring** the risk of financial loss caused by death, especially premature death. Two basic approaches determine the necessary amount: the **human life value (income-based)** method puts a value on replacing economic worth; the **programming (needs-determination) method** analyzes the potential future need and, applying a present value, determines the required amount of insurance.
- Note: life insurance is a **cash substitute**. The economic need at death is for cash, so dependents can pay final expenses, settle the estate, and continue their lives without adverse financial consequences. The cash can come from existing assets or life insurance proceeds. Most people don't have the required assets, so they use life insurance. As your personal estate grows, you may begin retaining more of the risk yourself; however, it's unlikely anyone with dependents will completely outgrow the need for at least some life insurance.

### Income-Based (Human Life Value) Method (pp. 186-187)
- Per the principle of **human life value**, the purpose of life insurance is to replace an individual's economic value. Five steps:
  1. Estimate future average annual earnings.
  2. From these future earnings, subtract income taxes, Social Security taxes, insurance premiums, and personal living expenses. This shows how much income the rest of the family actually uses - the annual payment needed at the beginning of each year (**PMT**).
  3. Decide how many productive years (**N**) the individual has left (i.e., until retirement).
  4. Determine an appropriate discount rate (**I/YR**) for the future earnings (review time value of money concepts).
  5. Use **annuity due** mode to compute the present value (**PV_AD**).
  - **Learning Activity (p. 187):** average annual earnings $45,000; deduct all taxes, FICA, insurance premiums, and personal living expenses totaling $18,000; assume a 5% discount rate and 30 years to retirement. Set the calculator to **BEG mode, 1 P/YR, C ALL**:
    - $45,000 - $18,000 = **$27,000 PMT**
    - 30, downshift, N (30 compounding periods)
    - 5 I/YR
    - Solve PV_AD = **$435,808.99**
  - So the income-based need is **$435,809**. As long as the beneficiary earns 5% on the proceeds, the calculation makes sense. The **lower the interest rate, the higher the insurance need** - e.g., using a 2% discount rate produces a need of **$616,798**, which is **$180,989 more** than at 5%.

### Needs-Based Method (pp. 187-188)
- The needs-based method determines the needs of the income earner's dependents. While actual needs vary by family and situation, there are **five basic areas** that apply in most situations:
  1. **Final and fixed expenses** (e.g., burial, unreimbursed medical, estate settlement, mortgage elimination, bequests, education).
  2. **Income for the readjustment period** (a gradual adjustment to the family's standard of living - survivors go through a period of mourning and readjustment).
  3. **Dependency period income** (while the children are still at home).
  4. **Life income for the remaining spouse** (after the children are grown).
  5. **Retirement needs** for the surviving spouse.
- After determining needs, find what assets are available from existing sources (other than life insurance). The **difference between the needs and the available assets is the basic insurance need**.
- From a risk management standpoint, regardless of the approach used, it's the **insurance need** that matters most - not the type of insurance. (p. 188)
  - **Example - Harold and Susan (p. 188):** young children; Harold is the breadwinner. Their planner determined an insurance need of **$750,000** (considering the five areas of need and subtracting available assets). Within their budget, the planner offered a choice of a **$750,000 10-year level term policy** or a **$200,000 whole life policy**. With a $750,000 need, if Harold died in a fatal car accident the next day, the family would be taken care of with the term policy; with the whole life policy there would be a **$550,000 shortfall** - a significant difference that would cause the survivors financial struggles. So, within their budget, the **$750,000 10-year level term policy is preferred**. Obviously, if the client could afford $750,000 with either policy the conversation changes, but for many young families there isn't much excess cash. The 10-year term stays active during the important years when the children are young; it will end at 10 years or could be terminated when a permanent policy is preferred and affordable.

## Chapter 5.2 - Health Care Plans and Health Savings Accounts (pp. 189-195) [LO 5-4, 5-5]

### Purpose of Health Insurance (p. 189) [LO 5-4]
- One of the most potentially devastating losses is a significant medical claim. Events such as heart attacks, cancer, strokes, and catastrophic injuries can leave a person or family devastated with enormous medical bills. In retirement planning, health care is one of the top concerns for retirees.
- **Risk Management (p. 189):** health insurance helps manage the risk of medical events. Even with insurance, there can be significant out-of-pocket costs from **deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, and high maximum out-of-pocket limits**. So, in addition to a health plan, individuals and families would be well-advised to also keep enough **cash reserves** to handle a catastrophic medical event. Routine medical issues are relatively inexpensive, but the cumulative cost of doctor visits and prescriptions adds up over a year. Most individual policies now require the insured to pay a significant portion until the deductible is reached, then additional costs until a specified maximum out-of-pocket amount is reached.
- **The Need for Health Insurance (p. 189):** a coordinated plan that includes health insurance AND savings is important. Even without a catastrophic event, health care costs can mount and drain savings. Costs to consumers keep increasing each year as care costs rise, deductibles increase, and out-of-pocket maximums increase. It's often a choice between higher premiums with fewer/smaller bills, or lower premiums and more expensive bills. Coordinating health insurance (protection against large losses) with a cash reserve helps clients manage the out-of-pocket costs health claims generate.

### Policy Features and Benefits (p. 190)
- **Deductibles (p. 190):** works like a homeowners/auto deductible - the amount the insured must pay before the plan pays anything. But health deductibles do NOT apply to every service; preventive/wellness benefits (annual physicals, mammograms, well-baby care) are paid **100%** by the insurer. Another difference: health deductibles are **annual**, not per-incident. Where an auto collision deductible applies to each of three accidents, health claim costs apply **cumulatively** to the deductible. Example: a $2,000 deductible where the first of three claims is over $2,000 means no deductible applies to the remaining claims.
- **Coinsurance (p. 190):** a percentage of expenses paid by the insurer once the deductible is met for covered services. Example: a plan with a $2,000 deductible, then coinsurance of 80% up to a maximum out-of-pocket limit of $6,350. For a $5,000 service: the insured is responsible for the $2,000 deductible; the remaining $3,000 is split 80/20 - insurer pays 80% ($3,000 x .80 = $2,400), insured pays 20% ($3,000 x .20 = $600). With a $5,000 claim the insured is out of pocket **$2,600**, which applies toward the MOOP ($6,350 in this example).
- **Copayments (p. 190):** "copays" - easily confused with coinsurance but quite different. A copayment is a **set amount** the insured pays for a service (e.g., a doctor visit). Depending on the plan, the copay may or may not apply to the annual deductible or coinsurance percentage. It's another cost-sharing feature that varies among plan categories.
- **Maximum Out-of-Pocket Limit (MOOP) (p. 190):** a set amount that varies among plan categories - the dollar amount beyond which the insurer pays **100%** of expenses for covered services. It lets individuals know the most they might have to pay in any given year for health care.
  - **Example - the Smith family (p. 191):** Eric, Lauren, and child Vincent have a group health plan with a **$4,000 family deductible, 80% coinsurance, $40 copay for doctor visits, $100 copay for ER visits, and a $12,900 MOOP**.
    - **February:** Lauren takes Vincent for a checkup - covered 100% as preventive care; nothing applied to the deductible or coinsurance.
    - **March:** Eric falls off a ladder and breaks his arm, incurring $4,500 in costs. The Smiths pay the first $4,000 (family deductible). The insurer pays 80% of the remaining $500 ($400), leaving Eric and Lauren $100 in coinsurance. Total so far for Eric and Lauren = **$4,100**. The family deductible is now met, so further health costs (other than copays) are paid at 80% until the MOOP is satisfied.
    - **July:** Lauren goes to the doctor after not feeling well; she pays a $40 copay (which doesn't apply to coinsurance or the MOOP). The doctor orders tests totaling $3,000. Since the deductible is met, only coinsurance applies (subject to the MOOP): the insurer pays 80% of $3,000 ($2,400), and Eric and Lauren pay the remaining $600 - bringing year-to-date out-of-pocket to **$4,700** ($4,100 + $600).
    - **Diagnosis:** Lauren has leukemia. By December 31 the bills total $58,000, but the treatment worked and she now just takes a daily prescription. The remaining coinsurance payable until the MOOP is reached is paid by Lauren and Eric: they've already paid $4,700, so once they pay an additional **$8,200** in coinsurance they reach their MOOP of **$12,900**. Once they've paid $12,900 out of pocket, the remainder of any medical expenses is paid **entirely by the insurer**.

### The Affordable Care Act (pp. 191-192)
- Due to rising health care costs, large numbers of uninsured people, uneven quality of care, and considerable waste and inefficiency, in 2010 the federal government passed the **Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA)**. Five categories of health care plans are available on the public health insurance exchanges: **Catastrophic, Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum**. These vary based on how the insured and insurer share the costs of care.
- **Consumer Website (p. 192):** the Department of Health and Human Services was required to develop a website to help people evaluate their options; **www.healthcare.gov** was established.
- **Adult Dependent Coverage to Age 26 (p. 192):** children, whether single or married, can remain on their parents' health insurance until **age 26**. Upon turning 26, they are expected (but not required) to obtain their own insurance through an employer or the exchange. Some may consider keeping the parents' plan via **COBRA** if the plan has low out-of-pocket costs and they're currently receiving a lot of care.
- **Consumer Protections (p. 192):** three provisions implemented under the ACA:
  1. Lifetime limits on group health plans were eliminated.
  2. Rescission of policies is prohibited except in the case of fraud.
  3. Denying coverage to children with preexisting conditions - and including preexisting condition exclusions for children - is prohibited.
- **Preventive Care Benefits (p. 192):** certain preventive care must be provided with **no cost-sharing** - annual checkups, mammograms for women, prostate tests and exams for men, and wellness care for children are all included with no out-of-pocket cost.
- **Essential Health Benefits (p. 192):** the **10 essential health benefits** required in ACA policies: (1) Outpatient care; (2) Emergency services; (3) Hospitalization; (4) Maternity and newborn care; (5) Mental health and addiction treatment; (6) Prescription drugs; (7) Rehabilitative services and devices; (8) Lab services; (9) Preventive services; (10) Pediatric services.

### Social Security / Medicare (p. 193)
- **Medicare** = a federal health care program open to persons age **65 or older**, as well as certain fully disabled persons; most U.S. citizens are eligible (because retirees eligible for Social Security retirement benefits are also eligible for Medicare). It has several parts:
  - **Part A** covers hospital expenses (bed and board, operating room costs, lab tests). The Part A premium is normally **free**; however, there are deductibles and copayments for services rendered.
  - **Part B** covers physician and outpatient expenses, with a monthly premium based on income (automatically deducted from Social Security retirement benefits). Part B requires the patient to pay a deductible and **20% of any approved charges**.
  - **Part D** is a prescription drug plan; enrollees pay a monthly premium based on the plan chosen.
- Because Medicare has deductibles and copays, there are **"gaps"** in coverage, so supplemental Medicare plans - known as **Medigap** plans - are available. Under COBRA, a retiree may not be turned down for a Medigap policy as long as they apply within **six months** of enrolling in Medicare after reaching age 65.

### Health Savings Account (HSA) (pp. 193-194) [LO 5-5]
- HSAs allow individuals to pay for current health expenses and save for future qualified medical and retiree health expenses on a **tax-free** basis, usually through pretax payroll contributions. Sometimes employers also contribute.
- Two primary eligibility requirements: the individual must be covered by a **high deductible health plan (HDHP)** and must **not** be covered by any other health insurance.
- **High deductible plans that qualify for HSA (p. 193):** an HDHP for **2025** has a minimum deductible of **$1,650** (individual) or **$3,300** (family). Annual out-of-pocket expenses cannot exceed **$8,300** (individual) or **$16,600** (family). These amounts are indexed and adjusted for inflation each year.
- **Contributions** can be made by the employer, the individual, or both. Employer contributions are **not taxable** to the employee; individual contributions are an **"above-the-line" deduction**. The maximum annual contribution (**2025**) is **$4,300** for individuals and **$8,550** for family coverage. Individuals **age 55 and older** can make **catch-up contributions of $1,000** (not indexed for inflation). Contribution limits, deductibles, and out-of-pocket amounts are adjusted annually for inflation.
- HSA contributions are deposited into an interest-bearing account and, depending on the custodian, may also be invested in mutual funds (covered in Module 6). Funds can accumulate, and any amounts not spent during the year grow **tax-deferred** for future use (such as during retirement).
- **Qualifications and penalties (p. 194):** if a person becomes eligible to contribute at any point during a year, they can make the **entire year's contribution** (not just a percentage), but they must remain eligible for the following **12 months**. A limited, **one-time, tax-free trustee-to-trustee transfer** from an IRA into an HSA is allowed. If funds are used before age 65 for something other than qualified medical expenses, there's a **20% penalty** on top of ordinary income tax. At age **65** the 20% penalty goes away, but income tax would still be owed on amounts used for nonqualified medical expenses. Contributions to an HSA **cannot be made once an individual starts Medicare** (typically age 65).
- Expenses paid by HSA funds must be IRS-qualified health care expenses: deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, prescription costs, and certain over-the-counter medications and treatments. The **total** amount to be contributed for the year is available on the **first day of coverage**, even though contributions haven't yet added up to that amount. Usually the fund owner receives a **debit card** that can only be used for qualified expenses.

### Health Reimbursement Arrangement (HRA) (p. 194)
- HRAs are **employer-funded only**. Like HSAs, they are subject to use strictly for health care expenses. HRAs may be used for current expenses, or restricted to use only at retirement as part of a plan to pay for post-retirement health care expenses (again, funded by the employer).

### Flexible Spending Account (FSA) (p. 194)
- FSAs are somewhat like HSAs - they let employees with health insurance set aside money for IRS-qualified health care costs. One major difference: a high deductible plan is **NOT necessary** to qualify for an FSA - anyone can contribute to and use one. As with an HSA, employers may also contribute, and a debit card is used for qualifying expenses.
- The big difference between FSAs and HSAs (besides qualification): funds in an FSA must be used **within the year of coverage** (though, depending on the employer, the owner generally has until **March 15** of the next year to use the funds and pay with the account or submit receipts). Because of this, an enrollee must carefully estimate how much they'll spend in a calendar year and put no more than that in the FSA. Any amounts not used in the year covered (by March 15 of the following year) are generally **lost** ("use it or lose it"). Like the HSA, the total expected annual contribution is available on the first day of coverage. Additionally, an individual with an HSA may also be eligible to open an FSA, but only for **limited use** (usually dental and/or vision expenses only).

### Table 5.2 - Comparison of HSAs and FSAs (p. 195)
| | HSA | FSA |
|---|---|---|
| **Qualification** | Must have a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) | Must have a health plan, with no other eligibility requirements |
| **Contribution limit** | 2025 contributions capped at $4,300 for individuals or $8,550 for families | 2025 contributions capped at $3,300 |
| **Changing contribution amounts** | Changes can be made at any time during the year | Must decide ahead how much to contribute, decided at open enrollment; changes only with a change in employment or family status |
| **Amount of time to use the funds** | Unused balances roll over into the next year | Generally only for one year; any unused balance is not retained |
| **Available balance** | Total expected contribution amount may be used on day one before contributions are complete | Total expected contribution amount may be used on day one before contributions are complete |
| **Employer** | Can be held even if employment changes | In most cases an FSA is lost with a job change; might have an option to be held if COBRA is used after leaving employment |
| **Taxes** | Contributions are tax-deductible and often taken out of pay pretax; growth and distributions are tax-free | Contributions are generally pretax, and distributions are untaxed |

## Chapter 5.3 - Disability Income Insurance (p. 196+) [LO 5-6] - PARTIAL

### Overview (p. 196)
- There probably is not a more significant risk management concern than a **loss of income due to a disability**. In fact, until about age 55, you are almost **twice as likely to suffer a long-term disability as death**. The income loss from a wage earner's disability can be financially devastating because, while income stops, expenses continue or may even increase. **Disability income insurance** was developed to cope with this - it makes **monthly payments** to the insured to replace part of the income lost due to disability.
- Besides individual coverage from commercial insurers, disability income benefits can come from the federal government, some state and municipal governments, Social Security, workers' compensation, employers (through group plans, sick days, and self-insurance), and various other organizations (service groups, churches) on a specialized basis. Some benefits are available at no cost (other than taxes or dues), but most involve a premium.
- **General tax note (p. 196):** when premiums are paid with **after-tax** dollars, benefits are received **free of income tax**. When premiums are paid by the employer or with **pretax** dollars (e.g., through an employer's flexible spending plan), benefits are generally **taxable** to the recipient - either fully or in part on a pro rata basis (partial taxation occurs when part of the premium is paid with pretax dollars and the other part with after-tax dollars).
  - **Example - Barney and Betty (p. 196):** Barney has a disability policy and has paid premiums over the years with **after-tax** dollars. He becomes disabled and starts collecting a $3,000 monthly benefit - which he receives **tax-free** because the premiums were paid with after-tax dollars. Betty's disability policy is provided by her company, which pays the premiums with **pretax** dollars (taking a deduction for premiums paid on behalf of employees). Betty becomes disabled and starts collecting $3,000/month - and must pay **income taxes** on her $3,000 monthly benefit.

- **Government:** the two primary forms of government-sponsored or government-supervised disability income coverage are **workers' compensation** and **Social Security**. State-sponsored nonoccupational disability income programs also cover workers in several states. In most cases, though, disability income coverage must come from other sources.
