## Introduction (pp. 46-47)
- Opens with a road-trip analogy: driving cross-country without maps/GPS leaves you lost; with planning tools you can gauge progress and make course corrections. Personal financial statements are the traveler's "maps" - without them clients can't know if they're progressing toward goals or how to get back on track after setbacks.
- The two basic financial statements in financial planning: the **statement of financial position (personal balance sheet)** and the **cash flow statement**.
- Cash flow management: the only no-exceptions way to avoid "too much month left at the end of the money" is to **spend less than you earn**; still, most people sometimes spend more than they earn - historically people borrow. The *abuse* of debt causes serious trouble; prudent *use* of debt can significantly aid financial goals.
- Module covers: personal financial statements and their analysis; cash flow management emphasizing sensible debt usage; appropriate uses of debt, debt issues, and debt types; evaluating whether debt levels are acceptable and remedies for misuse; consumer-credit legislation; buying vs. leasing; budgets; and closely held business forms (sole proprietorships, partnerships, corporations, LLCs).

## Chapter 2.1 - Financial Statements (pp. 48-55) [LO 2-1, 2-2]

### Overview (p. 48)
- Planners, bankers, attorneys, investment specialists, accountants, and insurance agents all use the same basic client financial information; detail and presentation vary by purpose. Formats vary in practice; the course requires familiarity with the formats presented.
- **Statement of financial position (personal balance sheet):** assets and liabilities **on a given date**; determines current net worth = assets (owned) minus liabilities (owed).
- **Cash flow statement:** records inflows (e.g., salary, investment income) and outflows (e.g., loan and utility payments) **over a period** (usually a year); shows positive or negative net flow. The two statements are interconnected and together tell the story of a client's financial status.

### Statement of financial position (pp. 48-51)
- **Table 2.1 (p. 49)** presents an example family's balance sheet: assets grouped as Cash/Cash Equivalents (checking, credit union savings, money market fund, life insurance cash value), Invested Assets (stock portfolio, mutual fund, IRA, 401(k)), and Use Assets (home, automobile, personal property, coin collection); liabilities (credit card, auto loan, student loan, mortgage); example totals: assets $851,830, liabilities $321,450, **net worth $530,380**.
- Think of it as a **"snapshot" as of a given date** - values change with each new snapshot. Analogy: like a blood pressure reading, one statement doesn't forecast the future; successive statements reveal trends. (p. 49)
- Core formulas (p. 50): **Assets - Liabilities = Net Worth**, equivalently **Assets = Liabilities + Net Worth**.
- Transactions can change net worth or not (p. 50):
  - Example gist (Table 2.2): Jorge pays for a $4,000 vacation with $1,000 cash + $3,000 credit card - assets down $1,000, liabilities up $3,000, **net worth falls $4,000** (liability added with no offsetting asset).
  - Example gist (Table 2.3): Tariq buys a $100,000 airplane with $40,000 cash + $60,000 financed - use assets +$100,000, cash -$40,000, liabilities +$60,000, **net worth unchanged** (ignoring interest).
- **Assets - three categories (pp. 50-51):**
  1. Cash/cash equivalents (liquid): readily convertible to cash with little/no loss of principal - checking/savings, CDs, money market funds.
  2. Invested assets: stocks, bonds, mutual funds, vested retirement plan amounts, real estate, similar.
  3. Use assets: property not readily available to meet financial objectives - residence, autos, boats, personal effects (furnishings, clothing, jewelry).
  - Categorization can depend on intent (a coin collection kept for display = use asset; bought to sell at a profit = invested asset). (pp. 50-51)
  - Assets are shown at **current fair market value (FMV)**: what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller, both acting rationally, free of undue pressure, with reasonable knowledge of relevant facts. (p. 51)
  - Estate-planning ownership tags often used on statements (p. 51): **S1/S2** (property wholly owned by spouse 1 or 2), **JTWROS** (joint tenancy with right of survivorship between spouses), **TC** (tenants in common), **CP** (community property); details in Module 9.
- **Liabilities (p. 51):** any debt (mortgage, auto loans, credit cards). Categorized **short term** (payable in full within one year) or **long term** (more than one year); listed in order due; amount = outstanding balance as of the statement date; if delinquent, add overdue amounts plus accrued interest to the balance.
- **Net worth (p. 51):** assets minus liabilities; fluctuates between statements; comparisons over time reveal progress toward goals. Note: net worth may drop significantly after subtracting home equity, personal property, and retirement benefits - these three often represent the majority of an individual's net worth.
- **Footnotes (p. 52):** important component of all personal financial statements - they explain items and reveal values/circumstances not disclosed elsewhere (e.g., an expected inheritance or pending lawsuit affecting future assets/liabilities).
- **"Where to Find It" (p. 52):** table of common information sources - bank records/apps/checkbooks/tax records (cash); brokerage and fund statements, retirement statements, 1099s, K-1s, property tax documents, comparable-sale records (investments); pension administrator/HR (vested pensions); mortgage companies, tax invoices, agents, public records (residences); kbb.com/Edmunds and lenders (vehicles); professional appraisers, insurance agencies, auction houses (other use assets); current statements and account apps (credit cards/loans); mortgage/lending companies (mortgages); contacting the creditor (other debt).

### Cash flow statement (pp. 52-55)
- Reveals cash receipts and disbursements over a **past period** (usually one year): all income sources and patterns of spending, saving, investing - "the moving pictures between the snapshots." (p. 52)
- **Table 2.4 (p. 53)** example family: inflows (gross salaries, dividends, interest, trust payment) total $158,820; outflows = savings & investments ($15,000) + fixed outflows ($42,240: mortgage, home insurance/taxes, auto loan, insurance premiums, student loan) + variable outflows ($101,030: taxes, food, transportation, clothing/personal care, entertainment/vacations, medical/dental, utilities/household, charity, miscellaneous) = $158,270; **net inflows $550**. Footnotes clarify items (e.g., IRA contributions within savings; principal-and-interest notes; FICA and income taxes).
- **Inflows (pp. 53-54)** include all income: gross salaries/wages; Social Security; pension and other retirement income (e.g., annuities); interest and dividends; rental income; loan proceeds; withdrawals from savings; tax refunds; asset liquidation proceeds; any other income (grants, trusts, gifts, allowances, court-ordered alimony/child support if payee).
- **Outflows (p. 54):** three groups - (1) savings deposits and amounts invested, (2) fixed outflows, (3) variable outflows. *Fixed* = predictable, recurring, little individual control; *variable* = greater personal control.
  - **Savings & investments:** planned savings usually head the outflow list; rule of thumb - allocate **at least 10% of disposable income to savings**; builds the "pay yourself first" habit.
  - **Fixed outflows:** housing (rent/mortgage), insurance premiums, loan payments, court-ordered child support/alimony (if payer).
  - **Variable outflows:** out-of-pocket medical/dental, childcare, food/personal care, retirement account contributions, utilities, entertainment, vacations, taxes, miscellaneous (postage, gasoline, education - anything money is spent on).
- Distinction (p. 55): the mortgage **payment** is a cash flow item; the mortgage **balance** is a liability on the statement of financial position.
- **Taxes (p. 55):** placement opinions vary; they can fluctuate year to year, so **this course treats taxes as a variable outflow**.
- **Net inflow or outflow (p. 55):** total inflows minus total outflows; can be positive, negative (shown in parentheses), or zero; reveals spending habits and whether surplus can go to additional savings/investments.

## Chapter 2.2 - Financial Situation Analysis (pp. 56-60) [LO 2-3, 2-4]

- Personal financial statements help identify clients' financial strengths and weaknesses and gauge progress toward already-established goals.
- **Table 2.5 - Items for Evaluation (p. 56):**
  - **Emergency fund:** Is there one? Adequate for the circumstances? If inadequate, can net inflow or other liquid assets fund it?
  - **Level of debt:** How does current debt relate to the three debt ratios (discussion to follow)? What debt type (consumer, long term)? Can cash flow or asset liquidation accelerate payments or retire the debt?
  - **Level of savings:** How much liquidity is there (discussion to follow)? Are savings adequate for client goals?
  - **Diversification of assets:** Are assets allocated properly to accomplish goals?
  - **Planning for retirement:** Are assets being invested for retirement? Participation in employer-match plans if available? Are invested amounts adequate? Are funds available for additional retirement investing if needed?
  - **Tax issues:** Amounts of taxable investments? Can tax strategies improve investments' tax status? Deposits into tax-deferred retirement plans? Funds available for gifting, if appropriate? Is property titled properly for estate planning goals?

### Financial Ratios (pp. 57-60)
- Effective analysis relies on recognized, objective, numerical benchmark ratios. No need to memorize formulas - the final exam provides a formula sheet - but you MUST know what each ratio measures and what percentages are acceptable. (p. 57)

#### Basic liquidity ratio (pp. 57-58)
- **Liquidity** = an asset's ability to be converted to cash quickly and easily with little or no loss of principal; distinct from **marketability** (the ease of buying/selling an asset at any price). (p. 57)
- Measures the **number of months** a household could keep paying expenses from existing cash/cash equivalents after a **total loss of income**. (p. 57)
- Formula: total cash & cash equivalents / [(annual living expenses - savings & investments - taxes) / 12]. Savings and taxes are excluded because both would stop if income stopped. (p. 57)
- Example family (p. 57): $158,270 annual expenses - $15,000 savings/investments - $20,300 taxes = $122,970 net annual expenses; / 12 = $10,247.50 needed monthly. Cash assets $54,550 / $10,247.50 = **5.32 months** of expenses covered.
- Guideline: emergency fund of **3-6 months' expenses**. 3-month benchmark fits a dual-income couple both working full-time (one income continues if the other stops); 6-month benchmark fits a single earner or one-working-spouse household. (p. 57)
- Emergency funds take time to build - small, consistent starts pay off, and budgeting builds lifelong savings habits. The fund is for job losses AND other emergencies (major appliance replacement, costly car repairs); cash beats high-interest credit card debt. (p. 58)

#### Savings ratio (p. 58)
- Measures the **% of gross income reserved for future consumption**; **10% or higher** is considered appropriate.
- Formula: savings & investments / total income. Example family: $15,000 / $158,820 = **9.44%** - near the benchmark, but 10% is a MINIMUM; planners should encourage more.

#### Debt-to-income ratios (pp. 58-60)
- **Front-end ratio (pp. 58-59):** annual housing costs / annual gross income; should generally **not exceed 28%**. Mortgage institutions often use it to size the maximum loan they'll offer. Housing costs = **PITI**: **p**rincipal, **i**nterest, property **t**axes, homeowners **i**nsurance. Example: $24,500 P&I + $6,000 taxes/insurance = $30,500 PITI; $30,500 / $158,820 = **19.20%**, well below 28%. Caution: qualifying for a bigger mortgage doesn't mean the family should take it - with low net cash inflows, a larger payment would force permanent cuts to variable outflows.
- **Back-end ratio (p. 59):** annual payments on ALL fixed consumer + mortgage debts / annual gross income (income includes interest/dividends); shows the % of gross income consumed by debt repayment; **36% or lower** = adequate income for repayments. Example: $39,860 total repayments ($24,500 mortgage P&I + $6,000 home insurance/taxes + $5,760 auto loan + $3,600 student loans) / $158,820 = **25.10%**. Credit card payments would count here too (example family has none).
- **Consumer ratio (pp. 59-60):** annual NONmortgage (consumer) debt payments / annual **net** income. Net income = gross income - income taxes and Social Security taxes. Example: net income = $158,820 - $20,300 = $138,520; nonmortgage repayments = $5,760 auto + $3,600 student = $9,360; ratio = **6.76%**. **20% or lower is healthy**; above 20% warns of excessive nonmortgage debt.
- **Why gross vs. net (p. 60):** front-end and back-end ratios use gross (pre-tax) income because mortgage interest gets tax-favored treatment; consumer debt gets no tax favor, so the consumer ratio uses net (after-tax) income.
- **Table 2.6 - Debt Management Guidelines (p. 60):**

| Ratio | Debt considered | Guideline |
|---|---|---|
| Front-end | Housing costs (PITI) | no more than 28% of annual **gross** income |
| Back-end | Total debt | no more than 36% of annual **gross** income |
| Consumer | Nonmortgage (consumer) debt | no more than 20% of annual **net** income |

## Chapter 2.3 - Credit and Debt (pp. 61-77) [LO 2-5, 2-6, 2-7]

### Chapter intro (p. 61)
- Personal expenses sometimes exceed income, often unexpectedly (e.g., medical bills); shortfalls are covered from savings or borrowing. Borrowing is easy today - obtainable credit, widespread cards, relatively low rates.
- Debt cuts both ways: borrowing to buy a home is generally wise (the only path to ownership for most); impulsive, unnecessary credit purchases create unmanageable debt.
- Chapter covers: uses of credit, credit issues, qualifying, types of credit, buying vs. leasing, credit legislation, and appropriate debt levels.

### The uses of credit (pp. 61-62)
- **Credit** = receiving goods, services, or money in exchange for a promise to pay in the future **with interest**; buying on credit = incurring a debt by effectively borrowing the purchase price. (p. 61)
- Why people use it: convenience; monthly statements list purchases; avoids carrying cash (theft risk); easier online shopping; some transactions (car rentals) essentially require a card. (p. 61)
- Credit lets you use an item sooner, and can even "save money" when prices are rising rapidly or an item is on deep sale - but only if you account for interest. Example: a $1,000 item that will cost $1,050 next year seems cheaper bought on credit now, but at 12% simple interest (lower than many card rates) you'd repay $1,120 total - credit costs money. (pp. 61-62)
- Cards as an emergency backstop: even with a cash reserve, back-to-back emergencies (new tires paid in cash, then a major transmission failure a week later on the household's only car) can force credit use. (p. 62)

### Credit concerns (pp. 62-63)
- **Overspending** is the major downside: credit lets purchases far outrun the ability to repay, and using credit surrenders FUTURE income. In the extreme, nearly all future income goes to debt service and bankruptcy becomes the only likely solution. In a survey of consumers who filed bankruptcy 2013-2016 (most recent stats), **over 44%** agreed that living/spending beyond their means contributed. (p. 62)
- It's easy to lose track of credit spending: at-risk users should record every credit purchase (amount, cost, repayment terms). Paying $50 cash at a restaurant is far more eye-opening than swiping a card. (p. 62)
- **The cost of credit (p. 62):** a common mistake is checking only whether the monthly payment fits, ignoring what the credit costs. Creditors charge interest + fees to cover default risk and their loss of use of the money. Interest varies with creditor type, amount borrowed, purpose, secured vs. unsecured, repayment terms, and the borrower's assumed ability to repay (**credit rating**). Each **state** (not the federal government) has **usury laws** capping allowable rates.
- **Late payment fees (p. 63):** charged when payments miss the due date; can also trigger a HIGHER interest rate. Auto-debiting by the payment date avoids them.
- **Other fees (p. 63):** over-the-limit fees; cash advance transaction fees (plus higher rates on advances); annual fees. Those least able to afford fees pay them most; an emergency fund plus bank deposits can eliminate most fees. Airline miles/cash back aren't financially worth it if rewards tempt you into carrying high-interest balances.
- **Credit checks (p. 63):** card issuers can (and do) check customers' credit status as often as daily - spotting risks (bankruptcy filings) or opportunities (credit line increases). Legal, though it raises privacy questions; these checks do NOT affect the consumer's credit rating.

### Qualifying for credit - the C's (pp. 63-64)
- **Character** - apparent honesty/reliability; the credit record shows follow-through on obligations; a prior relationship with the lender helps. (p. 63)
- **Capital** - net worth (from the statement of financial position); more capital = better rating. (p. 63)
- **Capacity** - income available to make payments; job stability, substantial income, and few other debts indicate capacity. (p. 63)
- **Collateral** - assets securing the debt; not always required, but when used must be sufficient to repay the loan via liquidation on default. Classic example: the car loan (lender repossesses and sells on default). (pp. 63-64)
- **Conditions** - the general economic environment: "tight" money -> less willingness to lend, possible rejection; "loose"/plentiful money -> easier credit at lower rates. (The book recommends Ray Dalio's YouTube video "How the Economic Machine Works.") (p. 64)

### FICO credit scores (pp. 64-65)
- Developed by **Fair, Isaac and Company**; used by the vast majority of lenders; an objective summary of a detailed financial history. Range: **300 (poor) to 850 (excellent)**. (p. 64)
- "**Risk-based pricing**": borrowers with credit-report problems get "priced up" to higher rates. April 2021 myFICO.com data for a $300,000 30-year fixed mortgage: 2.796% APR for FICO 760-850 vs. 4.385% for FICO 620-639 - roughly **$268/month difference** ($1,232 vs. $1,500). (p. 64)
- **Figure 2.1 - what makes up a FICO score (p. 64):** payment history **35%**; amounts owed **30%**; length of credit history **15%**; new credit **10%**; credit mix **10%**.
- Many institutions/card companies now provide free scores (often with identity-theft alerts); more info at myFICO.com. (p. 65)
- **Three major credit bureaus:** TransUnion, Equifax, Experian. Getting a report from EACH annually is advisable - each bureau collects from different creditors, so files differ; reviews catch inaccuracies (which can mean unfairly high rates) and identity theft. (p. 65)
- **FACT Act** (Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act): entitles every consumer to **one free credit report per year**. (p. 65)
- Federal Reserve Board + FTC rules (with the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act) require lenders to give consumers their credit score when the consumer: (1) applies and is approved with the score setting material terms; (2) is denied based on the score; (3) is approved but on less advantageous terms than other consumers; (4) has a card account's APR raised based on the score; (5) applies for a mortgage. (p. 65)

### Types of consumer credit (pp. 65-66)
- Two basic types: **installment (closed-end)** and **revolving (open-end)**. (p. 65)
- Installment: longer repayment schedules (over 1 year, up to 30 for mortgages); typical for large purchases (homes, cars, appliances, furniture); can also be a cash lump sum (e.g., home equity loan). (p. 65)
- Revolving: the most common form = the credit card (from retailers, banks, financial institutions); checking-account overdraft protection also qualifies; meant to be shorter-term, but balances can stretch across years. (p. 65)
- **Table 2.7 - Types of credit (p. 66):** Revolving/open-end - bank credit cards (MasterCard, Visa), overdraft protection, retail credit cards. Installment/closed-end - mortgage loans (fixed or variable), student loans, auto loans.

### Mortgages (pp. 66-69)
- The most common long-term installment credit. A **mortgage** places a **lien** on the property as security - the lender's legal right to obtain the property on default; the lien is removed at full repayment. Usually real estate, but personal property (tools, equipment) can be mortgaged too - a lien on personal property = **chattel mortgage**. (p. 66)
- **Fixed-rate:** the rate and P&I payment never change; the rate is set relative to the **10-year U.S. Treasury note** yield when "locked." **Adjustable-rate (ARM):** the rate changes at pre-specified times based on current market rates. (p. 66)
- The actual rate offered depends on the borrower's (**mortgagor's**) credit score - better score, lower rate. **Amortization** = repaying via periodic P&I payments; amortization schedules disclose the payment amount, the principal/interest split of each payment, the payoff period, and principal progress. Early payments are mostly interest; the principal share grows over the term. (p. 66)
- **15-year vs. 30-year (pp. 66-68):**
  - 15-year pros: quicker payoff, far less total interest, home owned lien-free after 15 years, and usually a LOWER rate (shorter term = less lender risk). Con: higher monthly payment. (pp. 66-67)
  - **Table 2.8 (p. 67):** $300,000 home, $60,000 down, $240,000 borrowed. 30-year @ 4.0%: $1,145.80/mo x 360 payments; total interest **$172,488.00**. 15-year @ 3.5%: $1,715.72/mo x 180; total interest **$68,829.60**. Payment difference only **$569.92/mo** (the 30-year payment is ~67% of the 15-year), but interest savings = **$103,658.40**.
  - First-payment split: 30-year - $345.80 principal / $800.00 interest; 15-year - $1,015.72 principal / $700.00 interest. (pp. 67-68)
  - **Table 2.9 amortization comparison (p. 68):** principal portion of the 60th payment: $420.81 (30-yr) vs. $1,027.62 (15-yr); 120th payment: $513.81 vs. $1,436.45; loan balance remaining at the 120th payment: **$189,081.50 vs. $94,313.00**.
  - Two other interest-saving strategies for ANY mortgage: **biweekly payments** (earlier payments = less interest) and **extra principal payments** whenever possible. (p. 68)
- **Types of mortgages (pp. 68-69):** buyers must understand every option's terms/pros/cons; a straightforward 15- or 30-year loan is often most appropriate. Brokers and bankers may earn more on certain products, so ask lots of questions - planners provide objective guidance. Post-2008 lesson: buyers in rising-rate ARMs assumed they could refinance; when home prices fell they went "**underwater**" (owing more than the home's value), lenders wouldn't refinance, and they were trapped in high-rate loans. (p. 68)
- **Table 2.10 - Common mortgages (p. 69):** **fixed-rate** (rate/payment constant for the life of the loan); **adjustable-rate** (rate changes periodically with the market; payments fall/rise); **FHA loan** (government-backed for low-to-moderate-income borrowers; lower down payment requirements; FHA insures the lender against default); **VA loan** (eligible veterans/active-duty; often no down payment; VA guarantees the loan on default).
- **PMI - private mortgage insurance (p. 69):** protects the LENDER on default; typically required on conventional loans (no FHA/VA-style outside guarantee) when the down payment is **under 20%**; adds cost but enables smaller down payments; usually collected inside the monthly payment; often cancelable once equity reaches **20%**.
- **Home equity borrowing (p. 69):** **home equity loan** = a second mortgage delivering a lump sum, with the home's value as collateral; **HELOC** (home equity line of credit) = works like a credit card with the credit line as collateral - funds drawn at the homeowner's discretion.

### Credit vs. debit cards (p. 69)
- Credit cards = the most common consumer credit today. **Debit cards** look and swipe like Visa/MasterCard but **debit the cardholder's bank account** (like writing a check; funds pulled immediately or within a day or two).
- Key difference = legal protection: credit cards fall under the **Fair Credit Billing Act**; debit cards under the **Electronic Funds Transfer Act**. The two differ greatly, and **credit card protection is more favorable**.

### Auto loans (pp. 69-70)
- Same debt mechanics, different environment: dealership sales pressure pushes fast decisions - do your financing homework BEFORE entering the dealership. Dealer financing can carry markup: the bank sets a required rate, and anything charged above it benefits the dealer. (pp. 69-70)
- BUT the dealer may still offer the best available financing (to move cars) - investigate all options. Review the **APR**; beware advertised "**teaser**" rates that rarely apply (or come bundled with a higher car price). Comparison shop; qualifying works like other credit. (p. 70)

### Student loans and education funding (pp. 70-71)
- Planners recommend saving early for college; most families still don't save enough. Alternatives: (p. 70)
- **Grants & scholarships:** from colleges, businesses, fraternal/charitable organizations, foundations, private individuals - money given outright, NO repayment. Two most common federal grants: **Pell Grants** and **FSEOGs** (Federal Supplemental Educational Opportunity Grants) - for undergraduates with exceptional financial need. (p. 70)
- **Federal/state loans:** the most common option; the biggest federal program = **Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized Stafford loans**. Direct Subsidized Stafford = **need-based, undergraduates only**; Unsubsidized = NOT need-based. Loan amounts are determined by the institution via the **FAFSA** (Free Application for Federal Student Aid); rates are fixed for the life of the loan; an origination fee is deducted. (p. 70)
- **PLUS loans** (Parent Loans to Undergraduate Students): parents borrow for dependent children's undergraduate studies; cannot exceed the total cost of schooling; part-time students ineligible (programs shorter than an academic year may qualify for reduced amounts); NOT need-based; fixed rate plus a hefty origination fee. Graduate/professional students borrow directly through PLUS themselves. (p. 70)
- Most government loans require repayment to begin **within 6 months of graduation**; some programs allow longer deferral or even cancellation; most (not all) are need-based; the FAFSA determines qualification and amounts. (p. 71)
- **Private loans:** banks/institutions; generally higher rates and more restrictive payoff options; students often need a **cosigner**. Strategy: exhaust federal/state options first. Consider the TERM, not just the payment (longer term = smaller payment but more total interest - the 15 vs. 30-year mortgage lesson); auto-pay can trim the rate slightly. (p. 71)
- **Federal College Work-Study:** federally sponsored, college-administered; students work 10-15 hours/week (typically on campus) as part of the aid package. (p. 71)
- **The high cost of education (p. 71):** rising costs mean large debts that can take decades to repay and hinder other plans (starting a family, buying a home). $30,000-$100,000+ balances are huge burdens when earnings are low; student loans (like cards) are almost too easy to get; an objective planner helps. Parents: co-signing is risky - if the child defaults, a portion of the parent's **Social Security income can be garnished**; excessive parental education debt also hinders retirement. Saving early remains the best long-term strategy.

### Buying vs. leasing (pp. 71-73)
- A **lease** = a contractual agreement to make monthly payments for the use of property over a set term; use reverts to the owner at term end. Primary decision factors: **cost** and **convenience**. Housing example: rapidly rising prices + higher mortgage rates -> leasing may be more financially beneficial; falling prices + low rates -> a signal to buy. (p. 71)
- **Auto leasing (p. 72):** typical for businesses, but many individuals lease too. Lease payments usually run below loan payments; but the car must be returned at term end; credit requirements are stricter (little or $0 down); leases are hard to exit early - you're committed for the term.
  - **Closed-end lease:** hand the car back; no extra charges except unusual damage beyond normal wear and tear. **Open-end lease:** the lessee may owe the difference between the car's current value and its sale amount. Leases generally include a right to purchase at an agreed **residual value** at term end; exceeding the mileage allowance costs extra. (p. 72)
- **House/apartment leasing (p. 72):** sensible when buying isn't possible or wanted; the most common driver = lacking a down payment (typically **5%-20%** of the price - often $20,000-$40,000+), so first-time buyers rent while saving. Renting pros: easier moves, few maintenance responsibilities, no resale-value worries.
  - Rent affordability guideline: **20%-30% of gross income** (many rental companies cap at 30%). Example: $60,000 gross -> $12,000-$18,000/year = **$1,000-$1,500/month**. Note: the guideline ignores other debt payments, which can affect what's truly affordable. (p. 72)
- **Home ownership advantages (pp. 72-73):** more privacy (single-family homes); more stable monthly costs (inflation punishes homeowners less); tax advantages (mortgage interest + property taxes deductible when itemizing above the threshold); creditors look favorably on homeowners; often (not always) an appreciating asset; "**psychic income**" (pride of ownership); freedom to express personal preferences (landscaping, interior design).
- The primary FINANCIAL advantages of owning = **tax savings + potential appreciation**. Relative cost: renting is generally more cost-effective in the EARLY years; buying wins in LATER years - but too many factors exist for a blanket rule; planners must review the client's specifics before recommending either. (p. 73)

### Protecting the credit user (pp. 73-74)
- Credit growth brought credit abuses - some from poor debt management, others pure fraud unrelated to the consumer's own actions. **Identity theft** is one of the fastest-growing crimes in the US: guard SSNs, birth dates, and financial account logins/passwords. Credit issuers have also abused the system, so Congress passed protective legislation. (p. 73)
- **Consumer Credit Protection (Truth in Lending) Act (p. 73):** requires full disclosure of borrowing costs - the **APR** (annual cost of credit as a %) and dollar finance charges - standardized so different lenders' rates are directly comparable. Prohibits mailing unrequested credit CARDS (unrequested applications are fine). Caps unauthorized-use liability at **$50 per card** (4 stolen cards -> up to $200); **zero liability if the loss is reported before the card is used**. Also regulates credit advertising, requires common terminology, and requires user ID on cards (photo or signature).
- **Consumer Credit Reporting Reform Act (pp. 73-74)** (updates the Fair Credit Reporting Act): regulates credit report use; consumers get file access and the right to correct errors; agencies must forward the consumer's side of disputes to certain businesses; consumers must be notified of investigative reports; limits how long information stays on file. If denied credit/insurance/employment because of a report: the consumer must get the reason plus the bureau's name/address, and has **30 days** to request a free report summary (the same info a lender would receive). Bureaus must maintain formal correction procedures; disputed items must be resolved **within 30 days** or removed; denied consumers must receive their current score.
- **Fair Credit Billing Act (p. 74):** billing-error procedures - the creditor must explain them; the consumer must complain **within 60 days** of the first bill containing the error; the lender then has **30 days to acknowledge** and **90 days to permanently correct** (credit the account, return overpayments, or justify the bill). Consumers may withhold payment for faulty/defective goods bought by credit card after a good-faith attempt to resolve with the seller. During a dispute, creditors may NOT collect the debt, file unfavorable reports about it, assess interest/penalties on it, or send **dunning letters** (repayment-demand notices).
- **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (p. 74):** prohibits credit discrimination based on sex, marital status, race, national origin, religion, age, or receiving public assistance. Women can't be forced to reapply after a marital-status change or be asked about childbearing plans. Joint accounts must be reported in BOTH spouses' names. Creditors must decide within **30 days** and give written reasons for adverse action. Lenders can't require disclosure of alimony/separate maintenance/child support income - though it may be counted if the borrower WANTS it considered.
- **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (p. 74):** guidelines for debt collectors (lawyers, banks, doctors collecting their OWN debts are exempt). Debtors must receive written notice **within 5 days** of first contact: amount owed, to whom, and how to dispute. Prohibits abusive/deceptive/unfair collection practices and misrepresentation; sets contact procedures; third parties may be contacted ONLY to locate the debtor; payments are applied as the consumer directs - and nothing may be applied to a disputed debt.

### Bankruptcy (pp. 74-76)
- Bankruptcy badly damages credit ratings, financial well-being, and self-esteem - consider ALL other options first. Overextended consumers should contact creditors proactively; creditors will often arrange a more workable repayment plan. (p. 74)
- Advice is available from banks, credit unions, sometimes employers; nonprofit credit counseling (e.g., **Consumer Credit Counseling Service, CCCS**) can help. Paying a FOR-PROFIT "credit repair" agency is probably unwise - the record usually can't be fixed and the fee is wasted. (p. 75)
- Common causes despite best efforts: divorce, extended illness, job loss, business failure, other catastrophes. Federal bankruptcy law lets individuals petition a court to dismiss debts; once granted, the debtor's assets and liabilities are administered by the **bankruptcy court trustee** for the creditors' benefit; the trustee repays creditors as fully as possible from available assets, including wages. State and federal laws govern what the debtor keeps - generally homes, non-luxury automobiles, and most unsecured personal property; remaining listed debts are **discharged** (canceled). (p. 75)
- **Chapter 7 - "liquidation"/"straight" bankruptcy (p. 75):** immediate liquidation of available assets and discharge of remaining debts. **NOT dischargeable:** alimony & child support; certain tax claims; government-funded/guaranteed education loans; debts for personal injury caused by intoxicated driving; claims arising from malicious acts. Allowed only **once every 8 years**. Afterward, some creditors actually view the debtor a bit more favorably (little debt, improved net worth, steady income), but mortgages and car financing stay difficult for years. File only as a **last resort** - bankruptcy fixes a DEBT problem, not a CASH FLOW problem. The **Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Act of 2005** made Chapter 7 much harder to use; many filers now must use Chapter 13 instead.
- **Chapter 13 - the "wage earner's plan" (pp. 75-76):** modified bankruptcy for individuals who intend to pay their debts, typically with creditors accepting less than full amounts, under a court-supervised plan. Criteria: stable and regular income; disposable income above basic needs to fund court payments; **secured debts <= $1,395,875**; **unsecured debts <= $465,275** (limits adjust every 3 years; next adjustment April 1, 2025). (p. 75)
- The debtor submits a **3-5 year repayment plan**; the court issues an **automatic stay** temporarily blocking creditor claims. Payments reflect anticipated income, amounts owed, and debt types (some, like tax liens, must be repaid in FULL); usually only part of total debt gets repaid. During the plan the debtor may not take on new credit without trustee permission, and **every debt must be listed** in the petition or it may not be discharged. Chapter 13 isn't for businesses - they use Chapter 11 (beyond this course's scope). (p. 76)

### How much debt is acceptable? (pp. 76-77)
- Simple answer: "**the amount the debtor can reasonably maintain**" - the foundation for setting appropriate debt levels using the financial ratios. Two primary considerations: (1) total debt load and (2) the monthly debt service/repayment schedule. Know the acceptable amount, set limits, and keep an emergency safeguard. (p. 76)
- American Bankruptcy Institute data: personal filings went from **under 300,000 (1980)** to **700,000+ (1990)** to **2 million+ (2005)**, then down to **~413,616 (2021)** as the economy improved into mid-2021. (p. 76)
- **Foreclosure** = the mortgage lender taking possession of mortgaged property because the mortgagor stopped paying. **Figure 2.2** charts foreclosure and mortgage delinquency rates 2006-early 2022 (source: Corelogic). (p. 76)
- By early 2022 delinquencies had returned to more favorable levels. The pandemic-era **federal foreclosure moratorium** protected unemployed homeowners from losing homes and sharply cut foreclosure rates; many delinquent owners resolved matters by becoming current or selling (easy with low rates and high prices). (p. 77)

## Chapter 2.4 - Budgeting (pp. 78-81) [LO 2-8]

- A **budget** = a forecast of cash flows: a planning tool projecting the anticipated AMOUNTS and TIMING of inflows/outflows for a FUTURE period, built from history plus the person's expectations and goals. (p. 78)
- Organized like the prior year's cash flow statement, starting with month 1. Large once-a-year outflows (e.g., an insurance premium) are shown in the month actually disbursed, even if the money is accumulated monthly. (p. 78)
- The budget flags when the person must withdraw savings, earn or borrow more, or cut spending. **Table 2.11** example: January projects a **+$300** net inflow; February is negative - largely a quarterly insurance premium - so the family needs **$320** of additional funds (options per the footnotes: cut other outflows, trim the savings/investment allocation, draw from checking/savings/money market, or earn/borrow more). (p. 78)
- **Table 2.11 - Example family budget, quarter ending March 31 (p. 79):** monthly inflows: gross salaries $12,150; trust fund income $833; interest $120; quarterly stock dividends $395 (March). Outflows: savings & investments $1,500/mo (a deliberately increased target); fixed - mortgage P&I $2,042, homeowners insurance & property taxes $500, auto note $480, quarterly auto insurance premium $595 (due Feb 15), student loan $300; variable - taxes $1,692 (FICA, federal/state income, property), food $1,483, transportation $1,167, utilities/household $717, clothing/personal care $800, entertainment/vacation $1,200, medical/dental $433, miscellaneous $815. Net projected flows: Jan +$300; Feb -($320); Mar +$370.
- **Creating a budget (pp. 79-80):** Step 1 = **setting goals** (short-, intermediate-, long-term). Realistic goal setting attaches DOLLAR AMOUNTS and TIMING (down payment amount and date; car purchase in 3 years; college share 16 years out). Then determine assets available to meet those goals: identify all income sources and where money goes - pulled from the cash flow statement.
- **Table 2.12 - Common budget items (p. 80):** housing (rent/mortgage); auto loan; insurance premiums (life, health, disability, auto, homeowners, umbrella liability); taxes (federal/state income, Social Security, property, special); regular savings/investments ("**pay yourself first**" - an excellent wealth builder); utilities; communication (phones/internet/computers - costs keep climbing); housing maintenance (older homes cost more); transportation (fuel prices matter); clothing/personal care; food; entertainment (incl. cable) & vacations; medical & dental (prescriptions, uncovered costs, deductibles - the "wild cards," with costs rising); education (college inflation has run 2x+ general inflation for decades); credit payments; large & small reserve funds (small surprises, true emergencies, planned big-ticket buys); charitable giving; children's allowances; miscellaneous (haircuts, pet supplies, toys, gifts, hobbies).
- Also budget for: specific gifts/donations, luxury purchases, special events (weddings), care for an elderly parent or special-needs child, saving toward a near-term home purchase, retirement plan contributions (may sit inside regular savings), and special accumulation plans (e.g., building an estate for heirs). **Escrow** (set aside) large periodic payments monthly so the money is ready when due. ANY goal or planned expenditure belongs in the budget - only a comprehensive, REALISTIC budget is a valuable planning tool. (pp. 80-81)
- **Implementation (p. 81):** put the budget into action - easy for people already on a plan, difficult for others (requires awareness + organization; gradual implementation may help). First step: record ACTUAL income/expenses - debit/credit receipts, mobile-payment (e.g., Venmo) records, the check register; note which account each expense came from; adjust the budget when actuals differ from projections. Online personal-finance apps (e.g., Mint, Simplifi) help build budgets and log spending anywhere.
- **Monitoring (p. 81):** budgets must be monitored and reevaluated regularly. Financial PLANS: review at least annually; BUDGETS: review more often - especially early on (monthly reviews are common) - comparing actual vs. projected. Goals, finances (raises, job losses), and inflation all change; initial estimates may prove unrealistic. Planners should expect clients to struggle: be empathetic and encouraging, never judgmental - record keeping and budgeting exist to measure progress and find improvements. Guide the process; don't dictate it.

## Chapter 2.5 - Closely Held Business Forms (pp. 82-85) [LO 2-9]

- Many planning clients own small, **closely held businesses** = companies with a limited number of shareholders. In 2019, about **10.6% of the population** were business owners - representing **over 60% of high-net-worth individuals** in the US. (Large public companies are beyond this course's scope.) (p. 82)
- Choosing the **form of business** is one of an owner's most important decisions: it determines the owner's LIABILITIES and TAXATION. Principal forms: sole proprietorship, partnership, regular (C) corporation, S corporation, limited liability company (LLC). (p. 82)

### Sole proprietorship (p. 82)
- Used by the majority of one-owner closely held businesses; NO formal organizational documents or annual state registrations required.
- The proprietor is personally **liable** for business obligations; all profits/losses flow directly to the owner as personal income - reported on **Schedule C** (business expenses deducted from profits).
- Persistent Schedule C losses invite the IRS to recharacterize the "business" as a **hobby**: show a profit in **3 of the last 5 years**.
- The owner can form an **LLC** to shield personal assets from lawsuits while STILL being taxed as a sole proprietor - a single-member LLC is a "**disregarded entity**" to the IRS.

### Partnerships (pp. 82-83)
- Require a business purpose + at least TWO owners; a written **partnership agreement** should define each partner's rights/responsibilities; the partnership registers with its state of domicile. (p. 82)
- Profits/losses flow through to the partners; partnership income arrives on a **Schedule K-1** (not a W2) and lands on each partner's personal return. (p. 82)
- Persistent losses -> hobby recharacterization; same **3-of-5-years** profit rule. Pairing with an LLC adds liability protection while keeping partnership taxation. (p. 83)
- **General partnership:** EVERY partner is personally liable for partnership obligations. (p. 83)
- **Limited partnership:** created under state law; at least one **general partner** (unlimited personal liability) plus at least one **limited partner** (liability typically capped at the amount invested). (p. 83)
- **Family Limited Partnerships (FLPs):** operate like limited partnerships but among FAMILY members; used mainly in **estate planning** - gifting limited partnership interests in a family business at a **substantial valuation discount**. The general partner is typically a parent who manages the business, contributes property in exchange for the interest, then gifts limited interests to children/grandchildren. (p. 83)

### Corporations (pp. 83-84)
- Registered through the state's secretary of state. Formation documents include: bylaws; a statement of corporate form and management structure; a description of the business type; and the number/classification of stock shares. (p. 83)
- **C corporations (pp. 83-84):** taxed under Subchapter C as **separate taxable entities** with their own rates - NO flow-through; **double taxation** when dividends are paid (profits taxed at the corporate level, then dividends taxed to shareholders). Liability protection is inherent (obligations stay with the company); owners can be employees receiving W2s; pairing with an LLC adds security.
- **S corporations (p. 84):** unique hybrid - **flow-through** income treatment for shareholders; the S election exists under FEDERAL tax law even though incorporation is under STATE law; taxed mostly like a partnership; shareholders owe tax on net profits even if no dividends are paid. Requirements: **no more than 100 shareholders**; **only one class of stock**; **no foreign ownership**. Can pair with an LLC to trim flow-through liability.

### Limited liability companies (LLCs) (p. 84)
- State-registered; combine corporate-style **limited liability** with **flow-through taxation**. Owners = "**members**." Members choose and file an IRS tax classification - commonly partnership or S corporation (a sole proprietor can also operate through an LLC).

### Specialized forms (p. 84)
- **Business trusts** (the "Delaware Trust" / "Massachusetts Business Trust"): a trust used as the business form; ownership units are held by individuals and are separately transferable; limited liability for unit holders; **taxed as a corporation**.
- **Tax-exempt entities:** organized under **Section 501** of the Internal Revenue Code; usually public charities or private foundations; exempt from taxation with minor exceptions.

### Operating the closely held business (p. 85)
- Most closely held businesses face **financing issues**; managers often lack money-management depth, so planners can spot cash flow problems and recommend fixes; needs change as the business grows.
- Early years typically rely on **debt** (sometimes the owner's or family's wealth): interest paid is tax DEDUCTIBLE, so the effective borrowing cost runs well below the stated rate.
- **Commingling:** family/personal funds often get mixed into the business. Keep business accounting fully SEPARATE from personal finances and fix commingling immediately - it creates taxation, credit, and company-valuation problems.
- Owners generally avoid adding shareholders (new obligations; dilution of existing shares).
- **Managing financing problems:** improve operations - tighter inventory control can dramatically improve cash flow; stricter payables/receivables controls (large firms famously stretch payables by delaying vendor payments a few days); scrutinize collections. Planners should analyze the company's financial statements for further cash-flow improvements.
