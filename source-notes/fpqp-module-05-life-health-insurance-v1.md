## Introduction (p. 174)
- For most clients, determining the correct levels of **life and health insurance** is one of the most important financial decisions they can make.
- The primary reason for buying **life insurance** is to financially protect a family when a family member dies. Life insurance can also **protect a business**, **transfer wealth** from one generation to another, **provide retirement income**, and serve as a **source of financing**. It is perhaps the most versatile form of insurance.
- **Health, disability, and long-term care** insurance are designed to protect against the risks associated with illness and injury. Many sources of coverage exist, typically including the **government**, **group coverage offered by employers**, and **individual plans**. This module reviews the features and provisions of the various types of insurance and provides guidelines on how much coverage is appropriate.

## Chapter 5.1 - Life Insurance (pp. 175-176+) [LO 5-1] - PARTIAL

### Why life insurance (p. 175)
- Life insurance helps relieve the financial strain a person's death may cause for surviving family members. The risk of death makes survivors financially vulnerable: the deceased's income ends at death, and for a one-income family the source of financial support ends. In a two-income family, losing one earner's wage can make it impossible to keep up the current standard of living.
- **Survivors** can be a spouse and children, or other loved ones who depend on the income earner - which could include aging parents or a disabled sibling.
- The death of a family member who does NOT have earned income can also create a burden worth insuring against - e.g., the death of a stay-at-home parent who provided day care or homemaking services can strain the widowed working parent's or the children's finances.
- The life insurance **death benefit** can replace a deceased individual's economic contribution to relieve or eliminate a surviving family's financial distress. While still alive, the insured has the peace of mind of knowing survivors are protected financially if the worst should happen.
- LO 5-1: Identify types and uses of group or individual life insurance.

### Group Life Insurance (p. 175)
- **Group life insurance** = a single contract covering an entire group of people. Typically the **policyowner is an employer** and the policy covers the employees or members of the group. It is often provided as part of a complete employee benefit package.
- **Term** life insurance is the most common type of group life policy. An employer can provide up to **$50,000** (face amount) of group term life coverage to an employee **without income tax consequences** to the employee. The **imputed value** of the premium paid for employer-provided coverage in EXCESS of the term premium for a $50,000 face amount IS taxable to the employee.
  - **Example - Victor (p. 175):** Victor's employer, Tahoe Corporation, provides $100,000 of term life insurance for each employee, at an annual premium cost of $350 for Victor. Because the employer can provide up to $50,000 of coverage tax-free, only **half of the $350 premium ($175)** is taxable. Victor pays taxes on $175 even though he did not receive the money directly.
- Some employers offer group policies providing **permanent coverage** (insurance with economic value for more than one year). Group permanent coverage increases in value over the years and **becomes an individual policy at retirement or upon termination of employment**. Permanent coverage may be provided by a traditional **group whole life (ordinary)** policy or **group universal life** (discussed later in this module).

### Types of Life Insurance and Features (p. 176)
- Life insurance policies have been designed and refined over three centuries. Life insurance can be divided into two basic classes: **term** and **permanent** policies. Policies sold today have the same fundamental structure as those created years ago, but insurers continue to refine their products to meet the demands of a changing society.

### Term Life Insurance (p. 176)
- The **simplest** form of life insurance. In its most fundamental form, term life insurance is protection against financial loss due to death occurring during a **specified period (or "term")**. The policy pays the **face amount** to the designated beneficiary if the insured dies during the coverage term. Term insurance offers **pure protection** and has **NO cash value** - all premium dollars go toward the insurance protection, and when the policy period ends, no cash value is paid.
- **Annual renewable term** (also called **one-year term**) is the most elementary form. It covers the insured against death for one year; typically, at the end of the period, the owner has the right to renew for another year or let it terminate.
  - With annual renewable term, the **premium increases each year** because the probability of death increases each year (life expectancy decreases with each passing year).
- **Level premium plans:** some owners who know they want coverage for a set period (e.g., five or ten years) prefer to budget a **level premium**, which most insurers offer. Premiums in the early years are set ABOVE the amount required for those early years' cost of insurance (per actuarial formulas), and the higher cost of insurance in the later years is supplemented by the excess premium paid in the earlier years. At the end of the prescribed period, many of these policies may be renewed at a new, higher level premium for the same number of years. With both annual renewable term and term for a set period, the **face amount** of coverage (e.g., $100,000) stays the SAME; premiums increase at each renewal, but coverage generally does not change.
- **Decreasing term insurance:** in some situations (e.g., buying insurance to cover a mortgage repayment schedule) there is no need for a level face amount. With decreasing term, the **premium stays the same while the coverage decreases**. Because decreasing term is most commonly used to cover loan repayment (especially mortgages), the decreasing schedule frequently follows a loan amortization schedule fairly closely - as the loan is repaid, the insurance amount decreases so that in any given year the coverage approximately equals the outstanding loan balance. Some planners do NOT recommend decreasing term because, even if the loan balance could be repaid, enough other needs would remain to warrant level coverage. With some companies there is not enough difference between level term and decreasing term premiums to justify decreasing term; with others, the premium difference can be more significant.
