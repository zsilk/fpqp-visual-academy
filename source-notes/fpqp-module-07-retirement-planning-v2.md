## Chapter 7.1 - Retirement Opportunities and Challenges (pp. 263-268) [LO 7-1]

### Overview (pp. 263-265)
- Encouraging saving is a valuable adviser service - the key is to get started and save what you can. Various retirement income streams: Social Security; pension/retirement account income; stock dividends; savings; annuity income; home equity; rental income; an inheritance; a full-time or part-time job.
- **EBRI Retirement Confidence Survey (p. 264):** **28%** of retirees are *very* confident about a comfortable retirement; **68%** of workers are *at least somewhat* confident; **74%** of retirees are at least somewhat confident about basic expenses; **60%** of workers rank saving/investing among their top three priorities. Workers **with plans** were more than twice as confident as those without.
- **Reasons people delay saving (pp. 264-265):** procrastination; confusion; poor cash management; feeling too young; college funding; paying off debt; saving for a house; caring for aging parents; not wanting to "lock up" money. Per EBRI, ~**19% of workers have saved under $1,000** and **40% under $25,000** - which is why Social Security matters so much.
- Aging challenges: **health care and long-term care costs**. **Medicare** (65+) has gaps and is NOT long-term care insurance; if a retiree runs out of money, **Medicaid** is the safety net (Module 9). **Longevity risk** = outliving your money.

### Retirement - A Time of Growing Significance (pp. 265-266)
- Most Americans retire between **60 and 64**. Retiring at 62 and living to the average age 83 = **over 21 years in retirement**, nearly **25% of one's life**. In 1900, life expectancy was ~46-49; by 2019 it was ~79 (male ~77, female ~81); by 2030 an estimated 83.3 (women) / 79.5 (men). Some planners plan to a life expectancy of **100**.

### Calculating Longevity (pp. 266-267)
- These are **averages** - about half live longer, so planners add a **cushion** (often to age 100 or 120); rule of thumb: plan for **20+ years**.

## Chapter 7.2 - Sources of Retirement Income (pp. 269-292) [LO 7-2 through 7-6]

- Three main income areas: **personal savings and investments; employer-provided retirement plans; Social Security.** (p. 269)

### Personal Savings and Investments (p. 269) [LO 7-2]
- Assets owned outside employer plans, in any of the four asset classes. Enter retirement with **little or no debt** (unpaid federal student loans can cause garnishment of Social Security). The ideal: mortgage paid off, no credit card balances, pension + Social Security.

### Retirement Plans (p. 270) [LO 7-3]
- **Table 7.2 - Retirement Plan Types (p. 270):**
  | Qualified Plans | Other Tax-Advantaged Plans | Nonqualified Plans |
  |---|---|---|
  | Defined benefit plans | Traditional IRAs | 457 plans |
  | Cash balance plans | Roth IRAs | |
  | Profit sharing plans | 403(b) plans | |
  | 401(k) plans | SIMPLE IRA / SEP IRA | |
- Beginning **2025, auto-enrollment is mandatory for most new employer plans when the employer has 11+ employees and the business is at least three years old.**

### Qualified Plans (pp. 270-275) [LO 7-3]
- A **qualified plan** qualifies for special tax treatment under **IRC Section 401(a)**. Common features: **pretax contributions; tax-deferred growth; vesting** (employee contributions always **100% vested**).
- **Defined Benefit (DB) Plans (p. 271):** a specified benefit - dollar amount or formula (e.g., 50% of final average comp with 25 years). Contributions actuarially determined, in a **general trust fund**; usually only employers contribute (private sector).
  - **Example - Fred (p. 271):** age 65, 24 years, formula 3%/year x highest-three-year average ($69,000): $69,000 x 0.03 x 24 = **$49,680**. A common married option pays the worker for life, then **50% to the surviving spouse**.
- **Cash Balance Plans (p. 271):** a **hybrid** (DB governed by DOL but stated as a **401(k)-style account balance**); more can be contributed than a 401(k).
- **Profit Sharing Plan (PSP) (pp. 271-272):** shares company profits via separate employee accounts; contributions can be irregular but the IRS expects them recurring and substantial. Employer contributions limited to **25% of total compensation**; max includible compensation **$350,000 (2025)**; per-participant max the **lesser of 100% of comp or $70,000 (2025)** (IRC §415).
  - **Ace Moving (p. 272):** 10% contribution; president earns $400,000 but comp caps at $350,000, so max is $35,000. **Pamela's Pantry (p. 272):** 25% contribution; Vance earns $345,000 (25% = $86,250) but caps at the **$70,000** per-participant limit.
- **401(k) Plans (pp. 272-274):** **Section 401(k)** lets the **employee** make pretax salary-reduction contributions; employer and employee may both contribute.
  - **Matching (p. 273):** e.g., 100% match on the first 5% guarantees a 100% return. **Todd (p. 273):** defers 5% of $50,000 ($2,500), Anvil matches $2,500. **Norio (p. 273):** Athena matches 30% of the first 10% of $70,000 ($7,000): 30% x $7,000 = **$2,100** (not the full $10,500 he deferred).
  - **2025 limits (pp. 273-274):** elective deferral **$23,500**; age **50+** catch-up **$7,500**; ages **60-63** catch-up **$11,250** (SECURE 2.0). Max deferral: **$31,000** (50+) or **$34,750** (60-63). Overall additions limit (employer + employee elective, excluding catch-up) = **lesser of 100% of comp or $70,000 (2025)**; max comp **$350,000**. Social Security tax is paid on deferrals; income tax deferred until withdrawal.
  - **Loan provision (p. 274):** typically repaid within **five years** at slightly above prime with after-tax dollars; on leaving, often payable in full or treated as a taxable distribution (10% penalty if under 59½).
  - **Government entities cannot** sponsor 401(k)s. Three funding sources: **employer discretionary** (profit sharing, vesting schedule); **employee elective deferrals** (always 100% vested; auto-enrollment mandatory for most new plans in 2025); **employer matching/nonelective** (may have a vesting schedule). SECURE 2.0 lets employers match an employee's **student loan payments** as if 401(k) contributions.
  - **Table 7.3 - Employer-Provided Plans (p. 275):**
    | Plan | Unique Characteristics |
    |---|---|
    | **Defined Benefit/Pension** | Typically only employers contribute; general trust fund; defined dollar/formula benefit; generous limits; mostly phased out privately (some governmental; small-business resurgence); age/service restrictions |
    | **Cash Balance** | Usually only employers contribute; general trust fund; benefit = accrued account balance; max employer contribution funds a benefit of **$280,000 in 2025**; small-business resurgence |
    | **Profit Sharing** | Share of profits; recurring & substantial (can be irregular); can favor highly compensated/older; vesting schedule; age 21 + one year of service |
    | **401(k)** | Employees contribute; sometimes matched; separate account; benefit = accrued balance; age 21 + one year; part-time eligible after two consecutive years of 500 hours (SECURE 2.0, 2025); 10% early-withdrawal penalty; more costly to administer |

### Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) (pp. 276-281) [LO 7-4]
- An **IRA** is a trust/custodial account for an **individual** (no joint IRAs). Anyone with **earned income** can contribute; 2025 limit = **lesser of $7,000 or 100% of earned income**; age **50+** catch-up **$1,000**. Deadline: **April 15** of the following year (a 2025 IRA can be funded Jan 1, 2025 - Apr 15, 2026).
- **Traditional IRAs (pp. 277-278):** contributions may be **deductible from AGI**. For married filing jointly, deductibility depends on three circumstances:
  1. Both spouses active participants -> deductibility phases out over a MAGI range. **Table 7.4 (2025 MFJ): $126,000-$146,000.**
  2. One spouse active, one not -> different phaseout per spouse. **Table 7.5 (2025):** non-active-participant spouse: full deduction $0-$235,999, partial $236,000-$246,000, none above $246,000; active-participant spouse: full $0-$125,999, partial $126,000-$146,000, none above $146,000.
     - **Example - Silas & Emma (p. 277):** MAGI $160,000; Silas (in a 401(k)) can't deduct (> $146,000); Emma (no plan) can (< $246,000).
  3. Neither spouse active -> no MAGI limits; each can deduct plus catch-up if 50+.
  - **Nondeductible contributions (p. 278):** always allowed; withdrawn tax-free at retirement (after-tax dollars).
- **Roth IRAs (pp. 278-279):** no deduction (after-tax), but **tax-free distributions and earnings**. Eligibility is **income-based only** (not age/active status). **Table 7.6 (2025 phaseouts):** Single **$150,000-$165,000**; MFJ **$236,000-$246,000**.
  - **Qualified distributions** (no tax, no penalty on earnings) require a **five-year holding period** (from Jan 1 of the opening year) AND the distribution being after age **59½, death, disability, or a first-time home purchase (max $10,000)**. Distribution ordering: (1) **return of contributions** (no tax/penalty); (2) **return of conversion amount** (no income tax; 10% penalty if under 59½ and converted funds under five years and no exception); (3) **return of earnings** (taxed + 10% penalty if not a qualified distribution and under 59½).
    - **Example - Sherita (p. 279):** age 39, contributed $15,000, account $23,000. Withdraw $10,000 -> no consequences (contributions). Withdraw $20,000 -> first $15,000 tax/penalty-free; the $5,000 of earnings is taxed + 10% penalty (under 59½, not qualified).
  - **Conversion from a deductible IRA to a Roth (p. 279):** subject to ordinary income tax but **excluded from the 10% penalty**. **Anton (p. 279):** age 40 converts a $12,000 traditional IRA -> $12,000 taxable this year, no penalty.
  - **Table 7.7 - Traditional vs. Roth (pp. 279-280):** Traditional - contributions generally deductible (if conditions met); tax-deferred earnings; taxable retirement income; **subject to RMDs at age 73, even if working**. Roth - after-tax contributions; tax-free earnings; qualified distributions tax-free; determine eligibility not deductibility; **no RMDs**.
- **Spousal IRAs (p. 280):** for 2025, a spouse with under $7,000 of earned income can contribute up to **$7,000** based on the working spouse's income (must file jointly; combined comp ≥ the contributions); age 50 catch-up applies. It uses the other spouse's earned income for the individual's own account (not joint). **Remy (p. 280):** stay-at-home dad; wife Gabrielle earns $80,000; Remy contributes up to $7,000 (+$1,000 if 50+).
- **Rollover IRAs (pp. 280-281):** **60-day rollover** - funds received in hand, redeposit within **60 days**; unrolled amounts are taxed (+10% if under 59½); **one per 12-month period**. **John (p. 281):** takes $20,000, redeposits only $15,000 -> $5,000 taxable + 10% penalty. **Trustee-to-trustee transfer** - no receipt of funds, **no tax reporting, unlimited per year** (Table 7.8).

### Plans for Nonprofit Organizations (pp. 281-283) [LO 7-5]
- **403(b) Plans (pp. 281-282):** for employees of charitable/religious/educational tax-exempt organizations (schools, colleges). Similar to a 401(k); 2025 elective deferral **$23,500**, age 50+ catch-up **$7,500**, ages 60-63 catch-up **$11,250** (SECURE 2.0). **Special catch-up:** employees with 15+ years of service at certain organizations can add **$3,000/year for five years, up to $15,000 total**. Loans allowed.
- **457 Plans (pp. 282-283):** nonqualified deferred comp for state/local government (and agencies) and 501(c)(3) nonprofits. Governmental 457 assets held in a tax-exempt trust; **nonprofit 457 funds are subject to the employer's creditors** (can be forfeited in bankruptcy - unlike 401(k)/403(b)). 2025 deferral **$23,500** (+catch-ups). The 457 limit is **NOT reduced** by contributions to other plans, so someone under 50 could defer up to **$47,000** combined ($23,500 to a 401(k) + $23,500 to a 457).
  - **Roger (p. 282):** defers $23,500 to a state 457 and could also defer $23,500 to a 401(k) at his brother's business = up to $47,000. **Victoria (p. 282):** two 401(k)s cannot exceed $23,500 total ($10,500 to one leaves $13,000 for the other).
  - **Special catch-up (p. 282):** in the last three years before normal retirement date, a 457 participant who under-contributed in prior years can contribute up to **twice the deferral limit** ($47,000) - but not the regular catch-up and the special catch-up in the same year.
  - **Table 7.9 (p. 283):** 403(b) - full- and (SECURE 2.0, 2025) certain part-time employees of tax-exempt orgs; 10% early-withdrawal penalty. 457 - state/local government and 501(c) nonprofits; government assets in a tax-exempt trust, nonprofit funds at risk in legal proceedings; **no penalty tax for early withdrawals** (amount still included in income).

### Small Business Retirement Plans (pp. 283-285) [LO 7-5]
- **SIMPLE IRA (pp. 283-284):** "savings incentive match plan for employees"; usable only if the company has **no other qualified plan**; only employers with **100 or fewer employees**. Eligibility: anyone who earned **$5,000+** in any two prior years; no age requirement; **immediate 100% vesting**. 2025 elective limit **$16,500**; age 50+ catch-up **$3,500**; ages 60-63 catch-up **$5,250** (SECURE 2.0). Employer must either **match up to 3% of salary** or make a **fixed 2% nonelective** contribution (max $7,000 = 2% of $350,000).
  - **Tarak (p. 284):** contributes 3% of $50,000 ($1,500); Genesis matches $1,500. **Bethanie (p. 284):** makes no deferral; Stable contributes 2% of $70,000 ($1,400) anyway. **Early-withdrawal penalty is 25%** (not 10%) in the first two years. **Blake (p. 284):** age 24, withdraws $500 -> taxable + a **25% penalty ($125)**.
- **SEP IRA (pp. 284-285):** "simplified employee pension"; **only the employer contributes**; **immediate vesting**. Eligibility: age **21**, worked **three of the last five years**, earned **$750 (2025)** - so not ideal for many part-time/seasonal workers. Similar to a profit sharing plan but minimal reporting (just an **adoption agreement**); contributions can be fully flexible. Max = **25% of compensation, up to $70,000 (2025)**. (Table 7.10.) *(Table 7.10 lists "employer and employee contributions" as a shared trait for the SEP - a book inconsistency, since only the employer contributes to a SEP; as printed.)*

### Nonqualified Deferred Compensation (pp. 285-286)
- For executives/highly paid, to **defer taxation** and provide benefits beyond rank-and-file plans: **excess benefit plans, SERPs, top hat plans, rabbi trusts** (beyond this program's scope).

### The 10% Early Withdrawal Penalty and Exceptions (pp. 286-287)
- Generally a **10% penalty** before age 59½, with exceptions.
- **Any plan (qualified plans and IRAs) (p. 286):** death; disability; a series of substantially equal periodic payments; medical expenses exceeding **7.5% of AGI**; qualified reservist distributions (active duty 180+ days); up to **$5,000 per parent** for birth/adoption of a child under 18; qualified disaster distributions up to **$100,000**; terminally ill distributions (terminal = 84 months, SECURE 2.0); domestic abuse survivors up to **$10,000** (SECURE 2.0, 2024); unforeseeable emergency expenses up to **$1,000** (SECURE 2.0, 2024).
- **Qualified plans only (not IRAs) (p. 286):** separation from service **in or after the year the recipient turns 55**; a **qualified domestic relations order (QDRO)**; distribution of dividends from an ESOP.
- **IRAs only (p. 287):** higher education expenses; a **first-time home purchase up to $10,000**.

### Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) (pp. 287-289) [LO 7-6]
- RMDs let the IRS collect tax revenue. **Roth IRAs and Roth employer accounts have NO RMDs.** RMDs begin at **age 73** (for those who turn 72 on or after Jan 1, 2023; SECURE 2.0 raised it from 72 to 73). The first RMD is due by **April 1 of the year after** the later of the year the participant reaches 73 or their retirement year. If the participant owns **>5%** of the company, distributions must start at 73. Working past 73 can delay employer-plan RMDs (but IRA RMDs still apply at 73).
  - **Helga (p. 287):** age 73, still working -> no 401(k) RMD yet. **Abigail (p. 287):** 100% owner -> must start RMDs at 73 despite working (>5% owner).
- **Formula (p. 288):** **RMD = (account balance on Dec. 31 of the prior year) / life expectancy** (from a Uniform Table).
  - **Table 7.11 - 2024 Uniform Table (abbreviated) (p. 288):** 73 = 26.5; 74 = 25.5; 75 = 24.6; 76 = 23.7; 77 = 22.9; 78 = 22.0; 79 = 21.1; 80 = 20.2.
  - **Mia (pp. 288-289):** account $150,000 on Dec 31, 2023; turned 73 in 2024. First RMD (2024) = $150,000 / 26.5 = **$5,660** (due by April 1, 2025). Second RMD (2025, due Dec 31, 2025) = ($150,000 − $5,660 + 6% growth) / 25.5 = $153,000 / 25.5 = **$6,000**. RMDs generally rise as the balance grows and life expectancy falls; it's a minimum, so more can be withdrawn.
- **Penalty (p. 289):** SECURE 2.0 penalty for a missed RMD is **25%** of the RMD, reduced to **10%** if corrected timely (was 50% before). In **2033**, the RMD age rises to **75** for those born 1960 or later.

### Vesting (pp. 289-291) [LO 7-6]
- The **vested (nonforfeitable) amount** is what legally belongs to the employee (40% vested on a $10,000 balance = $4,000). Vesting is based on **years of service**, not years in the plan.
- **Defined Benefit plans (pp. 289-290):** **five-year cliff** (Table 7.12: 0% years 1-4, 100% at 5) or **seven-year graded** ("three-seven": Table 7.13: 20% at 3, +20%/yr, 100% at 7). These apply only to DB plans.
- **Defined Contribution plans (pp. 290-291):** employee deferrals always **100% vested**; employer matching must vest under either **three-year cliff** (Table 7.14: 0% years 1-2, 100% at 3) or **two-to-six-year graded** (Table 7.15: 20% at 2, +20%/yr, 100% at 6).
  - **Joshua (p. 291):** two-to-six-year graded; $5,300 balance; four years of service -> **60% vested** = **$3,180** (no credit for a partial year). Six years would have made him 100% vested.

## Chapter 7.3 - Social Security and Medicare (pp. 293-306) [LO 7-7, 7-8]

### Background of Social Security (p. 293) [LO 7-7]
- Social Security = **Old Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance (OASDI)**, paid through the OASI Trust Fund and DI Trust Fund. In 2020, 65M+ beneficiaries (~49M retirement); total paid (incl. Medicare) over **$1 trillion**. Per the SSA: Social Security is about **33% of the income of the elderly**; among elderly beneficiaries, 50% of married couples and 70% of unmarried get 50%+ of income from it, and 21%/45% rely on it for 90%+.

### Origins (pp. 293-294)
- SS Act passed **1935** (Great Depression). A **social insurance** program (self-funded by participants), **not** a savings program - current taxes pay current benefits. Initial tax 2% of wages up to $3,000; first checks 1940. **Table 7.16 - Milestones (p. 294):** 1935 Act; 1937 first worker benefits; 1939 spouse/widow(er); 1961 age-62 early retirement; 1965 Medicare; 1975 automatic COLAs; 2006 Medicare drug program; 2022 trust funds run short in **2035** (then only 80% of benefits payable).
- **Payroll tax (p. 294):** employer and employee each pay **6.20% (12.40% total)** OASDI on up to **$176,100 (2025 wage base)**, and **1.45% (2.90% total)** Medicare HI on all compensation (uncapped). An additional **0.9% HI** applies to wages over $200,000 single ($250,000 MFJ, $125,000 MFS) - up to **3.8%** for high earners.

### Social Security's Funding (p. 295)
- Trust funds are an accounting entry (special Treasury bonds), not a cash reserve. FICA funds OASI, DI, and Medicare HI.
  - **Table 7.17 - Components of 15.3% FICA (p. 295):** OASDI **12.4%** up to $176,100 (split employer/employee); Medicare HI **2.9%** on all comp (split); total **15.3%**.
  - **Table 7.18 - FICA Rates (2025) (p. 295):** employed worker pays **7.65%** (6.20% OASDI + 1.45% HI) up to $176,100, plus 1.45% HI above; employer matches 7.65%; **self-employed** pays the full **15.30%** up to the wage base and **2.90%** HI above (with a deduction of one-half of self-employment tax); employee comp over $200,000 is subject to an additional 0.9% Medicare tax.

### Social Security Basics (pp. 296-297)
- **Not paying SS taxes (p. 296):** federal workers hired before 1984; railroad employees (Railroad Retirement System); children under 18 employed by a parent in an unincorporated business; certain state/local employees in a system that elected to exclude SS.
- **Credits/quarters (p. 296):** in 2025, one credit per **$1,810** earned, up to **four per year** (inflation-adjusted).
- **Fully insured (p. 296):** one credit per year since age 21, minimum 6, maximum 40 - **10 years** of covered work = always fully insured. All three benefit types (retirement, disability, survivor) require fully insured status for the worker.
- **Currently insured (p. 297):** at least **six credits in the 13-credit period** before death; provides child's/parent's benefits and a **$255 lump sum death benefit**.
- **Age criteria / FRA (p. 297):** minimum retirement age **62** (reduced benefits). **Full retirement age (FRA)** depends on birth year, leveling at **67** for those born 1960+. The 1984 change took effect 16 years later to protect near-retirees.
  - **Table 7.19 - FRA Increases (p. 297):** 1943-1954 -> **66**; 1955 -> 66+2mo; 1956 -> 66+4mo; 1957 -> 66+6mo; 1958 -> 66+8mo; 1959 -> 66+10mo; **1960+ -> 67**.

### Social Security Benefits (pp. 297-301)
- **Benefit Calculation (p. 298):** the **Primary Insurance Amount (PIA)** is the monthly benefit at FRA; benefits are a percentage of PIA. PIA is based on **average indexed monthly earnings (AIME)** (pronounced "Amy"; inflation-adjusted). *(The full benefit calculation isn't required for this course.)*
- **Retirement benefits (p. 298):** need to be **fully insured and age 62+**. **100% of PIA** at FRA; reduced before FRA; increased after FRA.
- **Benefit Adjustments (pp. 298-300):**
  - **COLA (p. 298):** annual Jan 1 increase based on Q3-to-Q3 CPI (2024 COLA 3.2%). **Table 7.20 (pp. 298-299):** 2022 5.90%, 2023 8.70%, 2024 3.20%, 2025 2.50% (among others).
  - **Reduction before FRA (p. 299):** **Table 7.21:** starting at 62 reduces the benefit to as low as **70% of PIA** (FRA 67) or **75%** (FRA 66).
  - **Delayed retirement credit (p. 299):** delaying past FRA adds **8% per year** up to age **70** (but doesn't raise PIA, so spousal 50%-of-PIA benefits aren't increased; a surviving spouse can benefit).
  - **Niko (p. 300):** PIA $2,590.20, retires at 62 (FRA 66), 25% reduction -> **$1,942.60** ($2,590.20 x .75). **Carmen (p. 300):** PIA $1,150, works to 70 (FRA 66), 4 x 8% = 32% -> **$1,518** ($1,150 x 1.32).
  - **Table 7.22 - Social Security Benefits (p. 300):** summarizes benefits for the worker, spouse, and children in the event of the fully insured worker's retirement / disability / death (spousal and child benefits are 50% of PIA if the worker is living, rising to 75% for survivor benefits; a spouse at FRA gets 50% of PIA, a surviving spouse at FRA gets 100%).

### Social Security Strategies (pp. 301-303) [LO 7-8]
- **When to start** is a major decision (not to be rushed). Some must start at 62 for income; those with shorter life expectancy may start early. Understand what a higher inflation-adjusted payment you give up by not waiting.
- **Reduction before FRA due to earnings (pp. 301-302):** if you take benefits before FRA and are still working, benefits are reduced by **$1 for each $2** earned over **$23,400 ($1,950/mo) in 2025** (ages 62 to the year before FRA), and by **$1 for each $3** over **$62,160 ($5,180/mo)** in the FRA year (for months before FRA). At FRA, no reduction; unearned income doesn't count.
  - **Luciana (p. 302):** started at 62 ($1,800/mo); at 63 earns $39,000 (2025), $15,600 over the limit -> benefit reduced by **$7,800** ($15,600/2); reduction stops at FRA.
- **Spousal Benefit Opportunities (pp. 302-303):** a spouse can claim **50% of the worker's PIA** at FRA, but the worker must file first (except an ex-spouse if the marriage lasted 10+ years). **Terry (pp. 302-303):** PIA $1,000, files at 62 ($750 reduced); husband Ray PIA $2,400; at Terry's FRA she gets a $200 spousal bump ($1,200 − $1,000) -> **$950** ($750 + $200), less than the full $1,200 because she started early. Generally, the longer you may live, the more attractive delaying is.
- **Earnings and Benefit Statement (p. 303):** verify earnings at **www.ssa.gov/mystatement**; corrections are easiest within the three-year/three-month/15-day limit, but if recorded earnings are less than paid, there's no time limit.

### Medicare (pp. 304-306) [LO 7-7]
- **Origin (p. 304):** enacted **1965**; the 2003 Modernization Act added the drug benefit. Four parts: **Part A** (hospital/skilled nursing), **Part B** (physician/outpatient), **Part C** (Medicare Advantage), **Part D** (prescription drugs).
- **Funding (pp. 304-305):** Part A funded by the **2.9% HI tax** (compulsory). Part B (SMI) funded by income-based premiums (deducted from SS); voluntary, with a **late enrollment penalty** of a permanent 10% increase per 12-month delay. Part D financed partly by premiums.
- **Eligibility/coverage (p. 305):** those receiving SS retirement 3+ months before **age 65** are auto-enrolled; a **seven-month** enrollment window starts three months before 65. Medicare eligibility is based on **age 65, not FRA**. **Part D:** initial deductible **$590 (2025)**, then 25% individual / 75% Medicare, with 5% coinsurance after the out-of-pocket max. Medicare does **not** cover most long-term/custodial nursing home care, eyeglasses, hearing aids, dental, cosmetic surgery, routine foot care, or (generally) care outside the U.S.
- **Gaps / Medigap (pp. 305-306):** six gaps - (1) Part A/B deductibles; (2) extended hospitalization under Part A; (3) 20% coinsurance under Part B; (4) costs above Medicare-approved charges; (5) drugs not paid by Part D; (6) custodial nursing home costs. Standardized **Medigap** packages fill these.

## Chapter 7.4 - Financial Planning for Retirees (pp. 307-310) [LO 7-9]

### Retirement Pitfalls (p. 307)
- Pitfalls: lump sum distributions when leaving an employer; managing plan loans; longevity risk; avoiding large losses early in retirement; minimizing fraud.

### Lump Sum Distributions (pp. 307-308)
- Vested benefits are often paid as a **lump sum**. Best course is often to keep the money in the former employer's plan or **roll it into an IRA** (stays tax-sheltered). One advantage of leaving it with the former employer if **age 55+ at separation**: 401(k) distributions avoid the 10% penalty (even before 59½) - an exemption **lost** if rolled into an IRA. Lump sums from a qualified plan carry **20% federal withholding**. (p. 308)

### 401(k) and 403(b) Loans (p. 308)
- Used improperly, a loan is "robbing Peter to pay Paul." Disadvantage: repaid with **after-tax dollars**, then taxed **again** at retirement. Leaving a job with a loan outstanding usually requires repayment in ~60 days or the loan becomes a taxable distribution (10% penalty if under 59½); the **Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017** lets you replace the money by the tax-return due date.

### Longevity Risk (pp. 308-309)
- Outliving income = **longevity risk**. Fixed annuities (esp. life annuities that pay until death) can substitute for part of the bond allocation, though fixed payments carry purchasing-power risk. **Qualified longevity annuity contracts (QLACs)** - approved 2014 - let up to **$200,000 (2025)** in a 401(k)/IRA fund a longevity annuity **exempt from age-73 RMDs**, typically starting at 80 or 85. Holding **three to five years** of living expenses in safe, liquid funds lets you avoid selling securities in a bear market (a bear market at retirement significantly raises longevity risk).
  - **Table 7.23 - Ordinary Life Expectancies (p. 309)** (Treasury Reg. §1.72-9, Table V): 70 -> 16.44; 75 -> 12.85; 80 -> 9.63; 85 -> 6.91; 90 -> 4.76; 95 -> 3.30 (among others).

### Avoiding Large Losses Early in Retirement (pp. 309-310)
- The **first decade** of retirement often determines overall success. One approach: start with **fewer stocks (~30%)** and **increase** equity exposure gradually (~0.5%/year) - the opposite of the usual glide path - to limit large losses when the retiree is most vulnerable.
- **The 4% rule:** withdraw 4% the first year, then adjust for inflation annually, aiming to last 30 years (a starting point to monitor). Every **$4,000** of desired first-year income needs about **$100,000** saved; $1 million yields **$40,000**.

### Avoiding Fraud (p. 310)
- Con artists target retiree nest eggs. **Red flags:** returns too good to be true; secrecy; no written information / privately generated statements; pressure/urgency. Con artists often exploit affinity groups (a church) or false credentials.
