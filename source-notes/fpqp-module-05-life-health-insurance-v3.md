## Introduction (p. 174)
- For most clients, determining the correct levels of **life and health insurance** is one of the most important financial decisions they can make.
- The primary reason for buying **life insurance** is to financially protect a family when a family member dies. Life insurance can also **protect a business**, **transfer wealth** from one generation to another, **provide retirement income**, and serve as a **source of financing**. It is perhaps the most versatile form of insurance.
- **Health, disability, and long-term care** insurance are designed to protect against the risks associated with illness and injury. Many sources of coverage exist, typically including the **government**, **group coverage offered by employers**, and **individual plans**.

## Chapter 5.1 - Life Insurance (pp. 175-188) [LO 5-1 through 5-3]

### Why life insurance (p. 175)
- Life insurance helps relieve the financial strain a person's death may cause for surviving family members. The deceased's income ends at death; for a one-income family the source of support ends, and in a two-income family losing one earner's wage can make it impossible to keep up the current standard of living. **Survivors** can be a spouse and children or other loved ones who depend on the income earner (including aging parents or a disabled sibling).
- The death of a family member who does NOT have earned income can also create a burden worth insuring against (e.g., a stay-at-home parent who provided day care or homemaking). The **death benefit** replaces the deceased's economic contribution; while alive, the insured has peace of mind knowing survivors are protected. [LO 5-1]

### Group Life Insurance (p. 175)
- **Group life insurance** = a single contract covering an entire group; typically the **policyowner is an employer** covering employees, often part of a benefit package.
- **Term** is the most common group life policy. An employer can provide up to **$50,000** (face amount) of group term life to an employee with **no income tax consequences**. The **imputed value** of the premium for coverage in EXCESS of the $50,000 term premium IS taxable to the employee.
  - **Example - Victor (p. 175):** Tahoe Corporation provides $100,000 of term life for each employee at a $350 annual premium for Victor. Since $50,000 can be provided tax-free, only **half of the $350 ($175)** is taxable. Victor pays taxes on $175 even though he didn't receive the money directly.
- Some employers offer **permanent coverage** (economic value for more than one year). Group permanent coverage increases in value over the years and **becomes an individual policy at retirement or termination of employment**; it may be provided by a traditional **group whole life (ordinary)** policy or **group universal life**.

### Types of Life Insurance and Features (p. 176)
- Two basic classes: **term** and **permanent** policies. Policies sold today have the same fundamental structure as those created years ago, refined over three centuries.

### Term Life Insurance (p. 176)
- The **simplest** form: protection against financial loss due to death during a **specified period ("term")**. Pays the **face amount** to the beneficiary if the insured dies during the term. Pure protection, **NO cash value** - when the period ends, no cash value is paid.
- **Annual renewable term** (one-year term) is the most elementary; the **premium increases each year** because the probability of death rises each year.
- **Level premium plans:** early-year premiums are set above the cost of insurance, and the excess supplements the higher cost in later years. The **face amount** stays the same; premiums increase at each renewal, but coverage generally doesn't change.
- **Decreasing term insurance:** premium stays the same while coverage decreases; commonly used to cover a mortgage (the decreasing schedule follows the loan amortization). Some planners don't recommend it because other needs remain that warrant level coverage.

### Life Insurance Premium (p. 177)
- Three main elements are bundled to set a premium: **Mortality** (relative frequency of deaths; death rate), **Expenses** (insurer operating costs), and **Interest** (earnings on premium dollars between collection and payout). Mortality charges rise with age, so the cost of coverage (especially term) increases each year.
- Term premiums become **prohibitive at older ages** (the cumulative cost approaches the benefit). This drove insurers to develop coverage people can keep for their **whole life**.

### Whole Life Insurance (pp. 177-178)
- **Whole life** = a permanent policy guaranteed to stay in force for the insured's **entire lifetime** (or to policy maturity), provided premiums are paid, and **premiums never increase**.
- Difference from term: whole life has a **cash value** (savings element), so higher initial premium; a level premium is possible. Cash value grows **tax-deferred** - no income tax on it unless withdrawn, and then only on amounts above premiums paid. This "forced savings" can fund retirement or college.
- Also called **straight life** / **ordinary life**; level premium paid for life. Builds cash value that can be **borrowed against** (keeping the policy in force) or **received outright** if terminated.
- On a $100,000 policy, the individual receives $100,000 if no loans are outstanding; a $15,000 loan reduces it to $85,000 - and the death benefit is likewise reduced by outstanding loans. Beneficiaries receive the **face amount less any loans**, NOT face value PLUS cash value.
  - **Example - Bob (p. 178):** $250,000 whole life policy taken 20 years ago; cash balance grows at least **3%/year**. Bob borrowed **$40,000** to buy a boat at **8%** interest; when repaid, 6% of the interest goes back to him and the insurer keeps 2% for admin - so in effect a **2% loan**. Bob dies in a boating accident with **$36,000** still outstanding; his wife receives **$214,000** ($250,000 − $36,000).
- **Limited payment life:** premiums typically paid only until age 65, then a **paid-up policy** for life. Total annual premiums are adjusted UPWARD vs. traditional whole life; payments are made for a specified number of years (15/20/30) or until a preset age (e.g., **Life Paid at 65**).
- Cash value in traditional whole life is invested **conservatively** in the insurer's **general account** (high-grade bonds/stocks, real estate), primarily for **safety** - lower returns but higher security.

### Universal Life Insurance (p. 179)
- Universal life (UL), aka **flexible premium adjustable life**: **flexible premiums and coverage**. Premiums accumulate as interest in the cash value; monthly interest credited is the guaranteed rate or current rate, **whichever is higher**. Each month a term cost of insurance and an admin fee are deducted from the cash value; as long as cash value covers them, the policy stays in force. Premiums can be increased, decreased, or occasionally skipped (within limits), but adequate premiums must be paid regularly.
- Returns are based on **short-term interest rates**, so UL reacts faster than ordinary life in both rising and falling rate environments.
- UL contracts reference a **target premium** (estimated amount to keep the policy in force). If insufficient, the owner must pay more or the policy **lapses**. UL's **unbundled structure** lets the owner see interest and expense in annual statements (cash value, loans, withdrawals, premiums). Increasing the face amount may require **evidence of insurability**.

### Equity-Indexed Universal Life Insurance (p. 179)
- Ties accumulation values to a stock index (e.g., **S&P 500**), with a minimum guaranteed interest component. Gives the security of fixed UL with market growth potential. Downside protection guarantees credited interest will **never be below zero**.

### Variable Life Insurance (p. 180)
- Combines traditional protection/savings with the growth potential of securities (especially stocks). Guarantees the death benefit **never falls below the initial face amount**, and guarantees **fixed premiums** (like whole life). As long as premiums are paid, coverage stays in force regardless of investment performance; the **cash value moves up and down with the market**.
- Investment choices are made by the **insured**, provided by an open-end investment company (a **separate account**) operating like a mutual fund, with a **prospectus**.
  - **Separate Accounts (p. 180):** funds in variable life separate accounts are usually NOT the same as similarly named outside mutual funds, even with identical names/management; they don't mirror the outside funds' structure or performance.

### Variable Universal Life Insurance (VUL) (p. 180)
- Combines UL's **unbundled flexibility** with variable life's **insured-directed investments** (aka **flexible premium variable life**). A VUL can **lapse** if cash value falls below the monthly insurance cost - so it does NOT have a guaranteed minimum death benefit.

### Table 5.1 - Comparison of Life Insurance Policies (p. 181)
| | Level Term | Whole Life | Universal Life | Variable Life | Variable Universal Life |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Death Benefit** | Expires at end of term | Fixed and level | Adjustable, level, or increasing options | Varies with investment performance; original face amount is guaranteed minimum | Adjustable; level or increasing options |
| **Premiums** | Fixed schedule, increases at each renewal | Fixed schedule, level amount | Flexible schedule, flexible amount | Fixed schedule, level amount | Flexible schedule, flexible amount |
| **Cash Values** | None | Fixed and guaranteed | Current interest with guaranteed minimum rate | Varies with investment performance; no guaranteed minimum and at risk for loss | Varies with investment performance; no guaranteed minimum and at risk for loss |

### Term or Permanent Life Insurance? (pp. 181-182)
- **Amount needed:** a large need favors term (more coverage per premium dollar).
- **Duration of need:** short/specific need (e.g., until kids finish college) favors term; long-term/beyond age 65 favors cash value.
- **Risk tolerance:** variable life (permanent + investment subaccounts) appeals to risk-takers; conservative clients prefer term (temporary need) or whole life (permanent need, known death benefit/premium).
- **Disposable income:** term is cheaper initially but rises at renewal, eventually costing more than permanent; significant disposable income may favor permanent (lock in a level premium); little disposable income favors term. The best answer is often a **combination**. (p. 182)
- **Rating (p. 182):** the **rating** describes the risk the underwriter believes the insured is (e.g., a healthy nonsmoker = **preferred**; a recent heart attack = **"rated up"**). Higher rating (risk) = higher premium.

### Policy Provisions (pp. 182-185) [LO 5-2]
- Most common provisions (p. 182): the policy + a copy of the application constitute the **entire contract**; the **insuring clause** sets the insurer's promise to pay at death; **misstatement of age** causes an adjustment in the amount of insurance.
- **Ownership/Assignment (pp. 182-183):** the policyowner may not be the insured (e.g., a business insuring an owner/key employee). With a demonstrable **insurable interest**, almost anyone can own a policy on another's life (you can insure your spouse, typically not an acquaintance). Ownership rights belong to the **owner** - assign/transfer the policy, receive cash values/dividends, name the beneficiary, or borrow.
  - **Viatical Agreements (p. 183):** **viatication** = selling an insurance policy on the life of a terminally/chronically ill person to a buyer (usually a **viatical settlement company**) for **60% to 90%** of the death benefit (depending on life expectancy), to help cover terminal-illness costs. Following IRS/SEC rulings, most viatical arrangements are treated as **investments**.
- **Grace Period (p. 183):** if the premium isn't paid on the due date, the policy stays in force up to **31 days** before lapsing (prevents unintentional lapse). Example: premium due June 1st, insurance in effect until June 30th.
- **Beneficiary (pp. 183-184):** the entity designated to receive proceeds. A **primary beneficiary** receives first; if unable/unwilling, a **contingent (second) beneficiary** receives. Several contingents are possible and often advisable.
- **Incontestability Provision (pp. 183-184):** after **two years** in effect, the insurer cannot claim an application statement was meant to defraud. Gives time to discover misrepresentation/fraud; adds value to an established policy (consider when replacing a policy).
- **Reinstatement (p. 184):** most lapsed policies can be reinstated (conditionally) within a specified period (e.g., three years); requires paying overdue premiums and current **evidence of insurability**. A new contestable and suicide exclusion period likely applies.
- **Suicide Clause (p. 184):** suicide is excluded during a specified period (normally **two years**, less in some states); within that period the insurer repays only cumulative premiums plus interest. Protects insurers against those who buy coverage shortly before a suicide attempt.
- **Policy Loan (p. 184):** the owner may borrow from a permanent policy's cash value (up to ~**90%**), using the policy as collateral. Interest is charged; an unpaid loan is deducted from proceeds at death. Unpaid loans plus interest can cause variable/universal policies to **lapse**.
- **Policy Dividends (pp. 184-185):** dividend-paying policies are **participating**; others are **nonparticipating**. Dividends are a **refund of premium** (not guaranteed, not taxable). Options:
  - **Cash:** insurer sends a check.
  - **Accumulate with interest:** insurer pays interest; the **interest is taxable** in the year received.
  - **Reduce/pay premium:** e.g., premium $1,500, dividend $300 - pay only $1,200.
  - **Paid-up additions (PUAs):** each dividend buys additional paid-up insurance that builds its own cash value and earns dividends (compound effect); tax-deferred.
  - **One-year term insurance (the "fifth dividend option"):** the dividend buys term coverage equal to the **guaranteed cash value**; if the insured dies, the beneficiary gets the death benefit PLUS an amount equal to the cash value.

### How much life insurance is needed? (pp. 185-186) [LO 5-3]
- You can't **avoid** or **reduce** the risk of death, and you can't meaningfully **share** it (death is individual). That leaves **retain** or **transfer**. The odds are 100% you'll face death. Life insurance is a **cash substitute** - the economic need at death is cash to pay final expenses, settle the estate, and support dependents.
- Two approaches to size coverage: the **human life value (income-based)** method (replaces economic worth) and the **programming/needs-determination** method (present-values the future need).

### Income-Based (Human Life Value) Method (pp. 186-187)
- Five steps: (1) estimate future average annual earnings; (2) subtract income taxes, Social Security taxes, insurance premiums, and personal living expenses to get the annual payment the family uses (**PMT**); (3) decide productive years left (**N**, until retirement); (4) set a discount rate (**I/YR**); (5) compute PV in **annuity due** mode (**PV_AD**).
  - **Learning Activity (p. 187):** earnings $45,000; deductions total $18,000; 5% discount; 30 years. **BEG mode, 1 P/YR, C ALL**: $45,000 − $18,000 = **$27,000 PMT**; 30 N; 5 I/YR; solve **PV_AD = $435,808.99** (~$435,809). The **lower the rate, the higher the need** - at 2% the need is **$616,798** ($180,989 more).

### Needs-Based Method (pp. 187-188)
- Determines the dependents' needs. **Five basic areas:** (1) final and fixed expenses (burial, unreimbursed medical, estate settlement, mortgage elimination, bequests, education); (2) income for the readjustment period; (3) dependency period income (while children are home); (4) life income for the remaining spouse; (5) retirement needs for the surviving spouse. The **need minus available assets = basic insurance need**. Regardless of approach, the **need** matters most, not the type of insurance. (p. 188)
  - **Example - Harold and Susan (p. 188):** insurance need **$750,000**. Choice within budget: a **$750,000 10-year level term** policy or a **$200,000 whole life** policy. With whole life there's a **$550,000 shortfall**, so the **term policy is preferred** - it covers the important years while the children are young.

## Chapter 5.2 - Health Care Plans and Health Savings Accounts (pp. 189-195) [LO 5-4, 5-5]

### Purpose of Health Insurance (p. 189) [LO 5-4]
- A significant medical claim (heart attack, cancer, stroke, catastrophic injury) is one of the most devastating losses. In retirement planning, health care is a top concern.
- **Risk Management:** even with insurance, out-of-pocket costs come from **deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, and high MOOP limits** - so keep enough **cash reserves** for a catastrophic event. **The Need:** costs rise yearly (higher deductibles/out-of-pocket maximums); it's often a choice between higher premiums with smaller bills or lower premiums with larger bills. Coordinate insurance (large-loss protection) with a cash reserve.

### Policy Features and Benefits (p. 190)
- **Deductibles:** the amount paid before the plan pays; preventive/wellness care (physicals, mammograms, well-baby care) is **100%** covered. Health deductibles are **annual** (cumulative), not per-incident. Example: with a $2,000 deductible, if the first of three claims exceeds $2,000, no deductible applies to the rest.
- **Coinsurance:** a percentage the insurer pays after the deductible, up to the MOOP. Example: $2,000 deductible + 80% coinsurance up to a $6,350 MOOP; on a $5,000 service the insured pays the $2,000 deductible plus 20% of the remaining $3,000 ($600) = **$2,600** out of pocket.
- **Copayments:** a **set amount** for a service (e.g., a doctor visit); may or may not apply to the deductible/coinsurance.
- **Maximum Out-of-Pocket (MOOP):** the amount beyond which the insurer pays **100%** of covered services.
  - **Example - the Smith family (p. 191):** $4,000 family deductible, 80% coinsurance, $40 doctor copay, $100 ER copay, $12,900 MOOP. Feb: Vincent's checkup is 100% preventive. Mar: Eric's broken arm ($4,500) - Smiths pay the $4,000 deductible + $100 coinsurance (20% of $500) = **$4,100**; deductible now met. Jul: Lauren's $40 copay + tests $3,000 - insurer pays 80% ($2,400), Smiths pay $600 (YTD out-of-pocket **$4,700**). Leukemia: bills reach $58,000; they pay an additional **$8,200** to hit the **$12,900** MOOP, after which the insurer pays 100%.

### The Affordable Care Act (pp. 191-192)
- Passed **2010** (Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, ACA). Five exchange plan categories: **Catastrophic, Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum** (vary by cost-sharing).
- **Consumer Website:** **www.healthcare.gov**. **Adult Dependent Coverage:** children can stay on a parent's plan until **age 26**. **Consumer Protections:** (1) lifetime limits on group plans eliminated; (2) rescission prohibited except for fraud; (3) no denying coverage or preexisting-condition exclusions for children. **Preventive Care:** provided with no cost-sharing. **10 Essential Health Benefits (p. 192):** (1) outpatient care; (2) emergency services; (3) hospitalization; (4) maternity/newborn care; (5) mental health & addiction treatment; (6) prescription drugs; (7) rehabilitative services & devices; (8) lab services; (9) preventive services; (10) pediatric services.

### Social Security / Medicare (p. 193)
- **Medicare** = federal program for persons **65+** and certain disabled persons. **Part A** (hospital): premium normally **free**, with deductibles/copays. **Part B** (physician/outpatient): income-based monthly premium (deducted from Social Security), plus a deductible and **20% of approved charges**. **Part D** (prescription drugs): monthly premium by plan. **Medigap** plans fill the "gaps"; under COBRA, a retiree can't be turned down for Medigap if they apply within **six months** of enrolling in Medicare after age 65.

### Health Savings Account (HSA) (pp. 193-194) [LO 5-5]
- HSAs pay current health expenses and save for future qualified medical/retiree expenses **tax-free** (usually pretax payroll). Eligibility: covered by a **high deductible health plan (HDHP)** and no other health insurance.
- **HDHP (2025):** minimum deductible **$1,650** (individual) / **$3,300** (family); annual out-of-pocket max **$8,300** (individual) / **$16,600** (family); indexed for inflation.
- **Contributions (2025):** max **$4,300** (individual) / **$8,550** (family); age **55+** catch-up **$1,000** (not indexed). Employer contributions aren't taxable; individual contributions are "above-the-line." Grows **tax-deferred**.
- **Qualifications/penalties (p. 194):** if eligible at any point in a year you may contribute the **entire** year's amount, but must stay eligible the following **12 months**. A one-time, tax-free IRA-to-HSA transfer is allowed. Nonqualified use before **65** = **20% penalty** + ordinary tax; at 65 the penalty ends but tax remains on nonqualified use. No HSA contributions once on Medicare. The full annual contribution is available on day one; a debit card is used for qualified expenses only.

### Health Reimbursement Arrangement (HRA) (p. 194)
- **Employer-funded only**; for health care expenses; may be current-use or restricted to retirement.

### Flexible Spending Account (FSA) (p. 194)
- Like an HSA but **no HDHP required** - anyone with a health plan qualifies. Funds must be used **within the year** (generally until **March 15** of the next year) or are **lost** ("use it or lose it"). The full annual amount is available day one. An HSA holder may also open a **limited-use FSA** (usually dental/vision only).

### Table 5.2 - Comparison of HSAs and FSAs (p. 195)
| | HSA | FSA |
|---|---|---|
| **Qualification** | Must have a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) | Must have a health plan, with no other eligibility requirements |
| **Contribution limit** | 2025: $4,300 individuals / $8,550 families | 2025: $3,300 |
| **Changing contribution amounts** | Any time during the year | At open enrollment; changes only with a change in employment or family status |
| **Amount of time to use the funds** | Unused balances roll over into the next year | Generally only for one year; unused balance not retained |
| **Available balance** | Total expected contribution may be used on day one | Total expected contribution may be used on day one |
| **Employer** | Can be held even if employment changes | Usually lost with a job change (COBRA may allow holding it) |
| **Taxes** | Tax-deductible, often pretax; growth and distributions tax-free | Generally pretax; distributions untaxed |

## Chapter 5.3 - Disability Income Insurance (pp. 196-201) [LO 5-6]

### Overview (p. 196)
- A **loss of income due to disability** may be the most significant risk management concern - until about age 55 you are almost **twice as likely to suffer a long-term disability as death**. Income stops while expenses continue or rise. **Disability income insurance** makes **monthly payments** to replace part of the lost income.
- Sources: federal/state/municipal governments, Social Security, workers' compensation, employers (group plans, sick days, self-insurance), and other organizations. **Tax note:** premiums paid with **after-tax** dollars -> benefits are **tax-free**; premiums paid by the employer or with **pretax** dollars -> benefits are **taxable** (fully or pro rata).
  - **Example - Barney and Betty (p. 196):** Barney paid premiums with after-tax dollars -> his $3,000/month benefit is **tax-free**. Betty's employer paid with pretax dollars -> she pays income tax on her $3,000/month benefit.

### Sources of Coverage (pp. 196-197)
- **Government:** the two primary government forms are **workers' compensation** and **Social Security**; some states have nonoccupational disability programs. In most cases, coverage must come from other sources.
- **Social Security / OASDHI (p. 197):** workers who are totally disabled may qualify under **OASDHI** (Old Age, Survivors, Disability, and Health Insurance). Many/most people do NOT qualify. A worker is entitled to OASDHI disability benefits if insured for disability based on their age before becoming disabled AND: is under full retirement age; has been disabled 12 months or is expected to be for at least 12 months (or has a disability expected to result in death - you can apply as soon as disabled); and has filed an application.
  - **Age Makes a Difference (p. 197):** coverage needed varies with the age at disability. Disabled before age 24: covered **6 of the last 12 quarters**. Ages 24-31: covered **1 of every 2 quarters** since age 21. Older than 31: **fully insured** and covered **20 of the last 40 quarters**.
  - **Definition (OASDHI):** disability = inability to engage in **any substantial gainful activity** due to a medically determinable physical or mental impairment expected to result in death or lasting/expected to last at least 12 months. If someone can still "push a broom," they aren't considered disabled by Social Security.
  - Normally a **five-month waiting period** (benefits begin the sixth month); payable retroactively up to 12 months. A disability beginning within five years after a prior disability (meeting requirements) may pay from the first month.
- **Workers' compensation (p. 197):** income compensation for an employee injured/disabled while working, **without determination of fault**; makes industrial-accident cost part of the cost of production. The employer shoulders all liability regardless of employee negligence (fewer litigation opportunities). All states (plus Puerto Rico, Guam, DC) have workers' comp laws.
- Government programs pay **minimal** benefits; professionals accustomed to higher income find both Social Security and workers' comp woefully inadequate - a group and/or individual policy is needed for substantial coverage.

### Group Policies (p. 198)
- Two basic forms - **short term** and **long term**. Short-term: brief benefit period (often no longer than 26 or 52 weeks), very short/no elimination period; designed to dovetail with long-term (ending as LTD and/or Social Security begins). Long-term: benefits typically a percentage (e.g., 60% or 75%) of regular monthly income, possibly with a preset limit (e.g., 60% up to $4,000/month); longer waiting periods (90 days, 6 months, a year).
- Most long-term group plans use a **coordination of benefits** provision - benefits are reduced by other benefits payable (workers' comp, Social Security, other government/employer disability, select pensions). Employer plans do NOT reduce benefits for **individually owned** policies.
- Benefits may or may not be taxable (after-tax premiums -> tax-free benefits; pretax/employer premiums -> taxable). Group coverage normally **terminates when employment ends** and **COBRA does not apply** like it does with health insurance - so consider an individual long-term policy.

### Individual Policies (pp. 198-199)
- Good to have if you lack group benefits or want to augment. If you already have a group plan, the individual policy is limited to the uncovered portion (e.g., group covers 50%, an individual plan allowing 75% would underwrite only the remaining **25%**).
- Written for a specified benefit amount, elimination period, and time period; can cover income lost due to accident alone or accident/sickness. Premiums vary with coverage amount, elimination period, max benefit period, accident-only vs. accident/sickness, occupation class, age, definition of disability, COLAs, and other provisions.
- **Riders (p. 199):** **waiver of premium**; **accidental death and dismemberment**; **cost of living adjustment (COLA)** (benefit increases tied to an index like CPI, subject to limits, beginning after benefits are paid for one year); **guarantee of insurability / automatic benefit increase** (raise coverage at intervals without new evidence of insurability, keeping pace with rising income); and a **residual or partial disability benefit** (pays when a disabled person returns to work but still has a loss of income).

### Common Features of Disability Insurance (pp. 199-200)
- Three key considerations: **benefit amount**, **benefit period** (incl. elimination period), and **definition of disability**.
- **Benefit amount:** max coverage is based on income - typically **60% to 75% of earned income** at issue (excludes investment/unearned income, though the insurer may use unearned income to reduce coverage).
- **Benefit period:** short term (6 months-2 years) or long term (to age 65 or occasionally for life). It begins with an **elimination period** (like a **deductible**) during which no payment is made - a longer elimination period lowers the premium. Occupation matters: "white collar" workers generally get better coverage/rates and longer benefit periods than "blue collar" workers.
- **Definition of disability (p. 200)** - three traditional definitions (an insured is disabled if they cannot work):
  - **Any occupation ("any occ"):** most restrictive/least generous - you must be unable to work in ANY occupation.
  - **Modified any occupation:** considers whether the insured can work at any occupation for which they're qualified (limits the types of work expected; may weigh prior education, training, experience, and sometimes prior economic status).
  - **Own occupation ("own occ"):** most liberal/most generous - disabled if unable to work at their own prior occupation; most expensive. (A heart surgeon could work as a family practitioner and still collect. High cost is pushing insurers away from own occ.)
- **Loss of Income Policies (p. 200):** may pay if income loss is due to illness/injury even if the insured keeps working; benefits generally trigger at a **20% to 25%** loss of income and pay in proportion (a 30% loss -> 30% of the benefit).
- **Split Definition (p. 200):** combines definitions - uses **own occ** for an interim period (e.g., first two or five years), then reverts to **any occ** or **modified any occ**; common with group plans, sometimes with retraining during the interim.

### How much and what type of coverage is appropriate? (pp. 200-201)
- Simple answer: as much as you can get. Two limits: (1) insurers cap coverage (e.g., **60%-75%** of predisability income - you can't buy more at any price); (2) cost (potential multi-million-dollar liability + high likelihood of disability makes it expensive).
- Rule of thumb: determine monthly expenses, add disability-caused expenses, subtract expenses of earning a living (clothing, transportation) and other income (investments) and other disability sources (group plan, Social Security - though many exclude Social Security). The remainder is roughly the coverage to carry.

## Chapter 5.4 - Long-Term Care Insurance (pp. 202-205) [LO 5-7]

### Overview (p. 202)
- Elderly people suffer illness/injury more frequently and for longer than young people; this plus increased longevity drives the rapid growth of **long-term care (LTC)** insurance. A one-year nursing home stay can cost **$100,000 to $120,000 or more** (by location); a long stay can greatly reduce or eliminate net worth.

### Sources of Coverage (pp. 202-203)
- **Medicare (p. 202):** limited LTC benefits under **Part A**, but only if the recipient spent at least **three days as an admitted hospital patient within the last 30 days**. Most who need LTC decline gradually and don't enter a hospital first. Two nursing home types: **rehab** and **residential**. Entry into a **rehab** facility (possibility of recovery) generates Medicare assistance; a **residential** facility is generally NOT covered. Medicare is for those who may recover; dementia/Alzheimer's decline isn't covered. Misperception: Medicare is a primary LTC source - in reality it pays very little, and its own guidebook says not to rely on it for LTC.
- **Medicaid (pp. 202-203):** designed to pay LTC costs, but you must basically **impoverish yourself** - income and assets nearly exhausted (as low as **$2,000 or $3,000** or less) before Medicaid pays for nursing costs, home health care, prescriptions. Retainable excluded items: a **house** (if the individual plans to return, or a spouse/dependent lives there); **one car of limited value**; **personal belongings**; **a small amount of life insurance**; **burial plots**; a few other miscellaneous items. Assets transferred to another person or a trust within **60 months** of applying may be subject to **"lookback"** rules (brought back into the calculation). Medicaid is partly federally funded but state-administered (an "impenetrable, labyrinthine maze"), and patients may have little say in where they stay or the quality of care.

### Individual Policies (p. 203)
- Fewer insurers now offer LTC; features vary. Premiums for **qualified** LTC policies may be deductible like medical expenses (with age-based limits). Cover personal/custodial care - skilled nursing, intermediate, custodial, respite, home health, hospice, etc.; read the policy for what is/isn't covered.
- Many early LTC insurers left the market (mispriced products/insufficient premiums). Fewer stand-alone options exist, so insurers developed **"hybrid"** products - life insurance or annuities with an **LTC rider** - which pay for LTC if needed but otherwise retain a death benefit or account value.

### Common Features of LTC Policies (pp. 204-205)
- **Daily Benefit (p. 204):** a set dollar amount per day; the average daily nursing home cost is a starting point. The insured chooses the daily amount (higher = more expensive) and the benefit period; many policies index the daily benefit for inflation.
- **Elimination Period (p. 204):** like disability's - no benefits during it; options of 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, or 180 days or longer (typical 30-90). Longer = lower premium; keep cash to cover 100% during it.
- **Benefit Period (p. 204):** the maximum time benefits are payable - two, three, four, or five years to lifetime. Typical nursing home stays after age 65 run **two to five years**. Longer = higher premium; lifetime coverage is expensive, so many policies cap at five years or less.
- **Benefit Triggers (pp. 204-205):** **activities of daily living (ADLs)** - **Dressing, Transferring** (bed to chair and back), **Toileting, Eating, Bathing, Maintaining continence**. Typically the insured must be certified unable to perform **two or more ADLs for at least 90 days**. Other triggers: **cognitive impairment** (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's - alone adequate to trigger benefits per policy provisions) and **medical necessity** (physician-ordered).

### How much and what type of coverage is appropriate? (p. 205)
- LTC costs vary dramatically by state/institution; contact local facilities for typical costs. Buying **earlier** (age 55 vs. 65) means a **lower premium** (like life insurance); health, gender, and optional benefits also matter. A skilled elder-care adviser helps navigate the interplay of Medicare, Medigap, Medicaid, and LTC. Most individuals don't consider LTC until their **50s or older**.

## Chapter 5.5 - Annuities (pp. 206-212) [LO 5-8]

### Overview (p. 206)
- Annuities provide steady cash flow in retirement and alleviate fears of outliving assets (**longevity risk**). They are insurance-company contracts purchased to (1) receive periodic payments for a lifetime or specified period, or (2) as a long-term, tax-deferred investment. They are long-term, complex, and fee-laden.
- Four main categories: **immediate vs. deferred** and **fixed vs. variable**. **Immediate** = payments start right away; **deferred** = payments start later (or never). **Fixed** = guaranteed interest rate; **variable** = investment subaccounts whose value changes.

### Definitions (pp. 206-207)
- **Single premium:** one payment to the contract.
- **Fixed premium:** a predetermined premium paid on each scheduled due date.
- **Flexible premium:** the deposit can be changed by the owner anytime (within limits).
- **Immediate annuity:** income starts shortly after a single payment (usually within six months); often for life.
- **Deferred annuity:** income starts later (a year+); bought for tax-deferred growth.
- **Individual annuitant:** payments to one person.
- **Joint and survivor:** payments while both annuitants live, with a reduced payment to the survivor after one dies (popular with couples).
- **Pure life annuity:** payments last the annuitant's lifetime; stop at death.
- **Life and period certain:** payments for the annuitant's lifetime with a minimum guaranteed period (e.g., "life with 10-year certain" pays for life or 10 years, whichever is longer; if the annuitant dies after four years, a beneficiary gets six more years).
- **Refund annuity:** if payments over the annuitant's life don't equal the annuity's value at annuitization, the balance is paid to a beneficiary (continued payments or lump sum).
- **Fixed annuity:** guaranteed minimum interest rate on invested dollars, which may increase if the insurer's general account earns more.
- **Variable annuity:** internal value invested (at the holder's direction) in separate accounts similar to mutual funds; value can rise or fall, so payments can rise or fall.

### Immediate and Deferred Annuities (pp. 207-208)
- Immediate annuities begin structured payments right away - popular with retirees (an income stream that can't be outlived if a lifetime option is chosen). Beyond a life-income option, choices include: **payments for a fixed period** (e.g., 10 years); **payments of a fixed amount** (insurer determines how long) - neither of these two is available with variable annuities; **life option with period certain** (payments continue to a beneficiary for the remainder of a guaranteed period if the annuitant dies early); **life option with refund certain** (beneficiary gets the difference between payments received and original principal); and **installment payments with refund certain**. (p. 208)
- A deferred annuity can also select a future payment option. Payments are part principal and part interest (or gains, for variable). Only the **interest (gain)** portion is taxable (ordinary income) until all **principal** is recovered; after that, the entire payment is taxable.
  - **Example - Perry (p. 208):** age 80, invests **$50,000** into an immediate fixed annuity paying **$600/month** for life. Of each payment, **$500 is nontaxable return of principal** and **$100 is taxable interest**. After **100 payments** (100 x $500 = $50,000), all principal is returned; starting with the **101st** payment, the entire **$600** is taxable.
- Deferred annuities have an **accumulation period** (from issuance until funds are removed) during which value grows tax-deferred. Early withdrawals before annuitization face two penalties: a federal **10% penalty** on withdrawals before age **59½** (to the extent includible in income; exceptions for death, disability, annuitization), and nonperiodic withdrawals are taxed **LIFO** (earnings out first) - only the earnings portion is taxed (plus the 10% penalty if before 59½).
  - **Example - Polly (p. 208):** invested **$25,000** into a deferred annuity 16 years ago, now worth **$40,000**; at age **58½** she takes a **$20,000** nonperiodic distribution. LIFO: the **$15,000 gain** is ordinary income, plus a **10% penalty ($1,500)** because she's under 59½; the remaining **$5,000** is nontaxable return of principal. Waiting one more year to 59½ would have avoided the penalty. *(Book switches the name to "Sally" mid-example - as printed.)*

### Surrender Charges (p. 209)
- Beyond taxes/IRS penalties, the insurer may impose **surrender charges** - typically **5% to 10%** the first year, decreasing each year until they disappear (five to ten years or more). After year one, investors can usually withdraw up to **10%** of value without a charge. Example 8-year schedule: **Years 1-3: 7%; Year 4: 6%; Year 5: 5%; Year 6: 4%; Year 7: 3%; Year 8: 2%**. Once you select an income option and payments begin, the account is **annuitized** (frozen) - the accumulated amount is exchanged for the income stream and no longer yours.

### Fixed Annuities (pp. 209-210)
- A deferred fixed annuity is effectively a tax-deferred savings account earning a fixed rate for a set period, after which the insurer sets a new rate. Rates track long-term Treasuries, but only a low rate is guaranteed (e.g., **2% to 3%**; an annuity might pay 4% with a floor of 3%). A **"bailout provision"** lets the owner withdraw without a company penalty if a new rate falls below a specified level (desirable but not universal). A **Section 1035** tax-free exchange to another annuity avoids the 10% penalty. Fixed-annuity assets are in the insurer's **general account** (not segregated) and are subject to creditors' claims if the insurer has financial trouble.

### Variable Annuities (pp. 210-211)
- A deferred variable annuity is a family of mutual funds (**subaccounts**) within an annuity; value fluctuates with the subaccounts. At annuitization, payouts can be fixed or variable. Most provide a **guaranteed death benefit** (the greater of total purchase payments less withdrawals, or the current account value), funded by a small monthly **mortality charge**. Variable-annuity assets are in **separate accounts** (not the general account), so they're NOT subject to creditors' claims. Advantages: investment flexibility, professional management, diversification, high-return potential, inflation hedge, plus tax deferral. Disadvantages: no guarantee of account value (except at death) and illiquidity; some offer minimum guarantees for a fee.
  - **Example - Gregory (pp. 210-211):** bought a variable annuity three years ago with a guaranteed death benefit no lower than his $50,000 investment; poor performance/fees dropped the account to **$40,000**. Needing cash, he withdraws and receives just **$37,480**: $40,000 − $4,000 (10% penalty-free withdrawal) − $2,520 surrender fee (7% on $36,000) = $33,480; + $4,000 = **$37,480**. He confused the death benefit (his heirs would have received **$50,000** at his death) with a living withdrawal.

### Equity-Indexed Annuities (p. 211)
- **EIAs** give more upside potential with more complexity/fees/downside; some stock-market growth with the downside protection of a guaranteed annuity. They have features of both fixed and variable annuities - a guaranteed minimum interest rate plus an index-tied rate (e.g., **S&P 500**). The **participation rate** determines how much of the index's gain is applied. Caveats: the calculation is never straightforward; there may be a cap on the upside but not the downside; and the insurer may reserve the right to **change the participation rate** after purchase.

### Annuity Selection (p. 211)
- Consider the company's **financial strength** (A.M. Best, Moody's, S&P), investment objectives/portfolio mix, whether the initial rate is supportable, portfolio management experience, interest rate history, and contract charges (mortality in variable annuities, expense, maintenance, management). A company can provide its actual interest rate renewal history.

### Annuities vs. Life Insurance (pp. 211-212)
- Both are insurance products, but purposes differ: **life insurance** pays money when you die; **annuities** provide tax-deferred retirement savings. Annuities are called **"upside down life insurance"** - both offer a death benefit, but that's where the comparison stops.
  - **Table 5.3 - Life Insurance vs. Annuities (p. 212):**
    | Life Insurance | Annuities |
    |---|---|
    | Can be used to create an estate | Can be used to liquidate an estate |
    | Protects against dying too soon | Protects against living too long |
    | Uses a series of payments to guarantee a lump sum of money upon death | Uses a lump sum of accumulated money to guarantee a series of income payments while living |
  - Both have tax benefits: life insurance proceeds are tax-free to the beneficiary; annuity money left to a beneficiary is taxed on any growth (interest).
