## Introduction (pp. 92-93)
- Motivating examples (p. 92):
  - A retiree with a fixed $2,000/month income facing 4.5% average inflation for 15 years: 15 years later that income buys only HALF the goods and services it did at retirement - standard of living cut in half.
  - A couple sets aside $5,000 for a child's college starting in 12 years; if college costs rise 7%/year, they must accumulate over $11,260 in that time to buy the same education the original $5,000 would have bought 12 years earlier.
- Core concept (p. 92): money's value - its purchasing power - changes/erodes with time. Even at ZERO inflation, a dollar received in the future is worth less than a dollar today (and a future payment obligation costs less than paying today) because a dollar invested wisely earns an expected positive return.
- Compounding power example (p. 92): $10,000 invested today at 4% grows to $32,434 at retirement 30 years out; at 8% compounded annually it grows to $100,626 - the return doubled, but the ending balance is over THREE times as much. Compounding (or discounting) money based on interest is the dynamic force behind TVM.
- Applications (p. 92): deciding how investment dollars best meet objectives; determining retirement needs for a client's plan; determining (in part) the financial effect of postponing taxes.
- Module shows how to calculate (p. 92): FV of a single sum; PV of a single sum; interest rate per compounding period; PV of an ordinary annuity; PV of an annuity due; and periodic payment.
- A **financial function calculator is REQUIRED** for this module, quizzes, practice exams, and the course final. Suggested (and used for all keystrokes in the course): **HP 10bII+** and **TI BAII Plus**. (p. 92)

## Chapter 3.1 - Calculator Basics (pp. 94-98) [LO 3-1]

<!-- NOTE: The printed chapter heading on p. 94 reads "Chapter 3.1 Financial Statements" (book's own printed text), but the TOC/LO table titles it "Basic Calculator Functions and Terminology," which matches the actual content. -->

### Getting started (p. 94)
- You will need a financial calculator to solve TVM problems throughout the course; this module explains how calculations are done and the reasoning behind the methodology. Start with the basics or jump straight to the calculations as a refresher, depending on familiarity.
- Keystrokes are provided ONLY for the HP 10bII+ and TI BAII+ (straightforward and intuitive); other calculators are welcome but unsupported.

### HP 10bII+ (pp. 94-96)
- **On/off (p. 94):** "ON" key at bottom left; "OFF" is its alternate function - strike the downshift key first (third up from bottom left, usually orange), then "OFF". The downshift key activates the alternate function of the NEXT key struck.
- **TVM function keys (p. 94 figure):** top row - N (number of periods), I/YR (interest per year), PV (present value), PMT (payment), FV (future value); plus [BEG/END], [DOWNSHIFT], [C ALL].
- **Clearing (p. 95):** before working ANY problem, clear memory: downshift, then the "C" key below it (alternate function "C ALL" = clear all). Without downshift you clear only the display - entries for other variables stay stored. Always "clear all" before each calculation.
- **Display decimal places (p. 95):** downshift, then "=" (alternate "DISP"), then the number of decimal places to show.
- **Decimal point vs. comma (p. 95):** a mis-keyed downshift + decimal key toggles the display between decimal and comma; strike downshift + "." again to switch back. Happens fairly often - know the fix.
- **Begin/end mode (p. 95):** whenever periodic payments are involved, set the calculator for payments at the BEGINNING of each period (**annuity due**) or the END (**ordinary annuity**). Most payments (house, car) are ordinary annuities. Retirement income calculations are annuity due (retirees need income at the start of each year for that year's expenses); education expenses too (paid at the start of the school year, not after classes end). Toggle: downshift + Beg/End key (second key down from top right). "BEG" on the display = begin mode; no "BEG" = end mode. The mode persists even when powered off; there is NO default - the last setting stays until changed.
- **Payments per year (pp. 95-96):** downshift + PMT key (alternate "P/YR" = payments per year). Quarterly interest -> 4 payments/year: strike 4, downshift, P/YR. To verify: downshift + hold "C ALL" -> display shows "4 P_Yr". The setting persists until changed (power off does not reset it). Semiannual example: 2, downshift, P/YR. (pp. 95-96)
- **Three prep steps before EACH AND EVERY problem (p. 96):** (1) correct mode - most problems use END mode; (2) correct number of payments per year; (3) clear all (which also lets you check P/YR). For TVM you use the top-row keys (FV, PV, I/YR, PMT, N); typically you are given three variables and solve for a fourth - with periodic payments, given four and solving for a fifth.

### TI BAII+ (pp. 96-98)
- **On/off (p. 96):** "ON|OFF" key, upper right corner.
- **Layout (p. 97 figure):** TVM function keys in the white row - N, I/Y, PV, PMT, FV; the 2ND key (yellow) accesses the yellow/gold alternate functions printed above keys; C/CE clears the display.
- **Clearing (pp. 97-98):** each function holds its inputs in memory. TVM memory: 2ND, CLR TVM. Other functions (e.g., uneven cash flows): CF, 2ND, CLR WORK. The TI has a number of different memories, and EACH must be cleared individually.
- **Display to four decimals (p. 97):** 2ND, FORMAT, 4, ENTER -> "DEC = 4.00"; then CE|C, CE|C.
- **Begin mode (pp. 97-98):** 2ND, BGN, 2ND, SET, CE|C -> small "BGN" appears above the numbers. Calculators usually come preprogrammed in END mode (the default); to shift back from BGN, repeat the same steps. Wrong mode = a common mistake.
- **Compounding periods (p. 98):** 2ND (yellow key), P/Y, 1, ENTER, CE|C. Verify: 2ND, P/Y -> "P/Y= 1.000" should display; CE|C to clear.
- **Three prep steps before each problem (p. 98):** (1) correct mode (most problems = END); (2) correct payments per year; (3) clear the calculator between EVERY problem. TVM uses the white-row keys: N, I/Y, PV, PMT, FV; given three solve a fourth, or given four solve a fifth.

## Chapter 3.2 - Time Value of Money Calculations (pp. 99-118) [LO 3-2 through 3-6]

### Sign convention - critical! (pp. 99-100, 104, 108)
- Enter numbers as NEGATIVE when cash is LEAVING your hands (making an investment/deposit); POSITIVE when cash is COMING INTO your hands (receiving payments, selling an investment). (p. 99)
- To enter a negative: type the number FIRST, then strike "+/-" (1000, +/- shows "-1,000"; strike +/- again to flip back). (p. 100)
- Getting signs wrong changes the answer: a positive PV with a negative PMT means money is being WITHDRAWN from the investment, not deposited. (p. 104)
- When entering both a PV and an FV, one must be positive and the other negative, or the calculator gives NO solution. (p. 108)

### Calculating future value (FV) (pp. 99-104) [LO 3-2]
- **FV** = the future amount of an initial deposit after it earns interest over time. Interest is earned on the deposit in period 1, then on the deposit PLUS prior interest in later periods - "**compounding**": interest earned on increasing amounts over time. (p. 99)
- Basic pattern (enter the three in any order): PV; I/YR (I/Y on the TI); N; solve for FV. (p. 99)
- **FV Problem 1 (pp. 99-100):** John invests $1,000 anticipating 8%/year for 3 years. HP: 1000 +/- PV; 8 I/YR; 3 N; solve FV = **1,259.71**. TI: END mode; 2ND P/Y 1 ENTER CE|C; 2ND CLR TVM; same entries; CPT FV = 1,259.71. So $1,000 for 3 years at 8% grows to $1,259.71.
- **FV Problem 2 (pp. 100-101):** same problem with SEMIANNUAL compounding. HP: end mode; 2 P/YR; clear all; 1000 +/- PV; 8 I/YR; then 3, downshift, N -> "xP/YR" multiplies years entered x periods per year (6 shows). Solve FV = **1,265.32**. TI: 2ND P/Y 2; 1000 +/- PV; 8 I/Y; 3, 2ND, xP/Y (6 appears), N; CPT FV = 1,265.32.
  - Tip (p. 101): some students ALWAYS strike downshift before N, even for annual compounding - multiplying years by 1 is harmless and builds the habit.
  - Rule (p. 101): compounding more frequently than annually -> GREATER future value.
- **FV Problem 3 (pp. 101-102):** Aniyah saves $500 at the END of each quarter for 10 years at 6% compounded quarterly. 4 P/YR; 500 +/- PMT (cash outflow -> negative); 6 I/YR; 10 -> 40 periods for N; solve FV = **27,133.95**. End-of-period payments = an "**ordinary annuity**." (p. 102)
- **FV Problem 4 (pp. 102-103):** same scenario but investing at the BEGINNING of each period (an "**annuity due**"): BEGIN mode; same entries; FV = **27,540.96** - higher than $27,133.95 because each deposit compounds longer. (p. 103)
- **FV Problem 5 (pp. 103-104):** final variation - Aniyah has ALREADY saved $5,000 and invests $500 at the beginning of each quarter at 6% compounded quarterly for 10 years: begin mode; 4 P/YR; 5000 +/- PV; 500 +/- PMT; 6 I/YR; 40 N; solve FV = **36,611.05**. Note (p. 104): representing each cash flow with the correct sign is critical - a positive PV with negative PMT would mean withdrawals, giving a very different answer.

### Calculating present value (PV) (pp. 104-108) [LO 3-3]
- PV matters when comparing dollar values received at different future points; PV = the CURRENT value of a future sum. Calculating forward to FV = **compounding**; calculating back to the present = **discounting**. (p. 104)
- Basic pattern: FV; I/YR; N (any order); solve PV. (p. 104)
- **PV Problem 1 (p. 105):** Huan wants $25,000 to start a side business in 7 years; he can earn 4% compounded semiannually - how much must he ALREADY have saved? END mode; 2 P/YR; 25000 FV (positive - cash he will have to fund the business); 4 I/YR; 7 -> 14 N; solve PV = **-18,946.88**. If Huan invests $18,946.88 today at 4% semiannual, he reaches $25,000 in 7 years.
- **"Present Value Problem 2" (labeled so in the book; it actually solves PMT) (pp. 105-106):** Simon wants $100,000 in his 401(k) in 10 years believing he can make 6%/year - annual contribution? END mode; 1 P/YR; 100000 FV; 6 I/YR; 10 N; solve PMT = **-7,586.80**.
- **Retirement lump-sum concept (p. 106):** you can solve for the lump sum needed at the START of retirement to pay an inflation-adjusted benefit for the rest of estimated life expectancy. Use an **inflation-adjusted ("real") return** - a return that considers inflation's effects - and **BEGIN mode** (funds are needed at the beginning of each year to pay that year's expenses). Pattern: PMT; I/YR (inflation-adjusted return); N; solve PV (annuity due - begin mode).
- **PV Problem 3 (pp. 106-107):** Suhana wants $25,000/year, adjusted for inflation annually, for an estimated 20-year retirement, using a 2% inflation-adjusted return: BGN mode; 1 P/YR; 25000 PMT (positive - she RECEIVES the payments to spend); 2 I/YR; 20 N; solve PV = **-416,961.55**. With $416,961.55 at the start of retirement earning a 2% real return, she can withdraw $25,000/year (the amount rises with inflation each year, maintaining buying power) and runs out of money after 20 years. Entering 0 for FV is optional. (p. 107)
- **PV Problem 4 (pp. 107-108):** same at a 3% real return - without clearing, just enter 3 I/YR and re-solve: PV = **-383,094.98**. A 1% higher inflation-adjusted return means Suhana needs almost **$34,000 less**. Interest-rate assumptions have a substantial impact on calculations, and the LONGER the time horizon, the MORE impact rate changes have (discussed more later). (p. 108)

### Calculating rates of return (I/YR) (pp. 108-111) [LO 3-4]
- Use when you know the starting balance, ending balance, and time between. Pattern: PV (changed to negative with +/-); FV (positive); N; solve I/YR (I/Y on the TI). One of PV/FV must be positive and the other negative or there is no solution - negative when cash leaves your hands (into an investment), positive when cash comes in (e.g., receiving an annuity payment). (p. 108)
- **Rate of Return Problem 1 (pp. 108-109):** Juliette invested $1,000 in a mutual fund 12 years ago; now worth $3,560 - annualized return? END mode; 1 P/YR; 1000 +/- PV; 3560 FV; 12 N; solve I/YR = **11.16%**.
- **Rate of Return Problem 2 (pp. 109-110):** George invested $150 EVERY MONTH for 5 years into a balanced mutual fund now worth $13,680 - annualized return with monthly compounding? END mode; 12 P/YR; 150 +/- PMT; 13680 FV; 5 -> 60 N; solve I/YR = **16.07%**.
- **Rate of Return Problem 3 (p. 110):** assume George invests at the BEGINNING of each month instead: don't clear - just switch to begin mode and re-solve: I/YR = **15.60%**.
- **Rate of Return Problem 4 (pp. 110-111):** George had ALREADY saved $12,500, deposited $150 at the end of each month, and the account grew to $27,300 in 5 years - annual rate of return? END mode; 12 P/YR; 12500 +/- PV; 150 +/- PMT; 27300 FV; 60 N; solve I/YR = **5.98%**.

### Calculating periodic payments (PMT) (pp. 111-114) [LO 3-5]
- Periodic payment calculations determine how much to save per period to reach a goal. As before: input three variables and solve for a fourth, or input four and solve for a fifth. Pattern: FV; I/YR (or I/Y); N; solve PMT. (p. 111)
- **Periodic Payments Problem 1 (p. 112):** the Andersons want $150,000 in 8 years for a mountain cabin, expect a 6% average annual return, and save at the BEGINNING of each year: BGN mode; 1 P/YR; 150000 FV; 8 N; 6 I/YR; solve PMT = **-14,297.54**.
- **Periodic Payments Problem 2 (pp. 112-113):** same but saving at the END of each year: switch to end mode; solve PMT = **-15,155.39** (end-of-year savers must save more).
- **Periodic Payments Problem 3 (pp. 113-114):** four variables, solve the fifth - the Andersons have ALREADY saved $20,000 toward the $150,000: BGN mode; 1 P/YR; 150000 FV; 20000 +/- PV; 8 N; 6 I/YR; solve PMT = **-11,259.13**.

### Calculating number of periods (N) (pp. 114-116) [LO 3-6]
- The unknown TVM variable can also be N, the number of compounding periods. (p. 114)
- **Number of Compounding Periods Problem 1 (pp. 114-115):** Sofia invests $1,000 believing she can earn 8% and wants to accumulate $3,500 - how many YEARS? END mode; 1 P/YR; 1000 +/- PV; 3500 FV; 8 I/YR; solve N = **16.28 years**.
- **Number of Compounding Periods Problem 2 (pp. 115-116):** Jacob invests $1,000 at 8% compounded SEMIANNUALLY, goal $3,500: END mode; 2 P/YR; 1000 +/- PV; 3500 FV; 8 I/YR; solve N = **31.94** - BUT that is total compounding periods, and the question asks for YEARS. Divide by the compounding periods per year: 31.94 / 2 = **15.97 years**. (p. 116)

## Chapter 3.3 - The Importance of Interest Rate Assumptions (pp. 119-123) [LO 3-7]

### Why rate assumptions matter (p. 119)
- To determine the savings/investment dollars and years needed for goals, several factors matter: the relevant time frame, the ultimate amount available for investment, and the client's risk profile - but perhaps the MOST significant factor is the **assumed rate of return**. In almost every forecasting situation, decisions rest on assumed future rates of inflation and rates of return.
- Effects of the assumptions: a HIGH assumed return + LOW inflation -> the planner recommends LESS investment for a goal; a LOW return + HIGH inflation assumption -> a LARGER investment is needed.
- A high inflation assumption can also LIMIT the available investment alternatives (high inflation hurts certain financial assets, especially **fixed income securities**) and can RAISE the future amount needed (e.g., college costs in 10 years grow more under higher inflation assumptions).

### Deciding which rates to use (p. 119)
- Because returns fluctuate widely, planners should be **somewhat more conservative** with assumed inflation and return rates during goal forecasting - better for clients to get surprises on the UPSIDE than the downside.
- Example: funding $40,000 of tuition in 10 years requires both a rate-of-return assumption AND an inflation assumption for future tuition costs. Forecasts fail when either assumption is too high or too low.
- **The conservative approach = assume a slightly LOWER rate of return and a slightly HIGHER rate of inflation.**

### Historical inflation (pp. 120-121)
- **Figure 3.1 - Average U.S. Inflation Rates, 1980-2022** (source: usinflationcalculator.com): wide range, from a HIGH of **13.5% in 1980** to a LOW of **-0.4% in 2009**. (p. 120)
- The approximate average inflation rate since 1980 is **3.36%**. Sounds modest, but at 3.36% average inflation your buying power is **cut in half in just 21 years** - 21 years from now it would cost $2 to buy what $1 buys today. Huge implications for retirement planning. (p. 120)
- Proof by calculator (p. 120): end mode, 1 P/YR, clear all; 1 +/- PV; 2 FV; 21 N; solve I/YR = **3.36%**.
- Same idea via DISCOUNTING (p. 121): start with $2 in the future and discount back 21 years to $1 - 2 FV; 1 +/- PV; 21 N; solve I/YR = 3.36%.

### Assuming various growth interest rates (pp. 121-122)
- Seemingly small rate differences have big long-term consequences. $10,000 over 20 years (end mode, 1 P/YR; don't clear between - just re-enter I/YR and re-solve FV):
  - 4% -> FV = **$21,911.23**
  - 6% -> FV = **$32,071.35**
  - 10% -> FV = **$67,275.00**
- The 10% assumption yields **over THREE times as much** over 20 years as the 4% assumption ($67,275 vs. $21,911). (p. 122)

### Overstating rate assumptions (p. 122)
- Any planner can make an apparently valid projection and be proved wrong later - one of the most important reasons to **review financial plans regularly**; incorrect assumptions get compensating adjustments periodically. Financial planning is an ONGOING, not a static, process.
- Clients may also lack funds to finance a goal: goals must be **prioritized**, met in "**stages**" over time, or in some cases scaled back.
- If reaching a goal requires a 16% annualized return and the client believes it's achievable, the planner should explain why it is NOT (if that's the case). **In no event should planners use unrealistic returns in projections** - they must always act in the client's best interests.

### The impact of inflation rates (p. 123)
- Use an **inflation-adjusted ("real") return** when forecasting retirement income and other future income streams: an investment returning 4% with 4% inflation has a real return of ZERO - no growth in real value.
- The inflation-adjusted rate is NOT simply return minus inflation. The formula:
  - **[(1 + rate of return) / (1 + rate of inflation) - 1] x 100**
- Skipping the proper inflation-adjusted rate when funding serial payments produces inaccurate results, and the inaccuracy grows as the inflation/return gap or the number of payments increases.
- **Problem 1 (p. 123):** 7% return, 3% inflation: 1.07 / 1.03 = 1.038835; (1.038835 - 1) x 100 = **3.88%** (rounded).
- **Problem 2 (p. 123):** 6% return, 2.5% inflation: 1.06 / 1.025 = 1.034146 -> **3.41%** (rounded).
- Quick double-check: the inflation-adjusted return is always a LITTLE LESS than (return minus inflation).
