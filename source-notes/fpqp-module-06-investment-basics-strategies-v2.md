## Introduction (p. 220)
- Many financial goals are achieved by saving and investing. Where you put your money is an investment decision, and options aren't limited to stocks and bonds. Major categories:

## Chapter 6.1 - Asset Classes (pp. 221-236) [LO 6-1 through 6-4]

- Begins with the most stable class, **cash and cash equivalents** - all part of the **money market** (vs. the **capital market**), short time horizon, relative liquidity. Generally, the more liquid an asset, the less its investment risk. Then covers stocks, bonds, and real estate.

### Cash and Cash Equivalents (pp. 221-224) [LO 6-1]
- **The Importance of Liquidity (p. 221):** an **emergency fund** is a foundational step; the best investments for it are cash and cash equivalents. **Cash** = legal tender (currency, coins, bank balances, money orders, checks). **Cash equivalents** = short-term investments with maturities of **90 days or less**, considered liquid. **Liquidity** = the ability to easily convert into cash with little or no loss of principal. Using stock investments for an emergency fund is unwise. Cash equivalents include: MMDAs, money market mutual funds, CDs, T-bills.
- **Money Market Deposit Accounts (pp. 221-222):** MMDAs pay more than savings accounts with accessible funds; they are **bank obligations and federally insured** (subject to the **$250,000 per ownership category** limit). Not the same as money market mutual funds (not federally insured). Require a minimum balance; offer debit cards/checks with limited monthly transactions.
- **FDIC Insurance (pp. 222-223):** covers cash/cash-equivalent deposits (e.g., a CD), not securities/mutual funds; money market mutual funds are NOT covered. Basic insured amount **$250,000**, separately per **ownership category** (single, joint, retirement/IRA, revocable trust - the last is per beneficiary).
  - **Example - Madison (p. 222):** joint savings $300,000 (with brother), checking $75,000, money market mutual fund $100,000, traditional IRA $275,000, CD $120,000 (joint with mother), CD $200,000. FDIC-insured total = **$710,000**: same-ownership cap $250,000 (of the $275,000 checking+solo CD); 50% of joint savings ($150,000); 50% of joint CD ($60,000); IRA up to $250,000. The money market mutual fund isn't covered. ($250,000 + $150,000 + $60,000 + $250,000)
  - **NCUSIF** covers up to $250,000 per account holder per institution in federally insured credit unions (same rules as FDIC).
- **Money Market Mutual Funds (p. 223):** a **mutual fund** (**open-end investment company**) pools investors' money, hires an adviser, and invests to meet objectives. Money market mutual funds hold high-quality short-term instruments (T-bills, commercial paper, negotiable CDs); typical minimum **$1,000**; withdraw anytime by check. Return is sensitive to the **federal funds rate**.
- **Certificates of Deposit (CDs) (p. 223):** fixed-income; maturities a few months to a few years (≤1 year for an emergency fund); early redemption may incur penalties; FDIC/NCUSIF insured in most cases.
- **Treasury Bills (p. 223):** backed by the U.S. government; terms of **4, 8, 13, 26, 52 weeks**; bought at a **discount**, mature at par (interest = the difference); liquid.
- **Summary (p. 224):** cash equivalents are kept liquid for emergencies; chasing higher returns risks loss.

### Common Stock (pp. 224-227) [LO 6-2]
- Stock = **equity**; the stockholder owns a proportionate share of assets/earnings. Equity's advantage over debt: no required fixed payments; disadvantage: dividends aren't tax-deductible (bond interest is). Equity investors demand higher returns.
- **Capitalization of a Business (pp. 224-225):** issuing **common stock** is a frequently used way to raise capital. Stock = **permanent capital** (no maturity). **Common stock** = direct ownership; **preferred stock** is more like debt (often convertible to common).
- **Public and Private Stock Ownership (p. 225):** **publicly owned** stocks trade on exchanges or **OTC**; **privately held** and **closely held** corporations are not exchange-traded; **venture capitalists** invest in start-ups; going public happens via an **IPO**.
- **Voting Rights (p. 225):** most common stock carries voting rights (one vote per share); multiple share classes can have different voting rights; absent shareholders vote by **proxy**.
- **Types of Return (pp. 225-227):** **capital appreciation** (growth) and **dividends** (income). Value drivers: sales/market share, gross revenues, net earnings, tangible assets, and **goodwill** (intangible value). Markets can be **bull** or **bear**; money needed within five years generally shouldn't be in stocks. **Dividends** are usually paid quarterly, board-approved; **qualified dividends** may be taxed at **0%/15%/20%**. **Total return** = capital appreciation + dividends.
- **Taxes and Inflation (pp. 227-228):**
  - **Tax-Adjusted Return = Return x (1 − Tax rate).** Sasha 8%, 24% federal + 2% state: 0.08 x (1 − .26) = **5.92%**.
  - **Inflation-Adjusted Return = (1 + Return) / (1 + Inflation) − 1.** Sasha 8%, 2% inflation: (1.08 / 1.02) − 1 = **5.88%**.
  - **Tax- AND Inflation-Adjusted:** compute tax-adjusted first, then inflation. [(1 + 0.0592) / 1.02] − 1 = **3.84%**.

### Bonds (pp. 228-234) [LO 6-3]
- **Terminology (pp. 228-229):** **debt** repays principal + interest on a schedule; bonds usually pay interest every six months and principal at the **maturity date**. Instruments maturing >1 year and ≤10 years are **notes**. Principal at maturity = **par value** (face/maturity value), min **$1,000**. The **coupon rate** sets each payment (a $1,000 par, 4% coupon = $40/year = $20 semiannual). **Zero coupon bonds** pay no interest, are bought at a deep discount, and pay par at maturity (buy $730, get $1,000 in 10 years).
  - **Table 6.1 (p. 229):** Regular bond - $1,000 par, 10 yrs, PV $985.53, 3.00% coupon, 3.17% market, $15 semiannual; Zero - $1,000 par, 10 yrs, PV $730.14, no coupon, 3.17% market, $0 payment.
- **Issuers of Debt (pp. 229-230):** **corporations** (risk of **default**; bondholders paid before stockholders in bankruptcy); **municipalities** - **GOs** (backed by full faith, credit, taxing power) and **revenue bonds** (serviced by projects; higher risk/yield); **U.S. government** - Treasury **bills** (≤1 yr), **notes** (1-10 yrs), **bonds** (30 yrs), inflation-adjusted **TIPS** (since 1997, CPI-U), and savings bonds **Series EE** ($25-$10,000, up to 30 yrs) and **Series I** (inflation-indexed: fixed rate + semiannual CPI rate).
- **Par, Discount, and Premium (p. 230):** below par = **discount** (coupon below current rates); above par = **premium** (coupon above current rates). **Yield to maturity (YTM)** = internal rate of return if held to maturity.
- **Calculations (pp. 231-233):** assume $1,000 par and semiannual payments unless stated.
  - **Current Yield = Annual interest / Current price.** Glenda: $50 / $980 = **5.1%**.
  - **YTM - National Corp (pp. 231-232):** 4% coupon, $1,000 par, 15 yrs, price $985. HP: 2 P/YR, END, C ALL; 1000 FV; 985 +/- PV; 20 PMT; 15 downshift N (30); solve I/YR = **4.14%**. (TI equivalent: 2ND P/Y 2, 2ND CLR TVM, same inputs, CPT I/Y = 4.14%.)
  - **Current Price (Intrinsic Value):**
    - **Acme, 5% coupon, 10 yrs, rates rise to 6% (p. 232):** $25 semiannual PMT; 1000 FV; 20 N; 6 I/YR; solve PV = **$925.61** (a **discount** bond).
    - **Rates fall to 4.5% (p. 233):** solve PV = **$1,039.91** (a **premium** bond).
  - **KEY - inverse relationship:** rates up -> prices down; rates down -> prices up.
- **Bond Indenture (p. 233):** the **indenture** specifies terms (coupon, maturity, collateral, covenants). **Protective covenants** require bondholders be paid before dividends. Violating a term = **default**; the **trustee** (usually a commercial bank) enforces provisions. **Default risk** is key for corporate bonds.
- **Bond Ratings (pp. 233-234):** letter grades; higher quality = lower default risk and lower coupon. Only corporate/municipal bonds are rated; U.S. government debt is highest quality.
  - **Table 6.2 (p. 234):** High grade - S&P AAA/AA, Fitch AAA/AA, Moody's Aaa/Aa; Medium - A/BBB, A/BBB, A/Baa; Speculative - BB/B, BB/B, Ba/B; Default/near default - CCC/CC/C/D, CCC/CC/C/DDD/DD/D, Caa/Ca.
- **Call Provision (pp. 234-235):** a **call provision** lets the issuer redeem the bond at the **call price** before maturity; a call price above face = **call premium**; **call protection** blocks calls for a set time (e.g., 5-10 yrs). A called bond forces reinvestment at lower rates = **reinvestment risk**. Illustration: a 7%, 30-yr bond callable in 5 yrs at $1,050 - if rates fall to 5.5%, it's likely called; the call premium is **$50**.

### Real Estate (pp. 235-236) [LO 6-4]
- A personal residence is a **personal use asset**. Real estate investing offers **leverage** (high debt financing) and deductions (mortgage interest, property taxes, depreciation).
- **Types (p. 235):** **Land** (raw/improved), **Residential** (homes, apartments, condos, hotels/motels), **Commercial** (offices, shopping centers, banks, restaurants, retail, stadiums), **Industrial** (plants, factories, warehouses).
- **Advantages/Disadvantages (pp. 235-236):** invest **directly** (ownership/general partnership - control) or **indirectly** (limited partnership, corporation, **REIT** - no control). Advantages: tax benefits, **inflation hedge**, leverage. Disadvantages: **illiquidity** (no national market; can be a **buyer's** or **seller's market**), management burden, high minimums, high transaction costs.

## Chapter 6.2 - Types and Measurements of Risk (pp. 237-242) [LO 6-5, 6-6]

### Types of Investment Risk (p. 237) [LO 6-5]
- Total (absolute) risk = **systematic (nondiversifiable) risk** + **unsystematic (diversifiable) risk**. Total risk is measured by **standard deviation**; systematic risk by **beta**. Because unsystematic risk can be diversified away, the risk to be most concerned with is **systematic risk**.

### Systematic Risk (pp. 237-238)
- **Nondiversifiable risk** - variability caused by factors affecting all comparable investments (economic, political, sociological changes). It CANNOT be diversified away within an asset class (owning 10 more stocks doesn't remove market risk; 10 more bonds doesn't remove interest rate risk). Exposure can be decreased by owning different types of assets (**diversification**).
- Examples remembered by **"PRIME":**
  - **Purchasing power risk** (aka **inflation risk**): inflation erodes buying power; an investment earning 5% with 4% inflation has a real pre-tax return of 1%. Bonds and preferred stocks are most affected (fixed dollar payments).
  - **Reinvestment risk** (reinvestment rate risk): rates may have fallen when payments are received, forcing reinvestment at lower rates (e.g., 4% bonds/CDs must be reinvested at 2.5%). Fixed-income investors have great exposure.
  - **Interest rate risk:** as rates rise, bond prices fall (and stocks/real estate can too). Fixed-income prices fluctuate inversely with rates.
  - **Market risk:** the most apparent systematic risk - being on the down side of a market cycle (political events, economic/social changes, investor psychology). Bearish sentiment drags most stocks down.
  - **Exchange rate risk** (currency risk): foreign investments - a rising foreign currency (falling dollar) makes money; a falling foreign currency (rising dollar) loses. Can also affect U.S. stocks with international business.

### Unsystematic Risk (pp. 238-239)
- **Diversifiable risk** - reduced/eliminated through diversification; unique to a firm, industry, or property (management, financial structure, labor strikes, consumer preferences). Types:
  - **Business risk (p. 239):** a firm's ability to operate profitably; a major risk with common stocks.
  - **Financial risk (p. 239):** related to the amount of debt a firm has.
  - **Credit risk (p. 239):** risk a bond (or preferred stock) is downgraded due to excessive business/financial risk.
  - **Default risk (p. 239):** a higher degree of credit risk - insufficient issuer cash flow to make interest/principal payments.
  - **Liquidity risk (p. 239):** uncertainty of converting an investment to cash quickly at or near the original principal.
  - **Marketability risk (p. 239):** (for this course) risk that an asset can't be converted to cash quickly at or near its current market price.
  - **Event risk (p. 239):** a security affected by an unanticipated, damaging event (major tax or regulatory change).
- **Table 6.3 - Types of Investment Risk (p. 239):**
  | Systematic Risk | Unsystematic Risk |
  |---|---|
  | Purchasing power risk | Business risk |
  | Reinvestment risk | Financial risk |
  | Interest rate risk | Default risk |
  | Market risk | Liquidity risk |
  | Exchange rate risk | Marketability risk |
  | | Credit risk |
  | | Event risk |

### Measuring Investment Risk (pp. 239-242) [LO 6-6]
- Risk and return must be viewed together - is the return high enough to justify the risk? (p. 239)
- **Risk-adjusted return** = return ÷ risk. Example (p. 240): Funds A and B both return 10%; Fund A std dev 15%, Fund B std dev 18% - Fund A has the better risk-adjusted return (less risk for the same return). Don't take more risk for a return achievable with less. Two main measures: **standard deviation** and **beta**.
  - **Figure 6.1 (p. 240):** Total Risk = Standard Deviation (Variability) = Systematic Risk (Beta / Volatility) + Unsystematic Risk.
- **Standard Deviation (pp. 240-241):** the single most important measure of a security's risk; measures **dispersion** (how far returns vary from the mean). Wider range = higher standard deviation ("bumpiness of the ride"). It measures **total risk**; a portfolio's standard deviation is lower when its investments are not highly correlated.
- **Beta (pp. 241-242):** a relative measure of **systematic risk/volatility**; best used within a diversified portfolio. The market has a beta of **+1.0**; **< +1.0** = **defensive asset** (less volatile), **> +1.0** = **aggressive asset** (more volatile). Example (p. 242): a 1.30 beta portfolio returns 13% when the market returns 10%; a 0.80 beta returns 8%. Negative betas are rare (move opposite the market). Beta gives a **risk-adjusted return** too:
  - **Example (p. 242):** Fund A return 12.50%, beta 1.18 -> 12.50 ÷ 1.18 = **10.59%**; Fund B return 9.75%, beta 0.85 -> 9.75 ÷ 0.85 = **11.47%**. **Return ÷ Beta = Risk-adjusted return.** Fund B is better despite the lower raw return. Choose the highest risk-adjusted return.

## Chapter 6.3 - Mutual Funds (pp. 243-248) [LO 6-7]

- A **mutual fund** (**open-end investment company**) pools investors' money and hires an adviser to invest per one or more objectives. Three fundamental objectives: **capital growth or appreciation**; **income**; **capital preservation**. A fund usually has one primary objective (the three can conflict).
- **Advantages (pp. 243-244):** **pooling of funds** (small investors get professional management; funds traded only at the **end of the day**); **diversification** (a risk-reducing strategy); **professional money management** (adviser hires a portfolio manager).
- **Costs (pp. 244-245):**
  - **Transaction costs (p. 244):** **load funds** carry a sales charge (added to NAV to get the public offering price); **no-load funds** don't (bought at NAV; families include Vanguard, Fidelity, T. Rowe Price). A **redemption fee** (often 1%-2%) may apply to shares sold within a short period (3-6 months).
  - **Operating expenses (p. 245):** **management fees**, **12b-1 expenses** (marketing/distribution; not all funds), and **other expenses**. The **expense ratio** = annual operating expenses / average net assets (bond funds < U.S. stock funds < international stock funds).
- **Prospectus & Summary Prospectus (pp. 245-246):** a **prospectus** is a legal disclosure document ("plain English" per SEC). A **summary prospectus** (condensed) must contain, in order: investment objective; fees and expenses (fee table, sales charges, operating expenses, breakpoints, portfolio turnover - not required for money market funds); investment strategies, risks, and performance (10-yr bar chart, best/worst quarterly returns, 1/5/10-yr average annual total returns); fund management; purchase/sale info; brief tax info; financial intermediary compensation.
- **Statement of Additional Information (SAI) (p. 246):** more detail than the prospectus (e.g., total brokerage commissions), sent on request. Funds report to shareholders at least twice a year and disclose holdings four times a year (available via the SEC's **EDGAR**).
- **Annual Report (p. 246):** must include "Management's Discussion of Fund Performance."
- **Types of Mutual Funds (pp. 246-247):** **index funds** (mirror an index; lower expenses) vs. **actively managed funds** (higher expenses). Categories:
  - **Bond (p. 246):** government (short/intermediate/long, mortgage-backed, TIPs), corporate (incl. high-yield junk), municipal (national/single state), foreign, multi-sector.
  - **Stock (p. 247):** by **capitalization** - **small cap** (up to $2B), **mid-cap** ($2B-$10B), **large cap** (over $10B); plus growth, value, index, sector, global, international.
  - **Blend (p. 247):** growth and income, equity income, balanced (~60/40 stocks/bonds), lifestyle (conservative/moderate/aggressive), target-date (more aggressive far from retirement). Lifestyle and target-date funds are "**fund of funds**"; target-date funds are common in 401(k) plans.
- **Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) (pp. 247-248):** open-end investment companies that trade throughout the day like stock; a basket following an index or a sponsor-created group (S&P 500, Dow, industry funds). Mostly passive; the first actively managed ETF began March 25, 2008. Transparent (disclose holdings daily); may have **tracking error**. ETF assets went over **$13 trillion in 2024**; can be bought on margin and sold short; some have options.
  - **Expenses (p. 248):** very low, usually slightly less than index funds; commissions apply on trades.
  - **Tax Efficiency (p. 248):** a major attraction (index funds have limited sales).
  - **Net Asset Value (p. 248):** ETFs trade at or near **NAV**, but price is set by supply/demand and can vary more from NAV in unsettled times.

## Chapter 6.4 - Investment Decisions (pp. 249-254) [LO 6-8]

- **Asset allocation** = apportioning wealth among countries and asset classes; the **main determinant of a portfolio's total return**. Broadened beyond cash/stocks/bonds to include real estate, international investments, collectibles, and precious metals.
- **Asset Allocation (pp. 249-250):** planners use optimization software based on goals, risk tolerance, time horizon, tax situation, forecasts. Client considerations: level of investment risk; past investment experience level; required rate of return for a goal; tax status. Four decisions (first three in an IPS): which **asset classes**; what **weights/percentages**; the allowable **allocation range**; which **specific securities**.
- **Time Horizons and Risks (pp. 249-250):** short-horizon investors (retirees) fear loss of principal (fixed-income tilt). Sample **conservative** allocation: **30% Stocks, 40% Bonds, 30% Cash**. Long-horizon investors (young professionals) want appreciation. Sample **high risk-tolerant** allocation: **70% Stocks, 20% Bonds, 5% Real Estate, 5% Cash**.
- **Asset Allocation Strategies (pp. 250-251):**
  - **Strategic asset allocation (p. 250):** the most common; optimal long-term balance (max return for min risk), a constant mix with periodic rebalancing to IPS weights.
  - **Tactical asset allocation (p. 250):** constantly adjusts the mix for market conditions (moving from overvalued to undervalued classes); active selection and market timing.
  - **Selection (pp. 250-251):** active managers tend toward tactical; buy-and-hold (passive) managers prefer strategic. Adhering to a chosen technique is the primary factor in long-term performance. The two basic styles are passive and active.
- **Buy-and-Hold Strategy (p. 251):** the classic **passive** long-term strategy (a benchmark). Advantages: minimizes transaction costs; long-term capital gains treatment (held >1 year); ensures you're not out of the market during an upturn.
- **Value and Growth Investing (p. 251):** **value** assumes the P/E is below its natural level and the market will correct it upward. **Growth** stocks have high earnings growth (15%+/yr), low dividends, above-average P/E, and higher betas. Value = slow and steady; growth = aggressive.
- **Investment Policy Statement (pp. 251-252):** an **IPS** is a written document of a client's objectives, boundaries on allocation/guidelines, and limitations on the manager. Not optional (adopted implicitly or explicitly). Main roles: readily implemented by current/future advisers (transportable); promote long-term discipline; protect against short-term shifts driven by panic or overconfidence.
  - **IPS template - "Investment Policy Statement for John Doe" (p. 252):** I. Investor Circumstances; II. Investment Objectives (General; Portfolio Return Objective); III. Risk Tolerance (Ability; Willingness; Risk Tolerance Objective low-high); IV. Liquidity Needs (Immediate cash needs); V. Time Horizon (15 years until retirement); VI. Tax Considerations; VII. Legal and Regulatory Factors; VIII. Other Unique Needs and Circumstances; IX. Asset Allocation example (Equity 70%, Bonds 20%, Real estate 5%, Cash 5%). Five constraints: (1) time horizon, (2) tax considerations, (3) liquidity, (4) legal/regulatory factors, (5) unique circumstances. The IPS is updated when circumstances or capital market conditions change (revising the **strategic asset allocation, SAA**).
- **SWOT Analysis (pp. 253-254):** **Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats.** Strengths/weaknesses = the **internal** environment (income, net worth, debt, insurance - client has some control); opportunities/threats = the **external** environment (interest rates, inflation, cost of education/retirement - no control). Helps focus the client and build an IPS.
  - **Example - Zack and Zoe Jones (pp. 253-254):**
    - **Strengths:** Zack's steady job in a recession-proof industry (>$70,000/yr); Zoe's part-time supplemental income; reasonable net worth for their age; both healthy with adequate health insurance.
    - **Weaknesses:** Zack wants a new (lower-paying) career; too much debt relative to net worth; unrealistic saving/investing goals; investments don't match risk tolerance; no long-term disability; inadequate estate planning.
    - **Opportunities:** historically low interest rates (affordable borrowing); a stock decline (chance for appreciation / dollar-cost averaging); lower housing prices.
    - **Threats:** high unemployment (may affect raises/employment); rising college costs (sending Zack Jr. to dad's alma mater); possibly increasing inflation.

## Module 6 Review Answers (pp. 256-259)

### Chapter 6.1 (pp. 256-257) [LO 6-1 through 6-4]
1. **Liquidity** is the ability to easily convert into cash with little or no loss of principal.
2. **Money market mutual funds** typically invest in high-quality short-term investments (U.S. Treasury bills, commercial paper, negotiable CDs). Typical minimum investment $1,000; funds can be withdrawn anytime without penalty by writing a check. Used as emergency funds (extremely liquid) and as a short-term depository for investors in managed (wrap) accounts awaiting a better time to invest.
3. Bond terms: a. **principal** - the money paid at maturity to the bondholder by the issuing corporation or government. b. **maturity date** - the date when the bond matures and principal is paid to the investor. c. **coupon rate** (nominal rate) - determines the annual dollar amount of interest; expressed annually in percentage but usually paid twice a year (a 3% coupon on a $1,000 par bond pays $30/year, or $15 every six months). d. **premium bond** - priced above par value in the secondary market. e. **discount bond** - priced below par value in the secondary market. f. **indenture** - the legal document describing (1) the terms and legal obligations the issuer must meet and (2) the rights of the bondholder.

### Chapter 6.2 (p. 257) [LO 6-5, 6-6]
1. **Standard deviation** tells an investor how far from the mean (average) return a security's returns are likely to vary; it measures dispersion (amount of deviation from the mean). A wider range of returns = higher standard deviation; a lower range = lower standard deviation. **Beta** is a relative measure of systematic risk or volatility; it may be used for a particular security but is better used within a diversified portfolio (or one with no unsystematic risk). Standard deviation is appropriate for all assets, including nondiversified portfolios.

### Chapter 6.3 (p. 258) [LO 6-7]
1. Mutual fund aspects: a. **pooling funds** - mutual funds pool investor funds to create the benefit of investing in many assets at a relatively lower cost. b. **diversification** - pooling allows better diversification than investors can achieve on their own (especially with limited resources). c. **professional money management** - an advantage over investors self-managing individual stocks and bonds. d. **trading** - mutual funds are traded only at the end of the day. e. **expenses, and transaction and operating costs** - may include transaction costs (buying/redeeming shares), operating expenses (the shareholder's portion of running the fund all year), a sales charge (though many funds have none), a redemption fee (for redeeming within a short time, generally 3-6 months), and other expenses (record-keeping, legal/auditing, directors' fees, custodial fees, annual meeting costs).

### Chapter 6.4 (p. 259) [LO 6-8]
1. Main roles of the **IPS**: be readily implemented by current or future advisers (transportable); promote long-term discipline for portfolio decisions; help protect against short-term shifts in strategy when markets or performance cause panic or overconfidence.
2. **SWOT** = **Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats** - helps the adviser and client understand the big picture; advisers develop an understanding of how internal and external environments affect their clients.
3. Client considerations in developing an asset allocation strategy: level of investment risk; past investment experience level; required rate of return for a goal; tax status.
