## Introduction (p. 262)
- Eventually, nearly everyone stops working for a living. Retirement is a dynamic concept - people are altering both their perception of retirement age and what retirement means.
- Traditionally, retirement income is illustrated as a **"three-legged stool"** from one of three sources: (1) **personal savings and investments**; (2) **employer-provided retirement plans**; (3) **Social Security**.
- One, two, or all of these can be lacking, so employment (part- or full-time) has become a sometimes necessary **"fourth leg."** Retirement looks different for each retiree.
- The module covers challenges/opportunities of retirement, the two phases (preparing/saving, then living it), sources of retirement income (Social Security, retirement accounts), and an overview of retirement pitfalls.

## Chapter 7.1 - Retirement Opportunities and Challenges (pp. 263-268) [LO 7-1]

### Overview (p. 263)
- We hear about the challenges of Social Security and that many haven't saved enough - but a sizable group is well prepared. A valuable adviser service is to **encourage saving**: when clients are told how much they need, the shock can lead to inaction. The key is to get started, save what you can, and go from there. A company 401(k) match left uncontributed is a guaranteed return and dollars left on the table.
- Various retirement income streams: Social Security; income from a pension or other retirement account; stock dividends; savings; annuity income; home equity; rental income; an inheritance; a full-time or part-time job.
- **"Do we know what we're saving for?" (p. 264):** per a recent Retirement Confidence Survey by the **Employee Benefit Research Institute (EBRI)**:
  - **28%** of retirees polled are *very* confident about a comfortable retirement.
  - **68%** of workers polled are *at least somewhat* confident about a comfortable retirement.
  - **74%** of retirees polled are *at least somewhat* confident they'll have enough for basic expenses.
  - **60%** of workers rank saving and investing among their top three longer-term priorities.
  - Concerns: government changes to the retirement system; inflation's impact. Workers **with retirement plans** were more than twice as confident as those without that they'd have enough for a comfortable retirement.
- **Reasons People Delay Saving for Retirement (pp. 264-265):** time is the greatest resource (compound interest, Module 3). Common reasons/excuses: procrastination; confusion about how to begin; poor cash management (can't afford to save); misperception about being too young; college education funding; paying off debt; saving for a house; costs of caring for aging parents; not wanting to "lock up" money in a retirement plan.
  - Per EBRI, about **19% of workers have saved less than $1,000** for retirement (most also have no plan) and **40% have less than $25,000** saved. This is why Social Security is so important - without it, paycheck-to-paycheck workers would have nothing. (p. 265)
- Challenges of aging: **health care and long-term care costs**. Ways to handle risk beyond insurance include staying healthy. **Medicare** (age 65+) has gaps needing extra coverage; it is health insurance, **not** long-term care insurance (assisted living/nursing homes aren't covered - that comes from LTC insurance and/or savings). If a retiree runs out of money, **Medicaid** is the safety net (spend down assets; Module 9). **Longevity risk** = outliving your money; controlled with proper allocation (pension/longevity annuity income that pays until death).

### Retirement - A Time of Growing Significance (pp. 265-266)
- Most Americans retire between **60 and 64** (many before 60). Someone who retires at 62 and lives to the average age of 83 spends **over 21 years in retirement** and 42 years working - nearly **25% of one's life** and nearly **33% of potential working life**. (p. 266)
- In 1900, a newborn male could expect ~46 years and a female ~49; few retired, and the average retirement age for working males was over 74 (70+ until 1950). In 2019, U.S. average life expectancy at birth was just over 79 (male ~77, female ~81); by 2030, an estimated 83.3 years for women and 79.5 for men (National Center for Health Statistics). Workers today mostly expect to retire at 65, 70, or older. Over the last century, the average expected retirement grew from under five years to more than 17 (a 300%+ increase); some planners advocate planning for a life expectancy of **100 years**.

### Calculating Longevity (pp. 266-267)
- Planning retirement length is necessary. These are **average** life expectancies - about half of retirees will live longer, so many planners add a **cushion** (often planning to age 100 or 120). A common rule of thumb: plan for **20 or more years**.

### Changing Demographics (pp. 267-268)
- The number of people over 65 is rising dramatically (a "tidal wave"). The **boomers** - nearly **80 million** born between **1946 and 1964** - are retiring at about **10,000 individuals each day**.
  - **Figure 7.1 - Defining the Generations (p. 267)** (Pew Research Center, ages as of 2018): Millennials (born 1981-96, ages 22-37); Generation X (born 1965-80, ages 38-53); Boomers (born 1946-64, ages 54-72); Silent (born 1928-45, ages 73-90).
  - **Figure 7.2 - Projected Population by Generation (p. 268):** Millennials passed baby boomers in size in 2019 (~72M rising to 75M); boomers (72M in 2019) decline as ~10,000/day retire; the smaller Gen X is expected to surpass boomers around **2028**; the Silent generation (~23M) declines.

## Chapter 7.2 - Sources of Retirement Income (pp. 269-276+) [LO 7-2 through 7-6] - PARTIAL

- Three main areas of retirement income: **personal savings and investments; employer-provided retirement plans; Social Security.** (p. 269)

### Personal Savings and Investments (p. 269) [LO 7-2]
- Assets owned outside any employer-funded accounts, in any of the four major asset classes (cash/cash equivalents, stocks, bonds, real estate).
- It's preferable to enter retirement with **little or no debt** - mandatory debt payments reduce flexibility, and the less income you have, the more burdensome debt becomes (a $300 payment matters much more on $1,200/month than on $5,000). **Unpaid federal student loans can cause garnishment of Social Security retirement benefits**; parents may carry their own debt or have co-signed for a child who stopped paying.
- The ideal is to pay off the mortgage and carry no credit card balances. A "traditional" retirement was working for a company for years and retiring with a **pension**, a paid-off home, and whole-life cash value for emergencies - debt-free, home owned outright, with pension + Social Security.

### Qualified Plans (pp. 270-275) [LO 7-3]
- A **qualified plan** is an employer-sponsored plan qualifying for special tax treatment under **IRC Section 401(a)**. Common features:
  - **Pretax contributions** (before income taxes).
  - **Tax-deferred growth** (earnings taxed only at withdrawal).
  - **Vesting** - if the employer contributes, employees may wait a period before entitled to those amounts plus interest. **Employee contributions are always 100% vested.**
- **Defined Benefit (DB) Plans (p. 271):** a **DB pension plan** provides a specified benefit at retirement - a dollar amount (e.g., $1,000/month) or a formula (e.g., 50% of final average compensation with 25 years of service). Being replaced by 401(k) plans in the private sector but still common in government jobs. Contributions are actuarially determined and paid into a **general trust fund** (no separate accounts); generally only employers contribute in the private sector.
  - **Example - Fred (p. 271):** age 65, retiring from Alpha Omega Limited; formula = 3% for each year of service on the average of his highest three years of compensation. 24 years of service; highest three years $68,000/$69,000/$70,000 (average **$69,000**). Benefit = $69,000 x 0.03 x 24 = **$49,680**. A popular option for married spouses pays the worker's benefit for life, then **50% to the surviving spouse** for life.
- **Cash Balance Plans (p. 271):** a **hybrid plan** (features of both DB and defined contribution). Governed as a DB plan by the DOL (offers lifetime retirement payments), but the promised benefit is stated as a **401(k)-style account balance** rather than a monthly income stream. More funds can be contributed than with a 401(k).
- **Profit Sharing Plan (PSP) (pp. 271-272):** gives employees a share of company profits via a retirement plan. Employer contributes to a **separate account** for each employee; can contribute even without profits and can skip contributions in a profitable year, but the IRS expects **recurring and substantial** contributions. Generally the same percentage for all employees (can be structured to favor older/highly compensated employees).
  - **Eligibility (p. 272):** typically age **21** and **one year of service**; a vesting schedule is likely. Annual employer contributions are limited to a max of **25% of participants' total compensation**; max includible compensation per participant is **$350,000 (2025)**. Deductible to the employer, excluded from employee income; grows tax-deferred.
  - **Contributions (p. 272):** under **IRC Section 415**, the max contributed to a participant's account for a plan year is the **lesser of 100% of compensation or $70,000 (2025)**.
  - **Example - Ace Moving Company (p. 272):** contributes 10% of compensation. Most employees earn ~$30,000-$40,000 (contributions ~$3,000-$4,000). The president earns $400,000, but includible compensation caps at $350,000, so the max on his behalf is 10% of $350,000 = **$35,000**.
  - **Example - Pamela's Pantry (p. 272):** contributes 25%. Executive Vance earned $345,000 in 2025; 25% would be $86,250, but the per-employee contribution caps at $70,000 (2025), so Pamela's Pantry contributes **$70,000** for Vance.
- **401(k) Plans (pp. 272-274):** a type of qualified plan; **Section 401(k)** lets the **employee** make pretax contributions. Both employer and employee may contribute. The employee defers a portion of salary (a **salary reduction** / **elective contribution**). Numbers have grown as companies shift away from DB plans.
  - **Matching (p. 273):** many plans match (not required) - e.g., a **100% match on the first 5%** of salary guarantees an immediate 100% return.
    - **Example - Todd (p. 273):** earns $50,000; Anvil matches 100% of the first 5%. Todd defers 5% ($2,500); Anvil adds $2,500. Coworker Maritza defers 2% ($1,000); Anvil matches $1,000 - to get the full $2,500 match she'd have to defer 5%.
    - **Example - Norio (p. 273):** Athena matches 30% on the first 10% of compensation. Norio earns $70,000, contributes 15% ($10,500). Athena matches 30% of the first 10% ($7,000): 30% x $7,000 = **$2,100** (it doesn't match the full $10,500).
    - Always try to maximize contributions to capture the match - unclaimed match is money left on the table.
  - **Limits (2025) (pp. 273-274):** employees must be age 21 with one year of service (1,000 hours). Employee **elective deferral** limit **$23,500**; age **50+** catch-up **$7,500**; under **SECURE 2.0**, ages **60-63** catch-up **$11,250** (2025). So the max elective deferral is **$31,000** (age 50+) or **$34,750** (ages 60-63). Employee elective and catch-up contributions are NOT subject to the 25% employer limit. Employees pay Social Security tax on deferrals but not federal/state income tax until withdrawal; no Social Security tax on employer contributions. The overall annual **additions** limit (employer + employee elective, excluding catch-up) is the **lesser of 100% of compensation or $70,000 (2025)**; max compensation used is **$350,000 (2025)**.
  - **Loan provision (p. 274):** common in 401(k)s (rare elsewhere) - typically repaid within **five years** in installments at slightly above prime, with after-tax dollars (interest paid back into the account). Disadvantage: on leaving the employer, the loan is often payable in full or treated as a **taxable distribution** (with a 10% penalty if under 59½).
  - Both for-profit and nonprofit companies of any size can sponsor 401(k)s; **government entities cannot**. Administrative expense means typically only larger companies offer them.
  - **Three ways a profit sharing 401(k) can be funded (p. 274):**
    1. **Employer discretionary contributions** - traditional "profit sharing," entirely employer-funded (often with a vesting schedule).
    2. **Employee elective deferrals** - "401(k)" pretax deferrals; employees are always **100% vested** in these. Auto-enrollment mandatory for most new employer plans starting 2025 (SECURE 2.0).
    3. **Employer matching and nonelective contributions** - matching = a set percentage up to a limit; nonelective = made for all employees regardless of deferral. Most employers prefer matching; may or may not have a vesting schedule.
  - Note: the **SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022** lets employers treat an employee's **student loan payments** as if they were 401(k) contributions for matching purposes (discretionary; not required). (p. 275)
  - **Table 7.3 - Employer-Provided Plans (p. 275):**
    | Plan | Shared | Unique Characteristics |
    |---|---|---|
    | **Defined Benefit/Pension** | Provide retirement income | Typically only employers contribute; general trust fund (no separate accounts); benefit is a defined dollar or formula amount; generous contribution limits; mostly phased out in the private sector (some governmental pensions; a resurgence for small businesses); can have age and years-of-service restrictions |
    | **Cash Balance** | Provide retirement income | Usually only employers contribute; general trust fund (no separate accounts); benefit is the accrued account balance; maximum benefits vary by age and are very generous (max allowable employer contribution funds a benefit of **$280,000 in 2025**); a resurgence of this hybrid DB plan for small businesses |
    | **Profit Sharing** | Provide retirement income | Share of company profits; employer must make recurring and substantial contributions (can be irregular - good for startups); can favor highly compensated/older employees; vesting schedule; age 21 and one year of service |
    | **401(k)** | Provide retirement income | Employees contribute; sometimes employers match; employee has a separate account; benefit is the accrued account balance; full-time employees age 21+ with one year of service; part-time employees eligible after two consecutive years of 500 hours (SECURE 2.0, effective 2025); 10% penalty on early withdrawals (amount also included in taxable income); more costly to administer |

### Other Tax-Advantaged Plans - Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) (p. 276+) [LO 7-4] - PARTIAL
- An **IRA** is a trust or custodial account created for the benefit of an **individual** (and, if not depleted during their lifetime, their beneficiaries). There are **no joint IRAs** - each IRA benefits its individual owner (the primary beneficiary). The trustee/custodian must generally be a bank, insurance company, or brokerage.
- **IRA Contributions (p. 276):** only an individual may contribute to a traditional or Roth IRA. Anyone with **earned income** can contribute; Roth contributions are also allowed at any age if the individual has earned income or is married to someone with earned income. **Earned income** = salary/wages (W-2) or self-employment income. 2025 contribution = the **lesser of $7,000 or 100% of earned income** (so someone earning $1,000 can contribute $1,000). Limits are inflation-adjusted.
- **Catch-up contributions (p. 276):** age **50+** by year end may add **$1,000** (2025).
- **Contribution dates and deadline (p. 276):** contributions for a tax year must be made by **April 15th** of the following year (a 2025 IRA contribution can be made January 1, 2025 - April 15, 2026); the deadline extends to the next business day if April 15th isn't one. Contributing early captures more **tax-deferred growth** (all return grows tax-deferred; taxation occurs only at withdrawal).
- Two IRA types: **"traditional"** IRAs (pretax contributions if eligible) and **Roth** IRAs (funded with after-tax dollars). [Detailed traditional-vs-Roth comparison continues on the next page - not yet captured.]
