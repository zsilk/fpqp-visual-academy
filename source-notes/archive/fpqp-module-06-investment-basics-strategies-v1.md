## Introduction (p. 220)
- Investments is a wide-ranging topic; many financial goals are achieved by saving and investing. People often think of stocks (perhaps bonds), but where you put your money is an investment decision and the options aren't limited to stocks and bonds (e.g., a savings account or real estate). The major categories are:
  - **cash and cash equivalents**
  - **stocks, or equities**
  - **bonds, or debt**
  - **real estate**
- Each category contains many investments. The module covers major classes (including grouping assets in mutual funds) and reviews risks, potential returns, and diversification of portfolios.

## Chapter 6.1 - Asset Classes (pp. 221-236) [LO 6-1 through 6-4]

- Begins with the most stable/foundational asset class, **cash and cash equivalents**. All the short-term vehicles here are part of the **money market** (as opposed to the **capital market**) and are characterized by a short time horizon and relative liquidity. Generally, the more liquid an asset, the less its investment risk. The chapter then covers stocks, bonds, and real estate.

### Cash and Cash Equivalents (pp. 221-224) [LO 6-1]

#### The Importance of Liquidity (p. 221)
- Creating/maintaining an **emergency fund** is a foundational step in sound planning - a financial "cushion." The best investments for an emergency fund are cash and cash equivalents. **Cash** is legal tender (currency, coins, bank balances, money orders, checks). **Cash equivalents** are short-term investments with maturities of **90 days or less** and are considered liquid. **Liquidity** is the ability to easily convert into cash with little or no loss of principal. Cash equivalents earn little, but funds are there when needed and principal is safe. Using stock investments for an emergency fund is unwise - the market could be down when you need the money.
- Cash equivalents include: **money market deposit accounts; money market mutual funds; certificates of deposit (CDs); U.S. Treasury bills.**

#### Money Market Deposit Accounts (pp. 221-222)
- **MMDAs** typically pay more interest than a savings account while keeping funds accessible. Not to be confused with money market mutual funds (offered by open-end investment companies and NOT federally insured). MMDAs are **bank obligations and federally insured**, subject to the **$250,000 per ownership category** limit - very safe and highly liquid. They require a minimum balance and offer debit cards and checks with a limited number of monthly transactions; investors may use them to park money for future investments.

#### FDIC Insurance (pp. 222-223)
- All individuals are entitled to **FDIC** insurance for a deposit at a U.S. financial institution (deposits payable in the U.S., not overseas). Only cash and cash-equivalent deposits (e.g., a CD) are covered; **securities, mutual funds, and other investments are not** - and money market mutual funds (unlike MMDAs) are denied FDIC coverage.
- The basic FDIC-insured amount is **$250,000**. Deposits in different **legal ownership categories** are separately insured, so a depositor can have more than $250,000 of coverage at one institution across categories. Common categories: **single ownership; joint ownership; retirement account (e.g., IRA) ownership; revocable trust ownership** (the $250,000 limit is per beneficiary). Each co-owner's shares across joint accounts at the same bank are added together and insured up to $250,000.
  - **Example - Madison (p. 222):**
    | Assets | Ownership | Balance |
    |---|---|---|
    | Savings account (ABC Bank) | Joint with brother | $300,000 |
    | Checking account (ABC Bank) | Madison | $75,000 |
    | Money market mutual fund (ABC Bank Securities) | Madison | $100,000 |
    | Traditional IRA (ABC Bank is custodian) | Madison | $275,000 |
    | Certificate of deposit (ABC Bank) | Joint with mother | $120,000 |
    | Certificate of deposit (ABC Bank) | Madison | $200,000 |
    - Madison's FDIC-insured amount is **$710,000**. Her same-ownership deposits total $275,000 ($75,000 checking + $200,000 solely owned CD), but same-ownership coverage caps at **$250,000**. FDIC covers 50% of the joint savings account (**$150,000**), 50% of the jointly owned CD (**$60,000**), and the IRA up to the **$250,000** limit. The money market mutual fund is **not** covered. ($250,000 + $150,000 + $60,000 + $250,000 = **$710,000**)
- Like FDIC, the **National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF)** covers up to **$250,000 per account holder per institution** in federally insured credit unions, with essentially the same rules. (p. 223)

#### Money Market Mutual Funds (p. 223)
- A **mutual fund** (also called an **open-end investment company**) pools the money of many investors, hires an investment adviser to manage the pool, and invests to achieve financial objectives. Open-end companies offer taxable and tax-exempt **money market mutual funds** that typically invest in high-quality, short-term instruments (U.S. Treasury bills, commercial paper, negotiable CDs). Typical minimum investment is **$1,000**; funds can be withdrawn anytime without penalty by writing a check. The rate of return is highly sensitive to short-term rates, particularly the **federal funds rate** (the overnight lending rate between Federal Reserve member banks). Used for emergency funds and as a short-term depository in managed (wrap) accounts. (Mutual funds are covered in more detail in Chapter 6.3.)

#### Certificates of Deposit (CDs) (p. 223)
- **CDs** are fixed-income investments from banks and savings and loan associations; maturities range from a few months to a few years. For an emergency fund, use maturities of **one year or less**. Early redemption may incur penalties. Principal is insured through FDIC or NCUSIF in most cases.

#### Treasury Bills (p. 223)
- **U.S. Treasury bills (T-bills)** are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government, issued for terms of **4, 8, 13, 26, and 52 weeks**. Buy directly at **TreasuryDirect (treasurydirect.gov)** or through brokerages (usually with a fee). T-bills are bought at a **discount** and mature at par (e.g., buy at $990, receive $1,000 at maturity - the interest is the difference). Liquid and easily traded; used to diversify and hold a secure, short-term investment.

#### Summary (p. 224)
- The role of cash/cash equivalents is sometimes underappreciated. Two things to remember: (1) this money is kept **liquid** for emergencies; (2) investors have no control over interest rates. Chasing higher returns with this portion risks loss because the higher-return investment carries more risk. Low-risk-tolerance investors may over-allocate to cash equivalents, sacrificing the higher returns of longer-term investments.

### Common Stock (pp. 224-227) [LO 6-2]
- Stock is frequently called **equity**. The equity owner (**stockholder**) is entitled to a proportionate share of the corporation's assets and earnings. In contrast, a bondholder gets a fixed payment and return of principal but no ownership.
- Equity's advantage over debt: no required fixed payments (which could deplete cash). Its disadvantage: **dividends are not tax-deductible** for the corporation, but **bond interest is**. Equity investors demand a higher return than bondholders because equities lack a fixed rate and payment schedule.

#### Capitalization of a Business (pp. 224-225)
- Businesses raise capital - often from the owner's funds, family/friends, or personal loans - and become self-sustaining. As they grow, owners must decide how to finance expansion; a frequently used method (beyond personal/family money) is issuing **common stock**.
- Stock is **permanent capital** (no maturity, unlike bonds). It's issued as **common** or **preferred**. **Common stock** = direct ownership. **Preferred stock** is more like debt (not direct ownership) but can often be **converted into common shares** under certain conditions. (p. 225)

#### Public and Private Stock Ownership (p. 225)
- **Publicly owned** stocks trade on exchanges or the **over-the-counter (OTC)** market - easily bought/sold. **Privately held corporations** are owned by a limited number of individuals and not exchange-traded (hard to buy/sell). **Closely held corporations** have most but not all shares held by one individual or family; they may also be owned by **venture capitalists** (who invest in start-ups before they go public). Small businesses issuing additional shares often become public via an **initial public offering (IPO)**; conversely, a public company can go private when large investors buy up the shares.

#### Voting Rights (p. 225)
- Most common stock carries **voting rights** - electing the board and voting on corporate policy, new securities, and major changes; **one vote per share**. Some companies have multiple classes (A and B) with different voting rights (even where one class has no vote). Shareholders who can't attend may vote by **proxy** (a written power of attorney to vote on their instructions).

#### Types of Return from Common Stock (pp. 225-227)
- Common stock offers two returns: **capital appreciation** (growth) and **dividends** (income).
- **Capital Appreciation (pp. 225-226):** growing public companies provide appreciation as revenues and earnings grow. A company's value is influenced by: **sales/market share** (percent of total industry sales), **gross revenues**, **net earnings**, **tangible assets** (land, buildings, equipment), and **goodwill** (intangible value - e.g., the "Microsoft" name's reputation/recognition, patents, creative properties). "Game changer" companies (Amazon, Apple, Google, Netflix) can be hard to value; hindsight is 20-20 (for every success, other once-promising companies vanished). The overall market can also be under/overpriced - stocks generally rise two out of three years but can have prolonged **bull** or **bear** markets. Because of this, money needed within the next five years should generally NOT be in stocks; for longer horizons (retirement), stocks offer much higher potential return than cash equivalents and bonds. (p. 226)
- **Dividends (pp. 226-227):** dividends are profits distributed to shareholders. Rapidly growing companies usually retain all earnings; larger, established companies retain some and pay some out (e.g., earn $1.00/share, pay $0.35, retain $0.65). Dividends are normally paid **quarterly** and must be approved by the board each time; companies avoid discontinuing expected dividends (a weak financial signal). Cash dividends are declared as income in the year received, even if reinvested. **Qualified dividend income** may be taxed at favorable long-term capital gain rates (**0%, 15%, or 20%**); most dividends from a domestic corporation's board are qualified. (Capital gains are covered in Module 8.) A stock's **total return** = capital appreciation plus dividends, if any. (p. 227)

#### Taxes and Inflation (pp. 227-228)
- Because taxes are often an individual's highest annual expense, a major investor goal is reducing current and future taxes. Remaining opportunities: **tax-exempt securities, tax-advantaged employee benefits, and awareness of exemption opportunities.**
- **Inflation** is a persistent increase in the price level that erodes purchasing power; the **inflation rate** is the percentage increase in prices, typically vs. the prior year.
- **Example - Tax- and Inflation-Adjusted Returns (pp. 227-228):**
  - **Tax-Adjusted Return = Return x (1 − Tax rate).** Sasha earns 8% and is in a 24% federal + 2% state bracket: 0.08 x (1 − .26) = 0.0592 = **5.92%**.
  - **Inflation-Adjusted Return = (1 + Return) / (1 + Inflation rate) − 1.** Sasha earns 8% with 2% inflation: (1.08 / 1.02) − 1 = 0.0588 = **5.88%**.
  - **Tax- AND Inflation-Adjusted Return (p. 228):** compute the tax-adjusted return first, then run it through the inflation formula. Using Sasha's 5.92% and 2% inflation: [(1 + 0.0592) / 1.02] − 1 = (1.0592 / 1.02) − 1 = 1.0384 − 1 = 0.0384 = **3.84%**.

### Bonds (pp. 228-234) [LO 6-3]

#### Bond Terminology (pp. 228-229)
- **Debt** is money borrowed, creating an obligation to repay the principal plus interest on a schedule. Bonds issued by corporations and governments generally pay interest **every six months** and repay principal at a future date; some short-term instruments (T-bills) pay interest and principal only at maturity.
- The date principal must be repaid is the **maturity date**. Bonds are typically issued for up to 30 years; instruments with maturities greater than one year and up to 10 years are called **notes**.
- A **bond** obligates the issuer to pay interest and principal when due. The principal paid at maturity is the **par value** (also **face value** or **maturity value**); most bonds have a minimum par value of **$1,000**. The **coupon rate** (the "coupon") is the annual interest rate that sets each payment. Example: a $1,000 par, 4% coupon bond pays **$40/year** (4% x $1,000), i.e., **$20 semiannually**.
- Exception - the **zero coupon bond** ("zeros"): no coupon rate and no interest payments during its life. Issued at a **deep discount** and pays par at maturity (e.g., buy for $730, receive $1,000 in 10 years - the $270 difference is the interest). (p. 229)
  - **Table 6.1 - Regular vs. Zero Coupon Bonds (p. 229):**
    | | Regular Bond | Zero Coupon Bond |
    |---|---|---|
    | Par value (face/maturity value) | $1,000 | $1,000 |
    | Term to maturity | 10 years | 10 years |
    | Present value | $985.53 | $730.14 |
    | Coupon rate | 3.00% | N/A |
    | Current market interest rate | 3.17% | 3.17% |
    | Coupon payment | $30 annually / 2 = $15 semiannually | $0 |
    | Amount at maturity | $1,000 | $1,000 |
- Bonds aren't always held to maturity: if rates fall, investors hold higher-rate bonds; if rates rise, they may sell to buy higher-paying bonds.

#### Issuers of Debt (pp. 229-230)
- Three main issuers: **corporations, municipalities, U.S. government.**
- **Corporations** (U.S. and foreign): the major risk is **default** (issuer unable to meet interest/principal obligations). Advantage over stock: in bankruptcy, bondholders are paid before common stockholders. Some corporate bonds are secured by assets (e.g., an airline's bonds secured by an airplane).
- **Municipalities** issue **municipal bonds**: **general obligation bonds (GOs)** (unsecured, backed by the issuer's full faith, credit, and taxing power) and **revenue bonds** (serviced by specific revenue projects like toll roads and hospitals). Revenue bonds tend to have higher risk and higher yields than GOs, though strong, predictable revenue streams can offer stronger assurances than GOs from struggling municipalities. Municipal bonds are mainly bought by individual investors and mutual funds, so they are less actively (thinly) traded in the secondary market than Treasuries. (p. 230)
- **U.S. government:** **Treasury bills** (maturities ≤1 year), **Treasury notes** (1-10 years), **Treasury bonds** (30 years). Inflation-adjusted **Treasury inflation-protected securities (TIPS)** (first offered **1997**) offer a return that rises with inflation as measured by the **CPI-U**. For smaller investors, **U.S. savings bonds** (**Series EE** and **Series I**): the electronic **Series EE** sells at 100% of face value in denominations from **$25 to $10,000** and earns interest up to **30 years**; the **Series I** is inflation-indexed, with a rate combining (1) a fixed rate for the life of the bond and (2) a semiannual inflation rate based on CPI changes.

#### Bonds at Par, Discount, and Premium (p. 230)
- Bonds are usually issued at par ($1,000 increments); many investors instead buy bond mutual funds for diversification. Prices fluctuate with interest rates and supply/demand. A bond trading **below par** is at a **discount** (e.g., $950; a large discount like $800 is a **deep discount bond**) - it has a coupon lower than current new-issue rates. A bond trading **above par** is at a **premium** (e.g., $1,030) - its coupon is higher than current new-issue rates, indicating rates have fallen; you "pay" for the above-market coupon by losing the premium at maturity. Discounts/premiums affect the **yield to maturity (YTM)** - the **internal rate of return**, i.e., the total return on a bond held to maturity.

#### Calculating Current Yield, Yield to Maturity, and Current Price (pp. 231-233)
- Assumptions: most U.S. bonds are **$1,000** par with **semiannual** payments (two per year) unless stated. When given a coupon rate, the semiannual payment = $1,000 x coupon rate, divided by two.
- **Current Yield (p. 231):** **Current yield = Annual interest / Current bond price.** Example: Glenda's corporate bond trades at $980 with $50/year interest -> $50 / $980 = .051 = **5.1%**.
- **Yield to Maturity (YTM) (pp. 231-232):** the total anticipated return if held to maturity (same as solving I/YR in Module 3).
  - **Example - National Corp (pp. 231-232):** 4% coupon, $1,000 par, matures in 15 years, price $985.
    - **HP 10bII+:** 2 P/YR, END mode, C ALL; 1000 FV; 985 +/- PV; 20 PMT (4% coupon = $40, /2); 15, downshift, N (30 periods); solve I/YR = **4.1352, or 4.14%**.
    - **TI BAII+:** END mode; 2ND P/Y 2 ENTER CE|C; 2ND CLR TVM; 1000 FV; 985 +/- PV; 20 PMT; 15, 2ND, xP/Y (30), N; CPT I/Y = **4.14%**.
- **Current Price (Intrinsic Value) (pp. 232-233):** the value of a bond based on its cash flows (interest payments act like an ordinary annuity).
  - **Example 1 - Acme Corp (p. 232):** 5% coupon, 10 years to maturity; if rates rise to 6%, what's the value? 5% coupon = $50/year = **$25 per semiannual payment**.
    - **HP:** 2 P/YR, END mode, C ALL; 1000 FV; 25 PMT; 10, downshift, N (20 periods); 6 I/YR; solve PV = **−925.61, or $925.61**.
    - **TI:** END mode; 2ND P/Y 2 ENTER CE|C; 2ND CLR TVM; 1000 FV; 25 PMT; 10, 2ND, xP/Y (20), N; 6 I/Y; CPT PV = **−925.61, or $925.61**. At 6%, intrinsic value $925.61 - a **discount** bond.
  - **Example 2 (p. 233):** if rates fall to 4.5%: same inputs with 4.5 I/YR -> PV = **−1,039.91, or $1,039.91** - a **premium** bond. (The negative sign on PV is just calculator logic.)
  - **KEY - inverse relationship:** when interest rates go **up**, bond prices go **down**; when rates go **down**, bond prices go **up**.

#### Bond Indenture (p. 233)
- Each bond issue has a legal document, the **indenture**, specifying terms (coupon rate, maturity date, collateral, restrictions on additional borrowing, etc.). It typically requires bondholders be paid interest before any dividends to common shareholders - these **protective covenants** add assurance that interest and principal will be paid. Violating any indenture term puts the bond in **default** (most commonly a missed payment). The **trustee** (usually a commercial bank) ensures indenture provisions are followed and acts for bondholders on default. **Default risk** is an important risk for corporate bonds.

#### Bond Ratings (pp. 233-234)
- **Bond ratings** are letter grades indicating quality; higher quality = less default risk and generally lower coupon rates. Example: an AAA (investment grade) bond might yield 3% while a BB (junk) bond yields 6% - the BB pays more but risks default. Ratings are given only to **corporate and municipal** bonds; U.S. government-backed debt (Treasuries, Ginnie Maes) is the highest quality.
  - **Table 6.2 - Rating Classifications of the Three Rating Agencies (p. 234):**
    | Grade | Standard & Poor's | Fitch | Moody's |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | High grade | AAA, AA | AAA, AA | Aaa, Aa |
    | Medium grade | A, BBB | A, BBB | A, Baa |
    | Speculative | BB, B | BB, B | Ba, B |
    | Default or near default | CCC, CC, C, D | CCC, CC, C, DDD, DD, D | Caa, Ca |
    - Note: each category represents more than one rating (e.g., S&P high grade may be AAA or AA).

#### Call Provision (pp. 234-235)
- Bonds issued when rates were higher benefit the bondholder but trouble the issuer, so many are issued with a **call provision** letting the issuer "call in" and redeem the bond at a predetermined **call price** before maturity. A call price above face value creates a **call premium** (often one year of interest). Bonds usually can't be called for a set time (e.g., 5 or 10 years) - **call protection**.
  - Illustration: a 7%, 30-year, $1,000 face value high-yield bond callable in five years at $1,050. If rates fall to 5.5% in five years, the issuer will likely call it (retire 7% bonds, reissue at 5.5%, saving 1.5% per bond - significant across large issues). The call price is $1,050; the call premium is **$50**. The risk of a bond being called is **call risk**; if called, the bondholder must reinvest at lower rates, incurring **reinvestment risk**. (p. 235)

### Real Estate (pp. 235-236) [LO 6-4]
- Many own a personal residence they consider an investment, but more appropriately a personal residence is a **personal use asset**. Real estate investing offers benefits including **leverage** (using a high percentage of debt financing to acquire property) and tax deductions (mortgage interest, property taxes, depreciation).

#### Types of Real Estate (p. 235)
- **Land:** unimproved (raw) or improved (farm/ranch land, lots with curbs/gutters awaiting improvement, recreational property such as a lake with campgrounds).
- **Residential real estate:** buildings where people live and pay rent - single-family homes, apartments, condominiums, hotels, motels.
- **Commercial real estate:** buildings rented to a business - office buildings, shopping centers, banks, restaurants, retail stores, football stadiums. (Hotels, motels, and apartments may also be commercial when owned by publicly traded corporations run as businesses.)
- **Industrial real estate:** buildings rented for a plant, factory, industrial park, power plant, or warehouse.

#### Advantages and Disadvantages of Real Estate Investing (pp. 235-236)
- Investors can invest **directly** (outright ownership or a general partnership) or **indirectly** (a limited partnership, a corporation, or a **real estate investment trust (REIT)**). Key difference is **control** - direct investors control management decisions; indirect investors do not.
- Advantages: tax advantages (some covered in Module 8); historically an effective **hedge against inflation** (as inflation rises, so does real estate value); readily available **leverage** (banks lend to buy real estate). (p. 236)
- Disadvantages: the greatest is **illiquidity** - like other real (tangible) assets, real estate is hard to convert to cash quickly; there's no national/regional market as with stocks/bonds, so sellers may wait months or years. Real estate is regional and can be a **buyer's market** (more inventory than demand - buyers have the advantage) or a **seller's market** (not enough inventory - sellers have the advantage). Other drawbacks: management burden, high minimum investment amounts, and high transaction costs.
