## Chapter 4.1 - Principles of Risk Management (pp. 137-154) [LO 4-1 through 4-3]

### The need for financial security (pp. 137-138)
- Psychologist Abraham Maslow's **hierarchy of human needs**: the most basic human need is physiological (food, shelter, protection from the elements); the next level up is **security/safety** - people want protection from crime, illness, and other threats to well-being. (p. 137)
- **Figure 4.1 - Maslow's Hierarchy of Human Needs** (bottom -> top): (1) physiological needs (breathing, food, water, shelter, clothing, sleep); (2) safety and security (health, employment, property, family and social stability); (3) love and belonging (friendship, family, intimacy, sense of connection); (4) self-esteem (confidence, achievement, respect of others); (5) self-actualization (morality, creativity, spontaneity, acceptance). (p. 137)
- Until basic survival and security needs are met, goals tend to be SHORT term; once they're satisfied, LONG-term goals can develop. (p. 137)
- **Figure 4.2 - Hierarchy of Financial Goals** (base -> top): base tier = Food/Shelter | Emergency fund | **Insurance coverage** (e.g., life, health, auto, disability); next = College funding, Retirement planning, Mortgage payoff; next = Business ownership, Vacation home; top = Travel. **Insurance sits on the base tier and should be handled FIRST**, before goals like retirement funding. (p. 138)

### Loss probabilities & the risk management process (p. 138)
- Many risks (a six-month disability, a house fire, a car crash injuring someone) can derail a financial plan. Some are insurable; some are not appropriately managed with insurance.
- **Insurance management** = the (narrower) process of deciding how much and what kind of insurance to buy. **Risk management** = the broader process of addressing all forms of risk and determining which risks are insurable and which are not. Risk management INCLUDES insurance management but covers much more.
- **The 5 steps of the risk management process:**
  1. **Clarify objectives** - what are the individual's/business's goals regarding managing risk?
  2. **Identify the risk** - what risk will prevent meeting the objectives?
  3. **Measure the risk** - what is the magnitude of the identified risk?
  4. **Manage the risk** - how will it be controlled or financed?
  5. **Monitor the risk and its management** - review regularly, based on the level of risk and its probability.

### Types of personal risk (pp. 139-140) [LO 4-1]
Among the most recognized personal risks is the potential **loss of income** - via loss of employment, disability, premature death, or divorce.
- **Unemployment (p. 139):** usually temporary, but without an emergency fund a jobless period can wipe out savings meant for vacations, education, or retirement. People often borrow (e.g., high-interest credit cards) - increasing debt while unemployed is generally a poor option.
- **Disability (p. 139):** clients CAN personally insure against lost income from disability. The probability of becoming disabled at any given age is GREATER than that of premature death. Two coverage types: **short-term** (generally up to one year) and **long-term** (generally up to age 65). Long-term disability is often overlooked; the potential lost income stream from permanent disability can be very large.
- **Death (p. 139):** a wage earner's death interrupts family income. Many own some life insurance, but coverage may not be adequate. Especially important for wage earners who are married and/or have children.
- **Divorce (pp. 139-140):** income-loss risk is real. When someone becomes suddenly single, living costs don't drop by half (auto insurance for one isn't half a couple's cost; rent/utilities exceed half). A two-income family becoming two single-income families means tighter budgets; add the loss of assets in a settlement. If investment assets are awarded to the former spouse, both the income AND the cash flow from selling those assets are lost. Some planners recommend **prenuptial agreements** (especially with remarriages or children from previous relationships), and some specialize in financial planning for divorce.

### Catastrophic losses (p. 140)
- Everyone faces possible catastrophic expenses. A common one is **medical**: a one-year back injury might cost $50,000+ in medical bills (plus lost earnings). Fire, flood, or earthquake can destroy property in minutes; a car can be stolen or a home vandalized. Without a risk management plan, such losses can be catastrophic.

### Negligence (pp. 140-141)
- Someone whose negligent/reckless act causes another's loss can lose ALL their assets in a lawsuit. Damages can be substantial, and legal fees are considerable even if found not liable (judges may not award attorney's fees to defendants, even when innocence is clear). Behaving responsibly doesn't guarantee no lawsuit; being liable isn't necessarily being wrong.
- **Four components of negligence** an injured person must prove to collect damages:
  1. **Existence of a legal duty** - e.g., homeowners must maintain property so as not to cause injury; drivers must drive safely.
  2. **Failure to perform a legal duty** - by ACTION (running a red light and hitting a car) or FAILURE to act (neglecting to repair a rotted deck that injures a guest).
  3. **Actual damage is caused** - e.g., a claimant hit by a red-light runner, or falling through a rotten deck and breaking a leg.
  4. **Proximate cause is established** - the unbroken chain of events between the negligent act and the damage. Example: Motorist A runs a red light and narrowly misses Motorist B; B then pulls out a phone and, two blocks later, rear-ends another car - A is NOT liable (too many intervening events). But if B swerves into a light post to avoid A, A IS liable (A was the proximate cause of B's swerve). (pp. 140-141)
- **Legal defenses against negligence (p. 141):**
  - **Contributory negligence:** if the injured person contributed IN ANY WAY, they cannot collect damages. (Some states require at least a 50% contribution by the claimant for a zero award.)
  - **Comparative negligence** (used far more often): damages are reduced by the injured party's degree of fault. E.g., B might still collect 75% from A if B's speeding was only 25% of the cause.
  - **Assumption of risk:** voluntarily exposing yourself to a known danger bars collection. Example: skiing - in many jurisdictions skiers assume the risk and cannot sue the slope owner/operator.
  - **Last clear chance:** if B SAW A about to run the red light but accelerated in anyway ("he had the right of way and wasn't going to give it up to some lawbreaker"), B could be held liable - B had a last clear chance to avoid the crash but entered anyway.

### Insuring potential catastrophic losses (p. 141)
- With a **limited insurance budget, insure against catastrophic losses FIRST**, even if unlikely; insure smaller losses only if money remains. Individuals can usually cover small losses out of pocket - it's the large UNINSURED loss that causes financial hardship or ruin (replacing a stolen car stereo is manageable; totaling a car that still has a $30,000 loan balance is not). Examples of insurance protecting against large catastrophic losses: homeowners, long-term disability, health, and umbrella liability insurance.

### Risk management techniques and examples (pp. 142-143)
- **Four essential risk management techniques:** risk avoidance, risk reduction, risk retention, risk transfer.
- **Scenarios (p. 142):**
  1. Inga won't let son Dmitri ride a motorcycle = **avoidance**. Dmitri wearing protective clothing/helmet = **reduction**. Buying the motorcycle on credit and risking losing it if he misses payments = **retention** (he's willing to retain that risk).
  2. Bill & Charlene want a traffic light instead of a stop sign to protect their kids = **reduction**; when their kids climb a tree they assume the injury risk but **transfer** most of the large-medical-expense risk via health insurance.
  3. Koby & Abriana buying life insurance = **transferred** the risk of lost earnings at either of their deaths.
  4. DDC Associates forming an **LLC**: each owner's financial liability is limited to their contribution and shared equally - incorporation = **risk reduction** for the owners and **risk transfer** to the corporate entity.
  5. "When in doubt, throw it out" leftovers = mom's version of **avoidance + reduction**: two financial risks are (1) tossing food wastes a few dollars, or (2) bad food means doctor bills + prescriptions; tossing it reduces the financial risk to just a few dollars. (pp. 142-143)
- **Definitions (p. 143):** **Risk avoidance** = finding a way around a risk (don't skydive if you don't want skydiving injuries). **Risk reduction** = lowering the chance of a loss OR its severity. **Risk retention** = keeping the risk yourself; you bear any loss (physical injury, property damage, legal liability). **Risk transfer** = mainly through insurance.
- **Two categories - risk control vs. risk financing (Table 4.1, p. 143):**
  | Risk Control | Risk Financing |
  |---|---|
  | Avoidance | Retention |
  | Reduction | Transfer |
  - Avoiding/reducing = CONTROLLING how you handle the risk; retaining/transferring = FINANCING the risk (paying premiums if you transfer, paying out of pocket if you retain).
- **Table 4.2 - Risk and Loss (p. 143):**
  | Risk | Maximum possible loss | Probability of loss |
  |---|---|---|
  | Liability due to negligence | Unlimited | Low |
  | Premature death | Generally the PV of future earnings | Generally low but depends on health/lifestyle |
  | Disability | Generally the PV of future earnings | Higher than premature death but relatively low in general |
  | Health care costs | Unlimited | High for small claims but generally low for catastrophic claims |
  | Long-term care costs | ~$48,000 to $120,000 annually (varies by level of care & geography) | High once client reaches age 60 |
  | Damages/losses to personal property | Limited to the property's value | Generally low |
  - Two perils GENERALLY EXCLUDED from most homeowners policies: **floods and earthquakes**. Clients in prone areas may add an endorsement or buy a separate policy. (p. 143)

### Rules of risk management (pp. 144-145)
- From Robert Mehr and Bob Hedges, *Risk Management in the Business Enterprise*, **three rules**:
  1. **Don't risk more than you can afford to lose** - determine how much loss you can retain without negative consequences. A home destroyed by fire is significant; a broken window isn't. You likely can't immediately replace a NEW car if it's stolen/destroyed, but replacing an OLDER car is easier.
  2. **Consider the odds** - if a loss is certain, insurance costs about the loss amount plus admin costs. Insuring front-yard pink flamingos that became valuable collector's items (and will likely be stolen) makes no economic sense - reduce the risk instead. Some people keep jewelry in a safe deposit box, taking it out once or twice a year; less exposure time = greatly reduced insurance cost.
  3. **Don't risk a lot for a little** - a $250,000 house vs. a $2,400 homeowners premium: NOT insuring would be risking a lot for a little.
- **Table 4.3 - Four Basic Methods of Handling Risk (p. 145):**
  | | High probability (frequency) | Low probability (frequency) |
  |---|---|---|
  | **High severity** | Risk Avoidance; Risk Reduction | **Risk Transfer** |
  | **Low severity** | Risk Retention; Reduction | Risk Retention |
  - Race-car-driver example: risky drivers -> avoidance (change profession) or reduction (race less, safer tracks, more safety features); safer drivers -> transfer (obtain adequate insurance).
  - Bicycle-rider example: reckless rider -> retention (do not use risk management) or reduction (wear a helmet/safety gear, ride only in low traffic or less often); safe rider -> retention (do not use risk management).
  - **KEY:** high-severity + low-probability events are the ones you **insure FIRST** - the ones that could be catastrophic. (p. 145)
- **Self-insurance (p. 145):** setting aside funds to cover potential losses (auto, home, health, even life). The term can mislead - no policy is bought and risk ISN'T transferred to a third party; funds are simply earmarked for losses, making it a form of well-thought-out **risk retention**. Companies that self-insure employee health build policies/processes that mimic insurance to manage cost-sharing and claims.

### Principles of insurance (pp. 145-146) [LO 4-2]
- **Risk** = a condition with the possibility of an adverse result versus the expected desired outcome; not subjective, but a condition of everyday life. Generally measurable by actuaries via the likelihood of an occurrence in a large, similar (homogeneous) group.
- **Pure risk** = you assume things continue as expected, barring an unforeseen loss: either NO loss or a loss occurs - loss or no loss, but NEVER a gain. (pp. 145-146)
- **Speculative risk** = the probability that EITHER a loss OR a gain occurs (stock market investing, gambling). Insurance will NOT cover speculative risks - they can't be properly evaluated or measured. (p. 146)
- **Four types of pure risk (p. 146):**
  1. **Personal risk** - becoming unable to earn income, causing loss of income and/or assets.
  2. **Property risk** - losing property (real or personal) to damage, destruction, or theft.
  3. **Liability risk** - accidentally causing a loss to another person.
  4. **Failure of others** - a loss from another person's action or inaction (e.g., paying a contractor for a patio who brings materials once and never returns).

### Perils, and moral & morale hazards (pp. 146-147)
- **Peril** = a cause of loss (fire, hurricane, explosion, heart attack, cancer). Policies insure against losses from perils.
- **Hazard** = something that INCREASES the chance of loss. Example: if a house fire is the risk, FIRE is the peril, and a pile of OILY RAGS in the garage is the hazard. (p. 146)
- **Moral hazard (p. 146):** exists when people are DISHONEST - e.g., an employee stealing from an employer, or an insured lying to boost a claim. It's the primary source of insurance fraud. Example: an employee facing layoffs suddenly claims "disability" ("Oh! My back!").
  - *Institutional example:* a repair shop using cheaper/inferior parts or overbilling the insurer commits insurance fraud. Insurers set MAXIMUM amounts they'll pay for a given repair to avoid moral hazard.
- **Morale hazard (pp. 146-147):** occurs when people are CARELESS or think "the insurance company will pay, so don't worry about costs." Example: handed the keys to a $100,000 car with NO insurance, you drive very carefully (or not at all); WITH insurance, you're more relaxed and possibly less careful. Morale hazard also appears when insurance is available to people who build on the beach in North Carolina, in the Colorado mountains, or on California cliffs - without insurance, less building would occur given the perils of hurricanes (beach), fire (mountains), and mudslides (cliffs).
- Note (p. 147): the insurance industry also uses "**risk**" to mean the PERSON or PROPERTY insured (e.g., an agent checking whether your house is an "acceptable" risk).

### Underwriting (p. 147)
- Origin: 15th-century ocean marine insurance in England - a merchant listed the ship, destination, cargo, etc., and individuals wrote their names and the conditions under which they'd accept a portion of the risk UNDER that description - writing "under" the agreement = **underwriting**.
- **Underwriting** = determining whether a risk is acceptable and, if so, at what price. Applications go to the company for underwriting; the **underwriter** is the employee who selects or rejects risks. When a policy is issued, the risk is "underwritten" - accepted and transferred from the insured to the company.

### Insurable risks - the four elements (pp. 147-148)
An insurable risk must meet FOUR requirements:
1. **The law of large numbers must apply** (a large enough number of similar exposure units).
2. **The loss must be accidental or fortuitous** (unintentional).
3. **The loss must be definite and measurable.**
4. **The loss must NOT be catastrophic to the insurance company.**
- **Law of large numbers (pp. 147-148):** with enough similar risks (e.g., houses), you can predict the group's losses; more risks = more accurate predictions. Insure 50,000 homes -> predict destroyed/damaged/burglarized/etc. -> convert to expected claim dollars, add operating costs, divide total expected outlay by the number of homes (adjusted for each home's value) -> the premium. (Simplified; real underwriting also weighs construction quality, distance to a fire station, area claims history, etc.)
- **Accidental/fortuitous (p. 148):** you can't insure what you control or what's certain; policies exclude intentional acts by the policy owner. A house burning from a wire short is covered (unless the short sparked for weeks and was ignored); setting your own house on fire is NOT. Exception: **death** - certain AND insurable, because although we'll all die, the TIMING is unknown (fortuitous).
- **Definite and measurable (p. 148):** the insurer must confirm a loss occurred and assign a dollar amount. Life insurance = the death benefit; health insurers measure losses via provider payments; a destroyed home's rebuild cost can be determined even before the loss.
- **Not catastrophic (p. 148):** your house burning is catastrophic to YOU but just one of many to the insurer. A catastrophic loss to the INSURER (thousands of homes burning) could put it out of business. **Hurricane Katrina (2005)** illustration: hit southeastern Louisiana and western Mississippi; **$41 billion in insured losses**; homeowner carriers there lost **$8 billion** - the worst insured loss event in insurance history to date. Some companies couldn't pay all claims and remain solvent because too many insured risks were geographically clustered on the western Gulf Coast; **Hurricane Rita** struck three weeks later, worsening it.

### The insurance contract (pp. 148-150)
- All valid insurance contracts must (p. 148): deal with a **legal activity/object** to be enforceable; consist of an **offer and an acceptance**; and provide for **consideration** (payment or performance).
- **Insurable interest (p. 149):** the applicant must face a personal financial loss if the covered event occurs. Life insurance: insurable interest required **at the time the policy is issued**. Property insurance: insurable interest required **both at issue AND at the time of the claim**.
- **Consideration (p. 149):** an exchange of value. For most insurance, the PROMISE to pay the premium is sufficient consideration; for LIFE insurance, the premium must be PAID before the contract binds the insurer. Submitting a completed application (offer) + the first premium (consideration) generally creates a binding contract if the application passes underwriting.
  - *Example:* attorney Karen helps neighbor Jeff with a real estate purchase in exchange for a car tune-up - consideration flows both ways even though no money changes hands. The insurer's consideration is its PROMISE TO PAY if a covered event happens; the policy owner's consideration is the PREMIUM.
- **Unilateral vs. bilateral (p. 149):** BILATERAL = both parties make enforceable promises ("I'll paint the fence by Tuesday, and you'll pay me $75"). UNILATERAL = only one party promises ("If you paint the fence by Tuesday, I'll pay $75" - no commitment to paint). **Insurance contracts are UNILATERAL** - only the insurer's promise to pay is legally binding; the insured pays premiums but makes no enforceable promise and can stop paying anytime (after which the insurer is no longer obligated).
- **Indemnity (pp. 149-150):** when people suffer a loss they should be **"made whole"** - restored to the position they'd have been in without the loss - but NOT profit from it. Example: $150,000 of coverage on a home destroyed by fire, but rebuilding costs only $138,000 -> the insurer pays $138,000. Parking-lot car damage: your insurer pays for repairs, then the at-fault driver's insurer pays; a **subrogation clause** (p. 150) says YOUR insurer is reimbursed before you receive more money - part of indemnity (made whole, not enriched).

### Insurance policy sections (p. 150)
All policies share the same **four basic sections**:
1. **Declarations** - statements of the insured/policy owner; info from the applicant or the application itself.
2. **Insuring agreement** - identifies what is insured, for what amount, under what conditions (auto: the vehicle & its coverages; health: the list of covered care types).
3. **Exclusions** - states what is NOT covered.
4. **Conditions** - what must happen for the insurer to be obligated to pay; the rights and duties of both parties; often explains how to file claims (claim form, proof of loss, value of loss - a big homeowners loss may need appraisals, receipts, estimates, inventories; life insurance generally needs only a claim form + death certificate).
- **Riders and endorsements (p. 150):** change or modify coverage. "**Rider**" is typically used with LIFE and HEALTH policies; "**endorsement**" with PROPERTY and LIABILITY policies. Typical life riders: **disability waiver of premium** (if disabled, the insurer waives - effectively pays - the premium), **accidental death benefit (ADB)** (extra payout for accidental death), and a **guaranteed purchase option** (buy more insurance later regardless of health/occupation).

### Insurance distribution (pp. 151-152) [LO 4-3]
- **Two major company types (p. 151):**
  - **Mutual company** - owned solely by its policyholders (no other stockholders). At a policy period's end, policyholders may receive a premium refund if expenses were lower than expected; this dividend isn't taxable as income unless it exceeds cumulative premiums paid. Owning via a policy lets you attend annual meetings and vote.
  - **Stock company** - a publicly traded company (like IBM, GM, or Intel) owned by stockholders who may or may not hold a policy from the company.
- **Types of life insurance policies - by how profits are shared (p. 151):**
  - **Participating policies** - policyholders receive dividends because they own the company. Mutual companies, by law, may NOT sell nonparticipating policies.
  - **Nonparticipating policies** - sold by STOCK companies only; dividends come from profits and typically go only to stockholders, not policyholders. Stock companies MAY also sell participating policies (dividends shared between stockholders and policy owners).
- **Agents and brokers (pp. 151-152):**
  - Policies of both company types are sold by agents, brokers, or both. Some agents have restrictive contracts barring them from selling other companies' policies; a person can be an agent for one company and a broker for others.
  - **Key difference = who the salesperson represents.** **Agents** are legal representatives of the INSURANCE COMPANY - loyalty is to the insurer (they should still sell suitable policies but must follow their employer). **Brokers** represent the BUYER - loyalty is to the client; they can shop policies from several insurers and are NOT employees of the insurer.
  - An **agent "is" the company** and can **bind coverage** (p. 152): when the agent says you're covered, the insurer is obligated even if you have an accident shortly after.
  - **Brokers CANNOT bind an insurer** (p. 152). If a broker binds coverage, it's actually provided by the broker until a broker affiliate accepts it - you're protected only up to the broker's assets and professional liability coverage, so paying a broker doesn't guarantee immediate coverage. However, once an insurer is selected, most states legally convert the broker's status to that of an AGENT of the insurer for issuing/placing coverage.
  - **Accountability (p. 152):** if you tell an AGENT you smoke infrequently and the agent fails to relay it, and the insurer issues a nonsmoker-rate policy, the insurer is still legally bound - even if it later learns you smoke. If a BROKER did the same, the insurer could cancel while the **incontestable period (generally two years)** hadn't expired; courts generally hold that after two years the insurer is bound, and only fraud (like faking a death) can void a policy after incontestability. Bottom line: advising an AGENT legally counts as advising the COMPANY.
  - **Insurance agent specialties:** two broad types - (1) **life and health**; and (2) **property and liability (P&L)**, aka **property and casualty (P&C)**. P&L is sometimes split into **personal lines** (home & auto) and **commercial lines** (business). Most agents/brokers focus on ONE of the three areas (life & health, personal lines P&L, or commercial P&L). The best planners surround themselves with specialists for trusted, current information.

## Chapter 4.2 - Homeowners Insurance (pp. 155-160) [LO 4-4]

### Policy basics (p. 155)
- Every homeowners policy has two sections: **Section I** (property losses) and **Section II** (liability losses).
- **Three types of homeowners policies:** (1) single-family homes (includes townhomes); (2) condominiums; (3) renters (tenant).
- All three provide **liability coverage**. Single-family homes also need coverage for the **dwelling**, possibly **other structures** (e.g., detached garage), **personal property**, and **loss of use**. Policies more often than not include **replacement cost** coverage (enough to replace the home if it's a total loss).
- **Condominium** coverage has minimal dwelling coverage because the condo association typically covers the exterior structure; the policy covers the interior space, personal property, and loss of use. **Renters** insurance covers only personal property and loss of use, plus liability.
- **Two major exclusions:** (1) damage from **earth movement (earthquakes)** and (2) **flooding**. Earth-movement insurance is available in earthquake-prone areas. Flood-zone/low-lying homes can be insured through the **National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP)**: coverage limits are **$250,000 on buildings and $100,000 on personal property**; more can be added via a specialty insurer. Read the policy for OTHER exclusions when it's issued - not when a loss occurs.

### Section I coverages - overview (pp. 155-156)
Section I is divided into four areas, A through D:
1. **Coverage A** - insures the dwelling, including additions/structures attached to the building, plus limited coverage on landscaping (trees, grass, shrubs) around the dwelling.
2. **Coverage B** - insures other structures ("appurtenant structures") on the premises, such as unattached garages, fences, and sheds.
3. **Coverage C** - insures general personal property (property not specifically named in the contract).
4. **Coverage D** - loss-of-use coverage: expenses while the dwelling is uninhabitable due to a covered peril.

### Coverage A: Dwelling and Other Attached Structures (p. 156)
- Insures the dwelling, including additions and the materials/supplies on premises used to construct, alter, or repair it. Because it's "homeowners" coverage, the building must be where the insured intends to LIVE. Standard forms extend some coverage for occasional rental of the residence, rental of part to no more than **two boarders**, or rental of part as an office, studio, school, or private garage.

### Coverage B: Other Structures Unattached (p. 156)
- Insures unattached structures on the premises (unattached garages, sheds, fences, hedges, swimming pools). Coverage does NOT extend to buildings used for business or rental - though a garage may be rented and covered as long as it's used only as a garage.

### The 80% coinsurance rule (pp. 156-157)
- Coverage A and B provide adequate coverage as long as the insurance is **at least 80% of the home's replacement cost**; then a loss is paid LESS the deductible. If coverage is (or becomes) less than 80% of replacement cost, a **partial** loss is paid at the GREATER of (a) actual cash value (replacement cost minus depreciation) or (b) the **coinsurance penalty formula** (only relevant for a partial loss):
  - **(Amount of insurance carried / Amount of insurance required) x Loss - Deductible = Settlement**
- **Example - Ellie (pp. 156-157):** She bought a house for $100,000 and insured it for $100,000, with a $500 deductible. Today the market value is over $250,000, but she never raised her coverage. A hailstorm damages the roof. The insurance adjuster determines the home's replacement cost is now **$200,000** (land value is NOT included). Repairing the 10-year-old roof will cost **$20,000**.
  - Required coverage = 80% x $200,000 = **$160,000**; Ellie carries only **$100,000**.
  - Settlement = (100,000 / 160,000) x $20,000 - $500 = 0.625 x $20,000 - $500 = $12,500 - $500 = **$12,000**.
  - So the **less-than-full replacement cost** payment costs Ellie **$8,000 out of pocket** ($7,500 shortfall + the $500 deductible). Had her coverage been at least 80% ($160,000+), the insurer would have paid the entire $20,000 minus the $500 deductible = **$19,500**, and Ellie would have paid just $500 instead of $8,000. (p. 157)
  - **Even at 80%+ coverage, a homeowner may still be underinsured for a TOTAL loss.** If Ellie's home were destroyed by fire and the replacement cost to rebuild were $200,000, then even $160,000 of coverage would not be adequate - the insurer would pay only $160,000 and Ellie would owe the remaining **$40,000**. (p. 157)

### Coverage C: Personal Property (p. 157)
- Insures personal property as defined in the policy: furniture, appliances, clothing, computers, and other possessions not attached as permanent fixtures. Basic personal property coverage is a **percentage** of the Coverage A amount (e.g., 50%). If the dwelling coverage is $150,000, personal property coverage might be $75,000.
- Because there are **limits** on personal property coverage, it may be increased if needed. Jewelry, art, firearms, and other collectibles generally have LOW coverage amounts and would need additional coverage at extra cost. Example: jewelry may only be covered up to $1,000, so if $10,000 of jewelry were stolen, the max collectible without additional insurance would be **$1,000**.
- **Inland marine policies** (sometimes called **personal article floaters**): used to cover personal property that is frequently moved from place to place, or for which there is not enough coverage under the homeowners policy. Commonly covered: jewelry, furs, electronics, silverware, golf and ski equipment, musical instruments, fine art, and coin and stamp collections. The homeowners policy may provide limited coverage for these items; an inland marine policy provides greater limits and broader coverage.
- Inland marine policies can insure the most precious items for their fair market value, often **without a deductible**, and the property is insured **anywhere in the world** against any type of loss not specifically excluded. They allow customization to each individual's unique coverage needs and can be **stand-alone** or written as **personal articles floaters on a homeowners policy**.

### Coverage D: Loss of Use (pp. 157-158)
- Provides protection against the loss of use of your premises. When insured property is damaged, Coverage D may provide for **additional living expenses** incurred. Payments are made for the length of time needed to replace or repair the damage, or for resettlement if necessary. Coverage D pays for costs ABOVE the regular expenses one would normally pay - e.g., an insured would still owe the monthly mortgage payment, but could collect the cost of residing in a hotel while the home is rebuilt.

### Additional Section I coverages (p. 158)
Homeowners insurance provides additional coverages that may include:
- **debris removal** if the debris is caused by a covered peril
- **reasonable repairs** made to protect the property
- **fire department service charges**
- coverage for damage to **removed property** while trying to protect it from further damage from the building
- **credit card, fund transfer card, forgery, and counterfeit money** - e.g., credit card losses, unauthorized use of ATM cards, depositors' forgery, counterfeit money, and costs to defend the insured
- **loss assessment** from condominium or homeowners associations
- **collapse** from specific perils in Coverage C: hidden decay, hidden insect or vermin damage, weight of contents, weight of rain or snow on a roof, defects in materials during construction
- **glass or safety glazing material** for glass breakage, such as windows and storm doors
- Most Section I coverages are subject to a **deductible**. As with most insurance, **higher deductibles generally mean lower premiums**.

### Section II (p. 158)
Section II of the homeowners policy has two parts: **Coverage E** and **Coverage F**.

### Coverage E: Personal Liability (pp. 158-159)
- If you (the insured) incur a legal obligation in a liability settlement, your insurance makes any required payments **up to policy limits**, and the insurer also provides for your **defense** in the lawsuit within policy limits.
- Liability coverage under Part E extends to all **residents of the covered household**: you, your spouse living with you, your relatives, and any person under age 21 in the care of you or your relatives. It also covers any persons responsible for the care of your animals or watercraft, and any employee or other person using a covered vehicle (e.g., an unlicensed motorized lawn mower) on an insured location with your consent. In most cases children are covered while in college, though courts have occasionally issued adverse rulings - check with your agent about how a particular insurer defines a "resident."
- **Exclusions:** **intentional injury** to another is not covered (e.g., getting angry and hitting your neighbor with a two-by-four). **Self-inflicted injury** is not covered under a liability policy, because you can't incur a legal liability to yourself - if you accidentally injure yourself on your property (p. 159) you can't rely on homeowners liability; that's what your **health insurance** is for. **Liability from renting your property** to others is also excluded, EXCEPT for occasional or partial rental (e.g., a temporary boarder or renting a small office), which typically IS covered. Check with your agent for details.

### Coverage F: Medical Payments to Third Parties (p. 159)
- Covers medical payments to third parties. If any third party with permission (a guest or worker) is injured on the premises of your home, this coverage pays their medical bills **regardless of fault or negligence** (similar to medical payments under an auto policy).
- Injuries to a third party **NOT on the home premises** are paid only if the injury is due to the negligence of:
  - the homeowner(s)
  - a member of the family
  - an animal owned by or being cared for by a member of the family
  - a worker hired by the family
- **Business activities:** business and professional activities are NOT covered, because homeowners policies are designed for **personal** protection. You can buy an endorsement for some limited business liabilities, but most are not covered and should be insured with a separate business liability policy. Some part-time paid activities (like a job your child may have) may be covered; however, once **significant income** (as defined by the policy) is earned, the activity is no longer covered.
- **Professional liability:** liability arising from professional activities (e.g., accounting services) is not covered on a homeowner policy - other insurance is needed. **Errors and omissions (E&O)** insurance covers **financial harm** sustained by professionals. **Malpractice** insurance covers **physical harm** and is carried by professionals such as doctors and dentists.

### How much insurance is needed? (pp. 159-160)
- For most homeowners: full replacement cost, or at least coverage equal to a minimum of **80% of the building's replacement cost**. Also carry adequate personal property insurance - make a **written and photographic inventory** of personal property and determine its actual value. Some property (jewelry, art) may require special coverage endorsements. (p. 159)
- **Comparison shopping** matters - not all insurers are equal; premiums, coverages, and service can differ significantly. Check with several insurers until you find a total package that meets your needs, and develop a relationship with an experienced agent or broker. (pp. 159-160)
- **Package discounts (p. 160):** most insurers offer discounts for carrying BOTH homeowners and auto coverage with them. Note: most insurers require you to carry both the underlying auto AND homeowners insurance with them before they'll issue an **umbrella policy** (discussed later in this module).
- **Learning Activity (p. 160):** review your homeowners or renter's policy - premises and personal property coverage limits, any endorsements/special forms, how your policy differs from the standard forms, when you last reviewed it, whether changes are needed, and (for owners) whether the home is still insured for at least 80% of replacement cost (excluding land value) or, conversely, whether falling home values have left you with more than 100% coverage.

## Chapter 4.3 - Automobile and Umbrella Liability Insurance (pp. 161-168) [LO 4-5, 4-6]

### Automobile Insurance (p. 161) [LO 4-5]
- Few consumer products affect daily life in the U.S. as much as the automobile - and it's also a major source of expense. Because an auto consumes so much of your net income, it makes sense to protect that investment; adequate insurance is one significant way to limit auto-related financial exposure.
- Losses extend beyond repair or replacement after an accident. As an owner you also have a heightened risk of **legal liability** - you may damage another's property (including the other vehicle), and people are often injured or even killed in accidents, where potential liability can be huge. Auto insurance protects you by covering all or part of that liability exposure.

### Types of Auto Policies (p. 161)
- Auto insurance has **four areas of coverage:** liability, medical payments, physical damage, and uninsured motorists.
- Auto **liability** insurance protects you against legal liability when your auto damages another person or their property. Coverage may be written with a **single limit** (as with homeowners) or with **split limits** (e.g., 25/50/15). Under split limits: the **first** number = bodily injury liability per **one person** (in thousands - 25 = $25,000); the **second** = amount for **all people** injured in a common accident; the **third** = **property damage** per accident.
- Every state requires drivers to have some auto insurance. Some people (poor driving records, or higher-risk groups like young male drivers) cannot buy through regular channels; options may include a **reinsurance pool operated by the state** for high-risk drivers.
- **State-mandated minimums are very low**, often around **25/50/10** ($25,000 for one individual, $50,000 for all individuals in a common accident, $10,000 property damage). $10,000 of property damage falls woefully short if you demolish a $50,000 car; and since medical costs for one seriously injured person can easily reach $100,000, $25,000 of coverage is inadequate.

### Cost of Auto Insurance (p. 162)
Four primary factors determine the cost of auto insurance:
1. **age and gender of the driver**
2. **use of the vehicle**
3. **type of vehicle**
4. **the driver's record**
- **Age and gender:** single male drivers under age 25 are the highest-risk category, with the highest premiums. Savings may be available if a young driver has taken approved driver training, maintained a "B" / 3.0 average or better, is in the upper 20% of the class, or is on the dean's list/honor roll (or equivalent). If a young driver is away at school at a minimum distance (usually 100 miles) and the car stays at home, a discount is available. Most companies give a **maximum of two** discounts.
- **Vehicle use:** the **covered automobile** is primarily the vehicle shown in the policy declarations and can be any number of vehicles you own. Autos driven for **pleasure only** cost less to insure than those driven to work daily; **low annual mileage** allows lower premiums. Longer commutes (15 miles or more) and business use both **increase** the premium; **farm use** generally reduces it. The geographic location of the car/driver (urban vs. rural, large vs. small city) also has significant impact.
- **Type of vehicle:** high-performance cars and sports cars tend to have higher premiums. SUVs typically have higher-than-average premiums due to the increased amount of damage they can inflict.
- **Multiple vehicles:** insuring more than one vehicle generally makes the **premium per vehicle lower** than insuring them separately.
- **Driver's record:** directly affects the rate charged. Operating while intoxicated or too many moving violations can lead to denied coverage - and in severe cases, a revoked license. Before those extremes, a company will often offer coverage with steeper and steeper premiums.
- **Credit score:** another factor that may come into play. Studies show those with higher credit scores tend to have fewer accidents and cost insurers less than those with lower scores.

### General Policy Coverages (pp. 163-164)
- The **personal automobile policy (PAP)** is a package plan, much like the homeowners policy. A PAP covers three main categories of auto-related loss: **legal liability**; **injury to the insured or other family members**; and **damage to or loss of the automobile**. (p. 163)
- To be covered by a PAP, the vehicle must be owned by an individual or jointly by spouses living in the same household, and the covered vehicle must be a **private passenger automobile** (which includes pickup trucks and vans, and - through a special endorsement and additional premium - motorcycles, motor homes, golf carts, and snowmobiles). A PAP is written in simplified, easy-to-understand language and is divided into **six parts:**
  - **Part A:** liability coverage
  - **Part B:** medical payments
  - **Part C:** uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage
  - **Part D:** coverage for damage to your automobile
  - **Part E:** duties after an accident or loss
  - **Part F:** general provisions
- PAP **liability limits** are denoted as bodily injury per person / total bodily injury per accident / property damage per accident - e.g., **100/300/100** = $100,000 bodily injury per person, $300,000 total bodily injury per accident, $100,000 property damage per accident. (p. 163)
- **Part A: Liability (p. 163):** rather than applying separate limits to each person and each occurrence, the PAP uses a **single limit of liability** that applies to the total covered liability losses from an accident, regardless of the number of persons injured or the amount of property damage. The amount needed depends on the individual's circumstances; state-mandated minimums are very low and should not be considered recommended amounts. With rising costs of responsibility and litigation, individuals should carry at least **$300,000-$500,000** of liability coverage. In addition, umbrella policies provide additional insurance and usually require at least **$300,000** of underlying coverage before purchase.
  - The PAP covers you and any family member in your household while using your own auto (or trailer) or when borrowing or renting another car. Anyone using your covered auto **with your permission** is covered; anyone using it **without** your express or implied permission is **not**. Generally, autos used for **business purposes** are not covered by a PAP (e.g., a food delivery business using the car to deliver food typically requires other coverage - affordable coverage is available for business use). (p. 163)
  - An auto policy covers **automobiles** and does NOT extend to motorcycles, watercraft, or recreational vehicles. A homeowners policy may provide limited coverage for some watercraft/RVs while stored on the property, but liability from their operation is excluded. These vehicles need separate policies. (p. 164)
- **Part B: Medical payments (p. 164):** different from liability coverage in that it pays medical expenses **regardless of personal liability**. It pays reasonable medical and funeral expenses for bodily injury caused by an accident and sustained by an insured. "Insured" here refers to:
  - the named insured and any family member who suffers bodily injury while occupying a covered automobile
  - the named insured and family members as **pedestrians** if struck by a vehicle designed for use on the roads, or any trailer
  - Individuals other than the named insured and family are also insured for medical payments; to qualify, they must **occupy the insured's covered automobile**. Coverage while in a **nonowned** auto may be provided under that vehicle's coverage or by the individual's own policy.
- **Part C: Uninsured/underinsured motorists (p. 164):** Why need it if the law requires everyone to have adequate insurance? **Uninsured motorist** coverage protects against loss caused by those who break the law and don't maintain the required coverage - generally, after a loss you receive the amount you should have been able to collect from the uninsured motorist had he been insured. It also protects you in a **hit-and-run** situation; however, it would NOT protect you if the uninsured vehicle belonged to or was being used by you or a household member. States generally require standard minimum amounts, and coverage can be increased.
  - **Underinsured motorist** coverage protects similarly, and most insurers merge the two into one coverage. It protects you when the other driver has insurance but in an amount **inadequate** to cover your loss, and is usually written for the same amount as uninsured motorist coverage.
  - The two coverages don't overlap: **uninsured** = the offending driver has **no** insurance; **underinsured** = the other driver has **some** insurance (usually enough to meet the state requirement) but it's inadequate, and a serious accident could result in expenses far in excess of policy amounts. In both cases your insurer reimburses you, then may try to collect from the other driver.
- **Part D: Damage to your own automobile (pp. 164-165):** physical damage coverage for direct and accidental loss to your auto or any nonowned auto you use. In most cases a **deductible** applies before you receive payment. The PAP provides coverage for **loss by collision** and **loss other than by collision**.
  - **Collision** coverage: losses from a collision between your covered auto and another object (another car or an object such as a tree). You may collect from your collision coverage even if the accident is not "your fault"; if the at-fault individual has no insurance, the PAP pays your loss (after the deductible, up to policy limits), then attempts to collect from the other party. (p. 165)
  - **Other than collision** coverage (previously known as **comprehensive**): applies to all non-collision physical losses except those specifically excluded - e.g., glass breakage, falling objects, theft or larceny, explosion, hail, contact with a bird or animal, and the like. Other than collision may carry a LOWER deductible than collision, so the policy is likely to pay more of the loss. (p. 165)
  - The PAP provides physical damage coverage for **nonowned** autos. Rental-car situation: renting from a national rental company, you're frequently offered an optional **collision damage waiver (CDW)** on the rented car. CDW premiums can be $10+ per day but are often justified because you're liable for any damage to the vehicle and the rental company does not provide physical damage coverage (though liability, medical payments, uninsured motorist, and no-fault coverage may be provided). If you have physical damage coverage on your PAP (collision AND other than collision), using a rental car is likely covered as a nonowned auto and you shouldn't need the optional CDW. HOWEVER, this may not apply if you're renting for **business purposes** - then your PAP may NOT cover you. This varies by state and company, so check with your insurer before renting. (p. 165)
- **Part E: Duties after an accident (pp. 165-166):** similar to the homeowners forms, including:
  - providing prompt notice to the insurer of a claim (how, when, and where the loss occurred)
  - cooperating in any investigation, settlement, or defense
  - sending the insurer copies of any notices or legal papers related to the claim
  - submitting to a physical exam if claiming injury, and agreeing to a verbal exam under oath for any claim
  - providing authorization to examine medical records and other pertinent records
  - submitting any required proof of loss
  - taking reasonable steps to prevent further damage to the vehicle
  - notifying police of any hit-and-run accident or theft of a covered auto (p. 166)
  - permitting the insurer to inspect damage prior to repair or other disposition (p. 166)
- **Part F: General provisions (p. 166):** other provisions include coverage NOT being provided if there is **fraud** on the part of the insured. A **subrogation clause** gives the insurer the right to recover a payment made for any loss for which the insured was not ultimately liable - if a third party is found liable but the insured's company has already paid, the insurance company is reimbursed before the insured receives any additional compensation.
  - **Application of deductibles:** most PAP coverages are subject to a deductible, and the amount may vary by area of coverage. You may be able to raise or lower deductibles (premiums fall/rise accordingly); using **higher deductibles** is a common way to save on premiums.

### How much / what type of insurance is needed? (p. 166)
- The **minimum** level of auto insurance is the amount required to satisfy your state's **financial responsibility requirements**. Most states have minimums, and all drivers must comply (though not all do - which is why uninsured/underinsured motorist coverages exist). State minimums (just $25,000 or $50,000 liability) will probably fall short if there are injuries, and should NOT be thought of as acceptable coverage guidelines. Auto insurance can be expensive, especially for young drivers with poor records; for some (high-risk) drivers, simply meeting the minimums uses up all the funds available for insurance.
- Other factors to consider:
  - What **deductible** is most appropriate? Lower premium + higher deductible, or a lower deductible?
  - Are you concerned about a large liability lawsuit? Does your **umbrella** carrier require certain limits of coverage?
  - If your car is older, are potential damage payments likely to be high enough to justify carrying **collision** coverage?
  - Is there a **lien** (outstanding loan) on the vehicle that demands a certain level of coverage?
- Bottom-line question: **"What is the best use of premium dollars relative to the amount of risk you want to transfer or are willing to keep?"** In general, premium dollars are best used to cover potentially large losses.
- **Learning Activity (p. 167):** review your auto insurance the same way you reviewed your homeowners/renters coverage - questions, ease of understanding, liability limits, deductibles, responsibilities after an accident - and review with your agent.

### Umbrella Liability Coverage (pp. 167-168) [LO 4-6]
- A liability insurance policy pays expenses that result when an insured incurs a **legal liability**; the insurer agrees to pay whatever the insured is legally obligated to pay, up to policy limits. The situation causing the liability must be covered by the policy (an auto liability policy would not cover a liability specifically related to your home). You and the insurer are the two main parties, and the person to whom payments are made is the **third party** (hence liability insurance is called **third-party coverage**). The third party is not an insured but can receive payments once the insured's liability is established. This module limits itself to liability claims against an **individual**. (p. 167)
- Liability insurance protects against two main categories of personal liability: **automobile** and **general**. General liability insurance for individuals is known as **comprehensive personal liability (CPL) insurance** - almost every nonbusiness type of liability is covered EXCEPT automobile liability. CPL coverage is included in **Section II of the homeowners policy**. (p. 167)
- The main problem with both homeowners and auto liability insurance is the relatively **low upper limit** of coverage. Today, individuals commonly face liability lawsuits with financial exposure of **$1 million or more**. Because most basic homeowners or auto insurance does not provide coverage in such high amounts, you could be responsible for settling a large judgment on your own. (p. 167)
- Fortunately, insurers provide liability coverage with high upper limits. The **umbrella liability policy** is designed to provide personal catastrophe liability protection, covering both auto and general liability up to policy limits (e.g., $2 million). It rarely stands alone - it is coupled with a minimum amount of underlying coverage in BOTH the homeowners and auto policies. The insurer determines the required underlying coverage; for example, it might require a minimum of **$300,000** of liability insurance on both the auto and homeowners policies before issuing an umbrella policy. Umbrella policies are usually written in **increments of $1 million**, commonly offering **$1 million to $10 million** of coverage, and will cover whatever the underlying auto and homeowners policies cover. (pp. 167-168)
- If the insured is sued and the claim is covered under the homeowners or auto policy, the **appropriate base policy pays first** - think of that payment as a **deductible** paid before the umbrella policy is called upon. If the base policy is not adequate to pay the claim, the umbrella covers the difference up to its limit. **Example:** a $1 million judgment against an insured who has a $1 million umbrella policy and a base policy with $300,000 of coverage - the base policy pays $300,000 and the umbrella pays the remaining **$700,000**. However, if the base homeowners or auto policy will NOT cover a particular claim but the umbrella policy does, the umbrella policy alone pays the $1 million judgment (with or without a deductible, depending on the policy). Umbrella liability insurance covers a broad range of liability exposures with **few exclusions**, so it may cover liability costs not covered in either the auto or homeowners policy. There is **no standard policy form** for umbrella policies, so coverage varies from company to company. (p. 168)
