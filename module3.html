Two rules, both common error sources: N is total periods, not years (monthly payments over 30 years → N = 360). And I/YR is always the annual rate — both calculators divide it by the payments-per-year setting internally.

🔁 The cash flow sign convention

Money flowing AWAY from you is negative. Money flowing TOWARD you is positive. Deposits, investments, and loan payments you make are negative. Withdrawals, loan proceeds you receive, and maturity values you collect are positive.

⚠️
Exam trap: if PV and FV (or PV and PMT) carry the same sign when they represent opposite directions of flow, the calculator errors out or returns nonsense. When an answer looks absurd, check signs first.

🎭 Annuity flavors: ordinary vs. due

📦 Ordinary annuity — pay at END

Payments land at the end of each period. Most loans, mortgages, and retirement contributions work this way. This is the calculator's default (END mode).

🏠 Annuity due — pay at START

Payments land at the beginning of each period. Think RENT — due on the 1st! Worth slightly more, because every payment compounds for one extra period. On the calculator, that's BEGIN mode.

🧠
Memory hook: “Rent is DUE on the 1st” → annuity due = beginning of period = BEGIN mode. Both calculators show a BEGIN indicator — make checking it a reflex, and switch back to END when you're done, or it silently corrupts your next problem.

Check yourself — 3.1

Why is the sign convention necessary at all?
TVM math requires inflows and outflows to be distinguishable. The calculator uses sign to encode direction — negative for money leaving you, positive for money coming to you. Without opposing signs on opposing flows, the equation has no valid solution.
A 5% annual rate compounded quarterly over 8 years, with P/YR = 1. What are N and I/YR?
N = 8 × 4 = 32, and I/YR = 5 ÷ 4 = 1.25.
Which mode for a lease with payments due on the first of each month, and why?
BEGIN mode. Payments occur at the start of each period, making it an annuity due. Each payment has an extra period to compound, so the value differs from the END-mode result.
A problem returns a wildly large FV. What do you check first?
Whether the registers were cleared — a leftover PMT from the prior problem is the most common cause. Then verify N and I/YR share a period basis, and that the rate was entered as a percent rather than a decimal.

Chapter 3.2 — Time Value of Money Calculations

There are only five variables — N, I/YR, PV, PMT, FV. Enter four, solve for the fifth. The real skill is reading a word problem and correctly identifying which variable is being asked for.

🧮 The core formulas

QuantityFormula
Future value of a lump sumFV = PV(1 + r)ⁿ
Present value of a lump sumPV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
Future value of an ordinary annuityFV = PMT × [((1 + r)ⁿ − 1) ÷ r]
Present value of an ordinary annuityPV = PMT × [(1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ) ÷ r]
Annuity due (either direction)Ordinary annuity result × (1 + r)

Here r is the rate per period and n the number of periods — the calculator does the arithmetic, but knowing these makes the period conversions obvious.

Check yourself — 3.2

Why does monthly compounding beat annual at the same stated rate?
Interest is credited more often, so it begins earning interest sooner. In Problem ② the same 7% over 12 years produced $57,768.02 monthly versus $56,304.79 annually — about $1,463 from frequency alone.
In a loan problem, why is PV positive while PMT is negative?
The client receives the loan proceeds (money flowing toward them, positive) and makes payments (money flowing away, negative). The sign convention encodes direction of flow, and opposite directions require opposite signs.
A client saved $180,000 over 30 years and ended with $502,257. What does this show?
Roughly $322,000 — about 64% of the final balance — came from compound growth rather than contributions. It demonstrates why time in the market dominates contribution size over long horizons, and why starting early matters more than starting large.
A client wants $40,000/year for 25 years and also to leave $100,000 to heirs. What changes in the setup?
FV changes from 0 to 100,000. Setting FV = 0 exhausts the portfolio exactly; a residual bequest requires a larger PV at the start of retirement.
Estimate the doubling time at 9% using the Rule of 72, and state its limits.
72 ÷ 9 = 8 years. It's a mental approximation, reasonably accurate around 5–12% and less so at the extremes — fine for illustrating a concept, not for computing a recommendation.

Chapter 3.3 — The Importance of Interest Rate Assumptions

Every projection in 3.2 rested on an assumed rate — and that assumption does more to determine the answer than any other input. Disclose it, keep it defensible, and show how sensitive the result is to being wrong.

🎯 Choosing the right rate for the job

There's no single correct rate across a plan — the rate should match the money it's funding:

ApplicationRate basis
Long-horizon retirement accumulationEquity-weighted portfolio return, net of costs
Short-horizon goal in cash equivalentsCurrent money market or CD yields
Retirement distribution phaseMore conservative — lower-volatility allocation
Comparing debt paydown against investingThe debt's interest rate (a guaranteed return)
Discounting an insurance needConservative, since the need is not optional

📉 The danger of overstating returns

8% produces more than twice the 4% result, from identical contributions. The errors aren't symmetric: a too-conservative assumption leaves a surplus (a good problem); a too-aggressive one leaves a shortfall discovered at retirement, with no time left to fix it.

🌡️ Show the range: sensitivity analysis

The professional response to assumption uncertainty isn't guessing better — it's running the projection at several rates and presenting all of them. It's honest about uncertainty, reveals which assumptions drive the outcome, and reframes the conversation from “will I have enough?” to “under what conditions do I fall short, and what would I do about it?”

🧮 Nominal vs. real returns

Nominal return is the stated return before adjusting for inflation. Real return is what actually buys more goods. The quick approximation everyone reaches for first — Real ≈ Nominal − Inflation — is good enough for conversation but not exact.

Exact formula: Real rate = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] − 1

Example: 7% nominal, 3% inflation → approximation gives 4.00%; the exact calculation gives 3.8835%. Small in one year, meaningful over 30 — the program expects the exact method.

🧩 Don't mix your frameworks

Two internally-consistent ways to run an inflation-adjusted projection: keep the goal in today's dollars and discount at the real rate, or inflate the goal to future dollars and discount at the nominal rate. Both give the same answer — the error is mixing them.

Worked example. 30 years from retirement, wants $60,000/year in today's dollars for a 25-year retirement, 3% inflation, 7% nominal return.

Real rate = (1.07 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 3.8835%. PV of a 25-year, $60,000 annuity at that rate = $948,974.75 in today's dollars — the capital needed, in purchasing power a client understands. Inflated to the nominal dollars that will actually appear on the statement: $948,974.75 × 1.03³⁰ = $2,303,410.79. Both are correct; they answer different questions.

Check yourself — 3.3

Why is overstating the return worse than understating it?
The errors aren't symmetric in consequence. Too conservative an assumption produces a surplus. Too aggressive an assumption produces a shortfall that surfaces at retirement, when there's no time left to correct it by saving more or working longer.
Compute the exact real return for a 6% nominal return with 2.5% inflation, and compare to the approximation.
Exact: (1.06 ÷ 1.025) − 1 = 3.4146%. Approximation: 6% − 2.5% = 3.5%. The shortcut overstates by about 0.085 percentage points.
What's wrong with stating a goal in today's dollars and discounting at the nominal rate?
It mixes two frameworks. Today's dollars must be paired with a real rate; nominal rates must be paired with inflated future dollars. Mixing them understates the required savings, because the goal isn't inflated but the discount rate assumes it was.
Why present a range of rates rather than one?
Because no one can forecast returns reliably, and a single figure implies a precision that doesn't exist. A range is honest about uncertainty, reveals which assumptions drive the outcome, and shifts the conversation toward what the client would do if returns disappoint.
A client says they'll need “$80,000 a year” in a retirement 25 years out. At 3% inflation, what will that actually cost in year-one nominal dollars?
$80,000 × 1.03²⁵ = $167,502. And it keeps rising through retirement — inflation doesn't stop at the retirement date, so later years cost more still.
